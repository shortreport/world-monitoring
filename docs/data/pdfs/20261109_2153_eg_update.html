dear hondo-san,

us midterms. how us policy will change...and how it won't. (maybe) the end of netanyahu. and a sudden financial crisis in paraguay.

après le déluge

politics in the united states does not want for excitement. two years after trump engineered the biggest comeback in modern us political history, he presided over the biggest loss in a midterm election since 1974.

as i said last week, if i was wrong in a view that the democrats were going to win easily, it was because i was still being too cautious. the results brought an extraordinary swing for the house (42 seats and counting), senate (7, on balance likely to become 9 once counting is complete), and governorships (5) that exceeded what even the staunchest democrats in office had thought possible. pretty much everything that was up for a potential flip—in an extremely divided united states—ended up turning blue. even several gerrymandered districts specifically redistricted to help republicans ended up going for democrats; mississippi ended up in play and the new york times hadn't even sent a journalist to the state to cover the race (they have now, as the vote counting continues).

what happened? trump asked voters to act as if he was at the polls...and they did. it's inflation, stupid. affordability has led to high turnout and big anti-incumbency moves in elections around the world, and that proved particularly true in the united states. some 94% of voters registered their concerns about gas prices, the most coordinated opposition on an issue i've seen in recent memory. trump was viewed as directly responsible for much of that (his decision to launch the iran war, inability to resolve russia-ukraine despite it being a top campaign promise, and the impact of historically high tariffs). and while there were other causes too (the ai boom), trump didn't help himself by being perceived as indifferent to the overall impact; with a heavier policy focus on helping the wealthy, efforts to enrich himself and his family/inner circle, and the distraction of vanity projects that occupied much of the president's attention but didn't resonate with voters.

for their part, democrats ran mostly competent and moderate candidates; stayed out of trump's way as he was making mistakes; and kept their messaging tied to trump's biggest vulnerabilities. unlike in 2024, they showed no signs of snatching defeat from the jaws of victory.

some implications:

1 - for trump himself. i've heard plenty of commentary that a "lame duck" trump becomes an "angry bird" on matters of policy. i don't buy the distinction. in part because trump is significantly more constrained by foreign leaders, both allies and adversaries, willing to hit back. he's more constrained domestically, by democrats and increasingly some republicans who don't want massive trump-driven disruption. he has also lost a lot of zip on his fastball; trump's energy level and mental focus are significantly less than what it was, and he's not driving the same level of policy (plus he's likely to lose many of the higher quality advisors that have been able to execute policy for him). and because much of his legacy focus is on monetizing the office for himself and inner circle, and physical manifestations of legacy (renaming things, building monuments, and the like); not broader policy, which has only occasionally been a core trump interest and is now less so. it's also worth remembering, trump's own inclinations don't skew toward warmaking, he continues to consider himself the president of peace. so if there's a sudden legacy policy push here, it's more likely to be an effort to announce big new deals that only trump could have negotiated (more on this below).

2 - for the administration. on the evening of trump's loss, incensed with the outcome, the president toyed with firing senior advisors including his chief of staff susie wiles and secretary of state marco rubio but was talked down. the former didn't last long, with wiles resigning under pressure late thursday night and being replaced with deputy chief of staff stephen miller. miller is completely loyal and trump is personally fond of him, but he's also not built close relations with many of trump's key advisors, is incendiary when it comes to organizational politics, and, to put it mildly, lacks the cordial relationships with members of congress that wiles had. couple that with the rise of natalie harp as the president's personal gatekeeper and trump is set to become more isolated in an echo chamber of supplicants, whose priority is willingness to respond to whatever the president wishes at the moment. that makes it much more likely that trump's most capable officials will leave quickly—rubio, treasury secretary scott bessent, energy secretary chris wright, and the like. while those most personally loyal and/or focused on monetization (jared kushner, secretary of commerce howard lutnick, steve witkoff) occupy more of the oxygen. which means less engagement/more confrontation with congress (rather than the dealmaking trump floated in the hours after the midterms), narrower and less effective (though more radical) executive orders, and a faster deterioration from peak trump more generally. which leads us to…

2a - jd vance. in many ways he has the biggest challenge. he wants to be the next president but knows the idea of being kamala harris for trump is a non-starter to win, even more so after last week. hence the decision to come out immediately and publicly with a list of why trump lost—all policies that he feels burnish his own record (since he wasn't in charge of the policies or could more credibly say he would have recommended a different direction)—on iran, cost of living/inflation, not putting "america first," and israel. three of which are about foreign policy (and so implicitly a direct knock on marco rubio, the most credible trump administration candidate along with vance), and one (inflation) being directly tied to it (iran, russia/ukraine, and tariffs...alongside the ai boom). the list also avoids impugning trump—no mention of corruption, epstein, vanity projects, and the like (all of which would be tantamount to resignation)—but still, it's remarkable given how much attachment trump has had to winning the war, and making the decisions himself (as opposed to because of israel). the intention here is to better position for the republican primary—and you would be hard pressed to see a clear path for vance to the nomination at this point—but whether that's as trump's candidate or, should trump further and quickly implode, after a public break with him remains an open question.

3 - on the left. the results are favorable for everyone on the left, but more impactful for democratic moderates. progressives and centrists in the democratic party picked up important electoral victories, but the size of the overall victory and the limited standing of democratic socialists mean the moderates are in charge. in the house, democratic socialists have all of 8 seats (i'm unreasonably hoping they start calling themselves "the ocho"), and are marginalized accordingly. and seniority determines committee leadership across house and senate, which means an absence of dsa in key positions and an overall direction that skews moderate. still none of that equates to coordination on policy; the democrats' big win reflects dissatisfaction with trump's agenda, not support for an alternative...and party leaders and aspirants have a lot of work to do before they agree on what comes next.

4 - state politics. democrats flipped key governorships, now holding a majority. party organization in those states is important, so too is control of 2028 state election procedures, where the idea of interference from an outgoing president trump in the next round has gone from implausible to impossible. also, three of the new democratic governorships are critical on the ai front—ohio, texas, and georgia are collectively responsible for nearly 50% of all the new data center buildout, which is now elevated to a top political issue...and a big reason for the sudden market drop for ai stocks in the election aftermath. (i'll note, relatedly, that recent texas transplant elon musk wisely deleted all of his anti-talarico/"talafreako" posts over the last few weeks.)

5 - on the right. the republican presidential primary will now be significantly more open than had previously been expected. those attached to the trump administration have a tougher road ahead—hence the vance list of why the republicans lost; the rubio rumors of an early post-election exit from the administration. florida governor ron desantis came out swinging against president trump over his responsibility for the sweeping losses (and is comparatively well positioned, governing the state that was the biggest "hold" for republicans against last week's blue wave). it's hard to imagine trump making an early move to endorse any contender, but absent that, the number of republicans that want to run away from trump on a '28 campaign is likely to be significant (think ted cruz, nikki haley, mike pompeo); i'd now expect at least 10-15 meaningful candidates rather than one or two trump administration contenders looking for the president's endorsement and a handful of less plausible outsiders.

implications for policy

i wrote at the beginning of the year that trump's effort at a political revolution—our top risk for 2026—would fail. and then in early summer, that we had passed the period of peak trump on the global stage. following trump's historic midterm loss, both of these realities will now prove more readily apparent to domestic and foreign leaders, leading to a dispersion and acceleration of policy processes anticipating a post-trump environment, with different implications in different parts of the world.

first, the pieces that won't change. us structural policy has shifted dramatically over the past nearly two decades in three major areas: globalization, where the united states has tilted from promoting free trade toward industrial policy, subsidies, and tariffs. security, with a structural shift toward weaker us guarantees of collective security, reduced international presence, more burden sharing, and more unwillingness to mount military interventions. and multilateral architecture, with restraint on international promotion of (and funding for) rule of law and democracy. those trends have been growing across the us electorate, are deeply present on the left and the right, and will continue post-midterms and irrespective of whether there is a republican or democrat that succeeds trump in 2029.

having said that, a look around the world at where we are likely to see significant impact.

iran is the most urgent. president trump now finds himself in a more difficult spot. having lost the election and admitting that the war in iran was a big (if not the most important) piece of it, the united states has handed the iranian government leverage, effectively taking dramatic escalation off the table.

trump's remaining four options are all problematic. 1) take a deal. but iran is now even less likely to offer the americans more favorable negotiating terms given the political events of the past week. and so, walking away with a negotiated settlement means president trump would need to accept an agreement that's considerably worse than anything he has been prepared to consider over the past months. 2) trump can instead just decide to maintain the blockade, but as long as that remains the case (and iran can't get their own oil out), they'll continue to engage in strikes on shipping in the region, spiking costs and significantly curtailing the transit of goods. 3) trump can order another round of limited strikes (against military and nuclear targets, and not much else), essentially a reprise of june 2025's operation midnight hammer—with little operational impact (iran's nuclear capabilities are now more dispersed and harder to assess) and also risking greater iranian retaliation than before, again in the context of their recently increased leverage. 4) finally, trump can unilaterally end the blockade and say the gulf states have to figure out the ongoing diplomacy. that's a decision he can also take after limited strikes, but withdrawing ships/troops from the region, and leaving us bases and embassies exposed, would be very difficult and slow. either way, it would reflect a significant loss for the americans and leave the gulf allies with a significantly less stable security environment than they had before the war. the reality that some variant of "the war," with ongoing disruptions and no settlement, remains in place through the end of trump's term looks increasingly likely at this point.

then there's israel. it's been an extraordinary week for them as well, with president trump announcing on the morning of the elections that he would no longer provide military aid to the country (which, to have had an electoral impact, he would've had to work into a strategy weeks before). and then vice president vance announcing that us israel policy was one of the top reasons for the republican midterm loss. never mind the fact that it's not true (israel is far from a top issue for most republican voters), but if the intention now is to bring the iran war to a close—either now or directly after one more round of strikes—then taking israeli interests in regime change out of the equation is connected.

on russia, trump is prioritizing efforts to reengage with the kremlin. last week, trump met with putin's special envoy kirill dmitriev and announced that there was going to be a "big agreement, involving more than ukraine." special envoy (and trump son-in-law) jared kushner added that the intention is a broader framework for long-term european security and stability...amounting to the americans and russians announcing some common overarching security principles separate from the ongoing war in ukraine.

the white house recognizes that there's not much near-term opportunity for a breakthrough on the ukraine war. both sides are escalating militarily to the best of their ability, and that's extremely unlikely to change at least until the end of winter. and any effort by the americans to cut ukraine off would elicit immediate hostility from across congress; an area where there's a serious brake on trump's interest in russian normalization. but trump remains interested in announcing a peace deal with moscow, normalizing us relations with putin, undermining a unified european position that opposes trump's interest, and further monetizing his remaining tenure in office. on all of those fronts, finding a way to cut a putin deal—potentially facilitated by beijing, and over the heads of the europeans—is of strong interest in the coming months.

i don't see significant change in policy on china. us-china relations continue to be strong and stable, and trump and xi both are comfortable with that. trump will remain steady on taiwan and continue to hold back on a major arms sales agreement (though a smaller sale remains likely to go through in 2027). china is prepared to increase demands for extending the trade truce in return for increasing purchases of us agricultural goods, aircraft, and the like—and i suspect that all works out reasonably well through the end of the year.

beyond that, on trade, a lame duck trump has fewer cards and accordingly will be more willing to act flexibly with partners. we're already seeing that with early outreach with canada and india; and i suspect that will be the case with a host of global south countries as well, especially those with critical minerals and other resources the united states is interested in.

in latin america, where trump has had the greatest success of his second term foreign policies to date, there's an open question based on whether marco rubio (who has run point on all the key policies, especially venezuela and cuba) remains in place. absent that, trump's ability to execute on political transition in venezuela, and force the cubans to accept a breakthrough economic deal (both of which, with rubio, are baseline expectations) becomes more limited. i don't see trump suddenly ordering major military intervention in cuba; he doesn't have any advisors pushing such a policy (not even secretary of defense pete hegseth).

a wild card: north korea, where i expect a summit meeting between the two leaders in the coming year (which xi jinping has been at least moderately inclined to facilitate), and trump will try to announce a breakthrough deal. he has cards to offer: recognition of north korea as a nuclear state and accordingly ending the long-term us calls for denuclearization; an end to the state of war that exists between north and south (and becoming another area where trump thinks he should win a nobel). will kim jong un play ball? at least nominally, offering a suspension of nuclear and icbm tests, that's the sort of thing i can see (though he'll negotiate from a stronger position, due to his recently upgraded relations with both russia and china). it doesn't have the durable change in policy of the abraham accords, a true change in geopolitics that trump gets credit for from his first term; but the symbolism of trump as the "president of peace" will very much appeal to him.

and then there's the issue of particular concern: artificial intelligence. president trump has neither the in-house talent nor the interest in creating tools for ai governance, which he sees as tantamount to risking the principal growth engine in the us economy (as well as the principal tool to ensure us supremacy over the chinese). but the next two years are critical given the state of ai development—the risks of ai tools being used to damage critical infrastructure; not to mention what happens when there's recursive self-improvement (rsi). the laissez-faire approach isn't sustainable. gravity will shift to congress (where moderate democrats will do most of the drafting, and trump will veto) and individual states, and there's plenty of political will there, but the areas of policy focus are all over the map; much of what we end up seeing depends on what near-term urgency/crisis emerges in the coming months. cyber crises are most likely; i'd also note a major investigation into the spygate around nvidia ceo jensen huang and national security concerns with china is set to be the first of the new senate...

*

in israel, it's now more likely than not that the gadi eisenkot-led opposition will form a minority government (with aligned support from the arab parties), removing benjamin netanyahu from power (and passing new legislation barring him from holding office in the future). us defense aid would wind down in any case, but the change in government would bring more stable relations with the us congress in managing that transition, as well as with a number of european governments that consider netanyahu a pariah. we'd expect no significant change on key foreign policies (specifically iran and lebanon), but a willingness to end the expansion of illegal settlements in the west bank and take on israeli terrorism there would also help calm the waters.

and a stunning turn of events in paraguay, where president santiago peña offered his resignation on the back of a massive private banking scandal—which wasn't accepted, instead leading to his impeachment, stripping him of immunity, following a unanimous vote from all legislators in the country. the president was personally involved in the affairs of ueno bank, with much of the government having turned to conduct their banking there...and as it became clear the books were problematic (and covering up irregularities from an extraordinary and unsustainable spike in returns), he essentially kept the issue under wraps until it exploded. which now means a majority of the country's entire population no longer has access to their money—they'll have a full-blown financial crisis and massive social unrest if there isn't a bailout of several billion dollars in short order.

*

i'm heading to abu dhabi for the sir bani yas policy conversations (have asked our folks how quickly we can get eurasia group branded flak jackets). a fascinating and critical time to be in the middle east, to be sure.

yours truly,
