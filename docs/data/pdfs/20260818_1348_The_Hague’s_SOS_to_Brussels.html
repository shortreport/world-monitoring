DRIVING THE DAY

BREATHING ROOM, PLEASE: The Netherlands’ centrist government is asking the European Commission for more flexibility in applying EU nature rules, in a bid to break a seven-year crisis that has stalled housing, farms and industrial projects and become a flashpoint for the far right.

“I really need [the Commission’s] support now,” Agriculture Minister Jaimi van Essen told Playbook from his office in The Hague, overlooking a patio shared with the economy ministry.

How did we get here? The Netherlands has one of Europe’s most intensive livestock sectors. Years of nitrogen pollution — much of it from farming — have left protected nature reserves in poor condition. EU law requires governments to prevent those habitats deteriorating further, making it difficult for Dutch authorities to approve building projects that would add more nitrogen near damaged nature.

It got even tougher after a Dutch court ruled in 2019 that promises to offset pollution weren’t enough to justify permits.

The building paralysis costs the Dutch economy around €15 billion a year, van Essen said, adding that his priority is to unlock housing and projects around Eindhoven — the high-tech hub that’s home to chipmaking giant ASML — and the Port of Rotterdam, one of Europe’s biggest trade hubs.

State of play: Van Essen belongs to Prime Minister Rob Jetten’s centrist Democrats 66, which formed a minority government with the center-right VVD and the Christian Democrats after winning the election in October. Jetten is facing the tough task of striking a budget agreement by the end of August to avoid another political crisis potentially toppling the coalition.

The centrist government needs to show it can solve the crisis “in order to stop the growth of the far right,” said van Essen, who has made €20 billion available to help farmers reduce nitrogen. The government has secured the support of the center-left PRO party for a nitrogen-reduction package — to be submitted to the parliament in October.

Rallying point: Nitrogen has become a defining political issue in the Netherlands, dominating successive elections, driving support for farmer protest parties and feeding broader anti-EU sentiment.

The Netherlands wants the Commission to say that authorities can approve projects that pollute now but deliver environmental gains over time. Van Essen’s hope is that official Commission guidance will give courts grounds to interpret the EU’s nature laws less restrictively, with permits flowing again and farmers’ anger waning. The minister says he isn’t asking Brussels to weaken environmental protection rules, just to apply them more flexibly.

On the chopping block: The laws are already in the crosshairs of a broader deregulation push led by the center-right European People’s Party, the dominant EU political force. “We need a better balance between environmental protection and the investments needed for the transition,” Belgian EPP head of delegation Wouter Beke told Playbook.

Red tape slashing: Meanwhile, the Commission is asking capitals and NGOs how to make the rules easier to apply and strip out bureaucracy. It’ll decide by the end of year whether to reopen the directives, as part of the ongoing deregulation drive in Brussels.

THE HAGUE CORNER

CONTINUING PLAYBOOK’S CULTURAL TOUR DES CAPITALES … Housed in a luminous modernist building, The Hague’s Kunstmuseum boasts the world’s largest collection of Piet Mondrian — famed for his grids of primary-colored squares. There’s also an entire section devoted to De Stijl, the Dutch movement Mondrian helped create, defined by simplicity, abstraction and function. Check it out, along with the museum’s other temporary exhibitions.

And for a koffiepauze: Head over to Bookstor, a 102-year-old cozy café-cum-bookshop in the city center with a large selection of poetry, essays … and chess boards. Get lost in a corner, sunbathe on the back terrace or be mesmerized by the drawings covering the wooden walls.

If you’re a political wonk: Playbook recommends skipping the Parliament tour for now, as the beautiful old Binnenhof complex has been a construction site since 2021 and isn’t due to reopen until 2031. The assembly currently sits in a hulking brutalist block more reminiscent of a prison more than a European house of democracy.

Instead, check out the Peace Palace, home to the U.N. International Court of Justice, which has a free exhibition on its history and mission.

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Cloudy with a chance of rain, high of 23C.

AUGUST FESTIVITIES CONTINUE: Brussels City Hall opens its doors from Wednesday through Sunday for an “immersive” dance performance in the historic corridors of the UNESCO-listed building to celebrate Brussels’ heritage. Tickets and schedules here.

PROCEEDS OF CRIME: Brussels City-Ixelles Police yesterday published its annual enforcement tally for the city center: 549 reports for offenses ranging from illegal employment and illicit trade to counterfeiting and public nuisance, with crimes targeting businesses including restaurants, cafés, vape shops, nail salons, souvenir stores and sex shops.

The loot: The unit seized more than €420,000 in cash and goods, including 8,387 units of poppers, 6,000 e-cigarettes, 366 packs of cigarettes, 67 kilograms of shisha tobacco, 353 counterfeit items, 47 prohibited weapons and 1,500 fireworks. BX1 has the report.
