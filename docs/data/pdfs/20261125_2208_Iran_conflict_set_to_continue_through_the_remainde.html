Iran conflict set to continue through the remainder of Trump’s term
25 Nov 2026 08:01 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 

summary
  • The US-Iran conflict will likely continue at varying levels of intensity through the end of President Donald Trump’s term (75%), as neither side is prepared to make major concessions or deploy the force necessary to secure a favorable resolution.
  • Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s near-term fiscal course correction will likely land on the constructive end of expectations, but the more significant policy risk rests in 2027.
  • Forthcoming US excess capacity tariffs will not increase headline country rates beyond trade deal levels, while the overall effective rate will remain below its level prior to the Supreme Court overturning International Economic Power Act (IEEPA) tariffs given broader exemptions.
  • Argentina’s congress will likely suspend the 2027 primaries, improving the electoral outlook for President Javier Milei in the October 2027 presidential elections.
  • Countries and sectors in this edition of the Daily Brief include the US, Argentina, Brazil, Canada, China, Colombia, Estonia, the EU, India, Iran, Iraq, Mexico, Russia, Singapore, Taiwan, geoeconomics, energy, sustainability, and trade.

the top line
US, Iran, Energy—Unresolved Iran conflict will define the rest of Trump’s term
  • The US-Iran conflict will likely continue at varying levels of intensity through the end of Trump’s term (75%), as neither side is prepared to make major concessions or deploy the force necessary to secure a favorable resolution. A more durable diplomatic deal (15%) and a unilateral US withdrawal (10%) are unlikely.
  • Iran’s push to control the Strait of Hormuz and its nuclear program will drive the conflict. Major unrest inside Iran will not compel capitulation. For Trump, a quick exit would be more costly than continuing the conflict.
  • The standoff will likely stay intense without major escalation through the first quarter of 2027, with Hormuz constantly contested (45% cumulative odds) and Brent oil at $80-$100 per barrel. The risk of an expanded conflict that causes a larger disruption to energy and significant damage to regional infrastructure remains considerable (25%).

Geoeconomics, US, Trade—Overcapacity duties will likely keep US effective tariff rate below IEEPA levels
  • New US tariffs related to the Trump administration’s Section 301 excess capacity investigation could be announced as soon as this week. These country-specific tariffs will layer on top of existing forced labor tariffs and will likely maintain the same scope of exemptions, including carveouts for certain food, electronics and energy products and imports subject to product-specific Section 232 tariffs.
  • For most of the sixteen economies investigated, we continue to expect overcapacity tariffs to restore headline country tariffs to their levels before the US Supreme Court overturned IEEPA tariffs (see note, 3 August).
  • However, the overall US average effective tariff rate (AETR) will remain below its pre-court ruling rate of 14.1% given a wider carveouts than under IEEEPA, likely rising from 10.9% to 12.4%.
  • China will continue to face the highest AETR, but its tariff disadvantage compared to ASEAN countries remains narrower than under IEEPA, somewhat reducing transshipment incentives.

Brazil—Lula’s near-term fiscal course correction will likely land on the constructive end of expectations
  • Lula’s near-term fiscal course correction will likely land on the constructive end of expectations, with a credible finance minister appointment and a constitutional amendment on mandatory spending before year-end.
  • Vice President Geraldo Alckmin is the most likely finance minister pick. The president’s concern over next year’s outlook is growing, reinforcing the odds that appointments to the Brazilian central bank will be aligned with Governor Gabriel Galipolo.
  • The more significant policy risk rests in 2027, as low growth and high inflation affect Lula’s popularity more than he expects. Lula’s approval rating currently sits at 47%, but it is likely to fall below 40% by mid-2027, and the risk of policy slippage rises sharply as approval ratings fall.

Colombia—Diluted fiscal bill still likely to pass amid mounting pressure on peso
  • President Abelardo De la Espriella’s fiscal adjustment bill suffered some modifications last week, but it also withstood demonstrations, suggesting it remains on track for approval in a watered-down form.
  • Concerns about the fiscal trajectory have seen the peso fall sharply, declining 14% this month. The move is likely exacerbated by both global factors and seasonal demand for US dollars from local companies ahead of year end.

Argentina—Likely primary suspension will improve electoral outlook for Milei
  • Congress will likely suspend the mandatory and open primaries (PASO) for 2027, which would remove an early poll, give Milei leverage over allies, and complicate the opposition’s candidate selection process. The full senate will take up the measure on 26 November.
  • Milei remains the slight favorite in the October 2027 presidential election and hopes the PASO suspension fragments the opposition enough to win in the first round outright, since a polarized runoff would be highly competitive.
  • Peronism will likely unify around a single candidate after a bruising process, and the PASO changes will give former President Cristina Fernandez de Kirchner more sway, which will likely favor former presidential candidate Sergio Massa over Buenos Aires Governor Axel Kicillof.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: latinamerica@eurasiagroup.net.
Taiwan, China—Local election gains will boost DPP before 2028 presidential election
  • Taiwan’s ruling Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) will likely win eight out of the 22 local executive races on 28 November, its best performance since 2014, while the Kuomintang (KMT) opposition will likely retain 13 localities.
  • DPP gains, including a likely victory in the opposition-held New Taipei City, will strengthen Lai’s prospects for winning the party’s 2028 presidential nomination and undermine KMT Chair Cheng Li-wun’s presidential ambitions.
  • Beijing is unlikely to retaliate in response to the results, but an unexpected DPP victory in Taipei would likely trigger a suspension of city-level exchanges. The KMT incumbent remains the likely winner of the Taipei mayoral race.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: china@eurasiagroup.net.
China—Shanghai purge shows Xi’s own appointees get no protection
  • On 24 November, Beijing announced that Chen Jining, Shanghai’s Communist Party chief and a member of the Politburo, is under investigation for “serious violations of discipline and law,” language that typically signals corruption.
  • Being promoted by President Xi Jinping offers no protection as he vets the leadership ahead of next year’s Party Congress. Chen is the fourth Politburo member purged since 2022, and all four were Xi’s own picks.
  • The probe more likely means Chen failed the party’s vetting than that Xi wanted to remove a potential successor. Beijing’s choice of Shanghai’s next party chief will be the clearest early signal of Xi’s plans for 2027, but the purge will not change China’s economic policies.

eg in brief

Iraq, Iran, US—Iraq will avoid major clash with militias as Iran keeps disarmament in check: An Iran-brokered compromise to the standoff between its proxy militia Kataib Hezbollah and the Iraqi government over control of a strategic area near Baghdad will avert short-term security shocks, including major attacks on energy infrastructure. Further US sanctions on Iraq remain unlikely, even if Iraqi Prime Minister Ali Zaidi is likely to miss the June 2027 disarmament deadline, as he retains support in Washington and the region. Iraq’s rapprochement with Gulf Cooperation Council countries will remain limited as Iran-backed Iraqi Shia militias keep Gulf states wary. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: mena@eurasiagroup.net.

Russia, Estonia—Moscow’s threats against Estonia aim to protect Baltic shipping: Moscow warned that alleged Estonian permission for Ukrainian drone overflights risked open war. Tallinn denies the accusation. The warning likely aims to protect Russian Baltic ports and shipping. Retaliation below the threshold of war and incidents at sea are more likely than a Russian land attack. Further border closures would deepen Russia’s isolation, but any cutoff of Kaliningrad would entail substantially greater risks of escalation. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: europe@eurasiagroup.net.

Sustainability—Disappointing COP31 confirms shift to smaller coalitions: The 9-20 November UN climate summit (COP31) ended without parties adopting its draft political text, the “Antalya Pact,” as divisions on fossil fuel language and discordant leadership exposed the obstacles to consensus-driven climate diplomacy. The failure has limited practical consequences, as existing climate commitments remain intact. The outcome confirms our view that climate action is moving from multilateralism to minilateralism—action taken through smaller coalitions and bilateral deals—though this shift risks excluding major emitters and weakening the UN’s credibility. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: sustainability@eurasiagroup.net.

Mexico, China—Sheinbaum will prioritize decreasing trade deficit with China following reset: Mexico and China have agreed to create a working group to increase Mexican exports, with Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard pressing Beijing to accelerate sanitary approvals for agricultural products. Stronger Mexican exports will only marginally narrow the bilateral deficit given the scale of Chinese shipments to Mexico. China is expected to export more than $130 billion in goods to Mexico in 2026, compared with roughly $10 billion in Mexican exports to China. President Claudia Sheinbaum will therefore focus less on rebalancing trade outright than on expanding export access and attracting investment. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: mexico@eurasiagroup.net.

Geoeconomics—Global political trajectories slip back into declining territory: After months in improving territory, the purchasing power parity-GDP-weighted average of EG’s political trajectories fell back below zero in October, for the first time since late 2025. The reversal reflects the erosion of buffers that have absorbed geopolitical shocks, including spillovers from the Iran conflict. Our basecase remains resilient growth through 2027, but persistently high long-term rates leave less room for error if political conditions deteriorate further. Read more here.
