DRIVING THE DAY

IT HURTS TO BE MERZ: European capitals are preparing for the prospect that German Chancellor Friedrich Merz could lose power — or at least become embroiled in a domestic political fight that pulls his attention away from the battle over the EU budget.

Wie sieht der Plan aus? At least two national governments have held internal war-gaming sessions on what happens if Merz’s fragile coalition collapses or he is replaced, according to two officials granted anonymity to speak on the sensitive talks. The sessions included mapping out what a fall would mean for negotiations over the proposed €2 trillion budget, since Merz leads the group of countries seeking “hundreds of billions of euros” in cuts.

Setting sun: With the far right notching up a string of election victories and the CDU-SPD coalition split on key policy decisions, German newspapers are declaring Kanzlerdämmerung — the chancellor’s twilight.

Diplomats see three ways out: The government collapses, Merz resigns or he plows ahead despite the growing distractions at home. But with the far-right Alternative for Germany leading the polls, “all coalition partners know that they have a great deal to lose from early elections” and will do whatever they can to avoid them, said German Green MEP Hannah Neumann.

No Mr. Compromise: The chancellor’s domestic weakness is unlikely to trigger a climbdown on the EU budget, three diplomats told Playbook. Merz will take a particularly hard line because he needs a win at home, one said. If he is forced to step down, a successor would likely follow his budget lead, according to another. Nobody “would be able to be more flexible than Merz is” on the budget because there’s no appetite in Germany for vastly increased EU payments, they said.

Keep plugging away: Those who know Merz say he won’t be distracted by his domestic woes. “Other politicians might say ‘OK, I focus less on EU politics.’ For him it’s just the opposite: It’s an additional incentive to show leadership in Europe,” said a person familiar with the chancellor’s thinking. On Thursday Merz did just that, making a landmark speech on the need to support Ukraine through the winter to convince a sometimes wavering public.

But domestic politics is already reshaping Berlin’s approach. Germany has backed away from a battle it would previously have fought over the Commission’s push to squeeze cheap imports from China. German manufacturing backs the bid, and the coalition hopes it can help stem the deindustrialization driving support for the far right. According to two national officials, Germany will reverse its historic opposition and is drafting a joint letter with France to the Commission backing the trade instruments.

BURNOUT IN THE BERLAYMONT

THE EU’S MENTAL HEALTH BILL: EU officials needing psychiatric care to deal with workplace trauma end up paying a hefty chunk of the bill themselves, warned a Brussels psychiatrist who treats burned-out European Commission staff.

Mind the gap: Thomas Van der Poorten, president of the Belgian Federation of Outpatient Psychiatry, told Sebastian Starcevic the institution must do more. The roughly €60 per session reimbursed by JSIS, the EU’s health insurance scheme for staff, can fall well short of what private psychiatrists charge — leaving employees to cover the rest, he said.

“The institutions are not bystanders to Europe’s workplace mental health crisis — they seem to actively contribute to one,” said Van der Poorten, who estimated that about 80% of his patients working at the Commission come to him after experiencing some form of workplace harassment. He argued the institution should set an “exemplary” standard in providing mental health care, given the harm he says is induced by toxic managers.

The reimbursement ceilings “are not enough,” agreed Nicolas Mavraganis, president of Union Syndicale Fédérale, the EU’s staff union umbrella group, highlighting how many staff report facing harassment. The ceiling was last raised in 2023 from €50, which is comparable to the Belgian state health insurance rebate, after a 15-year freeze.

Financial toll: While EU officials are typically highly paid and have job security, meaning they can presumably afford good care, Van der Poorten pointed out many of his patients have been off work for a while by the time they see him. “It’s the story of most mental health patients.”

Commission responds: A Commission spokesperson told Playbook the institution takes its “duty of care” toward staff facing mental health issues seriously and pointed to a “broad range” of “support mechanisms,” including support groups, an “Empathy Network” and an HR helpline.

NOT-SO-HYBRID WAR

FRONTIER STATES WANT BRUSSELS TO GET SERIOUS: Europe’s upcoming security strategy must include more intelligence sharing, investment in security services and rapid response capabilities, Estonian Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna told Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich.

This is war: Russia’s attacks on EU countries are “not like the classical military invasion” people might picture, but amount to “some kind of war,” Tsahkna said. Moscow is trying to “divide our societies, divide our unity and play with fear.”

The strategy — which is expected later this year — should distinguish sabotage and cyberattacks from the threat of a full-scale military assault, he said. The goal should be to prevent attacks, avoid panic if they happen, and show a united front. “Business as usual is not coming back.”

Make the stooges pay: Tsahkna urged EU countries to move swiftly against saboteurs caught doing the Kremlin’s dirty work, publicly attribute attacks and make the consequences clear. “Mainly they are not ideological, they are just people … after some easy money,” he said of those recruited to carry out attacks. The message to them should be: “If you do anything in our territories, we will catch you and we will put you in prison for years … so think many times before” agreeing to participate.
