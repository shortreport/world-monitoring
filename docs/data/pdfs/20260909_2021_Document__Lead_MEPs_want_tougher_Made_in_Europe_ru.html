Document: Lead MEPs want tougher Made in Europe rules, investment curbs in industry act
By Francesca Micheletti · Sep 9, 2026, 1:20 PM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — Lead lawmakers on the EU’s flagship industrial policy bill want a tightly knit “Made in Europe” club and more stringent screening of Chinese investments, a draft report obtained by POLITICO shows.

In an unusual three-way assignment, socialist Pierre Jouvet, Renew’s Christophe Grudler and Anna Cavazzini of the Greens were tasked by the European Parliament’s internal market, industry and trade committees with drafting a joint report on the Industrial Accelerator Act proposed by the European Commission in the spring.

In the report, due to be formally published as soon as Wednesday, the MEPs call for the bill to be decisively strengthened, starting with its core novelty: a Made in Europe preference in the public procurement of green technologies.

The IAA suggests that procurement in certain strategic sectors should be reserved for products made on the territory of the EU and of a potentially broad pool of trading partners — with an “opt-out” mechanism for the Commission to decide which third countries to exclude.

But French MEPs Jouvet and Grudler and German MEP Cavazzini want the Made in Europe concept to be worthy of the name.

In their 94-page draft report, which proposes a total of 199 amendments, they suggest that the starting point should be products from the bloc’s 27 member states — with the likely addition of countries that are geographically close to the bloc, such as the U.K., and members of the European Economic Area and the European Free Trade Association.

The Commission would then handle additions to the club through an “opt-in” mechanism.

The report also goes further in defining the scope of Made in Europe, spelling out seven criteria that include reciprocity, as well as respect for common environmental and labor standards. The checklist adds the risk of circumvention, “in particular through the re-export of products or components originating in third countries.”

The draft calls on the Commission to review the definition of “last substantial transformation” with the aim of requiring at least 50 percent of the value added to come from the Union and preventing circumvention of the rules of origin.

The position of the three lead MEPs puts them at odds with the Council, representing the EU’s 27 member states. In its most recent compromise text, the Council proposes a product-centric “partner origin” approach that takes trade and procurement deals into account and would avoid a binary “in or out” outcome for countries that is unsettling the bloc’s trading partners.

Investment firewall

The report calls to significantly reinforce conditions for foreign direct investment under the IAA by making all six criteria in the Commission’s proposal mandatory, rather than allowing investors leeway to choose.

Foreign investors would be required to have 60 percent of their workforce based in Europe, rather than the 50 percent threshold proposed by the Commission. Investors should also meet a 50 percent local-content requirement in certain circumstances — up from the Commission’s suggested 30 percent.

MEPs aim to finalize their report by the end of September. It would then be opened to amendments with a view to a final plenary vote in the European Parliament on Dec. 1.

If Parliament backs the line taken by the lead lawmakers on Made in Europe and FDI screening, that could set the stage for potentially difficult reconciliation negotiations with the Council, as the institutions seek a compromise.

Lawyers were skeptical that the FDI changes would survive in their current form.

“It’s an attempt to make the IAA stricter,” said Luca Picotti, a Rome-based lawyer specializing in FDI screening.

He warned that EU countries have different ideas. “Several states that are more exposed to China will push for caution, while certain elements in the report don’t always match the reality of supply chains and may entail the risk of shortages,” said Picotti.

“This will generate a flood of notifications,” commented Falk Schöning, a partner at Hogan Lovells, who noted that the Parliament is casting a much wider net than the Commission intended.
