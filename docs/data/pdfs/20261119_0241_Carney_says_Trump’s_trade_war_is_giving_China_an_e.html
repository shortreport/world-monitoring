Carney says Trump’s trade war is giving China an edge on EVs
By Zi-Ann Lum · Nov 18, 2026, 6:40 PM · View in your browser

Prime Minister Mark Carney said “America First” auto demands are holding up the Canada-U.S. trade deal as North America falls behind China on EVs.

“We are way behind in North America relative to China, as is Europe,” Carney told Andrew Ross Sorkin on Wednesday at The New York Times DealBook Summit. He said a “series of issues” is keeping Ottawa and the Trump administration from reaching an interim trade deal.

“We’re spending way too much time on a trade war around autos, as opposed to ‘where is this sector going and how are we pooling our resources?’ We are very keen to do that, but we need a willing partner in the United States.”

Carney joined the summit by video link from Shenzhen after attending an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation leaders’ welcome dinner hosted by Chinese President Xi Jinping. President Donald Trump and Carney met on the sidelines of the summit to discuss trade.

The Canadian leader said Trump is a different person behind closed doors, saying the president is “very personable” and “listens a lot more in private.” But there’s no trade deal yet.

“It should have been done six months ago, but we’re not going to sign something that doesn’t make sense for Canada,” Carney said, adding that his government has been busy signing new trade deals and chasing C$1 trillion in investments over the next five years.

“If the U.S. doesn’t want to do the deal, we’re busy,” he said.

Carney teased earlier this month that U.S. and Canadian officials will hash out a deal “in the coming weeks.” White House trade counselor Peter Navarro has since signaled Canada’s decision to move out of step with U.S. policy on Chinese EVs is “offensive in the extreme.”

Carney and his officials have played down the idea that closer ties with Beijing and opening Canada to Chinese EVs will damage Ottawa’s relationship with Washington.

The prime minister explained to reporters earlier in Shenzhen on Wednesday that while his government is making inroads with China, Canada’s trading relationship with the U.S. is more complex.

“There’s a wider range of issues,” he said, repeating the message that the U.S. has “fundamentally changed” its trade objectives with the world.

“We have been making progress,” he said, calling discussions a “two steps forward, one step back type thing.”

The two sides were on the brink of a deal to stave off Section 338 tariffs when trade talks collapsed in August. The Canadian side blamed last-minute concerns about sovereignty and unresolved issues over the refusal of U.S. negotiators to budge on tariff relief for Canadian imports of medium- and heavy-duty trucks.

Section 338 tariffs came into force, hitting 5% of bilateral trade, including products compliant under Trump’s free-trade agreement, goods valued around $20 billion. After talks collapsed, Trump threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian-made vehicles, steel and automotive parts to 50% on Jan. 1 — a 27-week runway to keep Ottawa around the negotiation table.

While U.S. and Canadian officials have communicated regularly since talks collapsed, those conversations intensified in recent weeks, right before the midterms where Trump’s trade war against Canada became a factor in some key races.

Reshoring manufacturing has been a core priority of the second Trump administration and the integrated North American auto industry has been stuck in the middle of a trade war.

Canadian sales of U.S.-built cars plummeted 20 percent in the first half of 2026 compared to the same period last year, while tariffs have forced some carmakers, such as Stellantis, to move models to U.S. facilities.

While Carney and Janice Charette, the chief trade negotiator with the U.S., keep saying Canada will always be a country that builds cars, it’s unclear how much of a role U.S. carmakers will play in the Canadian market.

Japanese automakers Toyota and Honda account for 75% of vehicle production in Canada. But stacked Trump administration tariffs and Ottawa’s countertariffs have made cars made in the U.S. and Canada more expensive, which has made cheaper foreign imports from South Korea more appealing to Canadian buyers.

The Carney government is working to woo South Korean and Chinese automakers to build plants in Canada. But the prospect of Chinese EVs being made in Canada, let alone being imported, sold and potentially driven across the Canada-U.S. border, has rankled the White House and congressional members on both sides of the aisle.

In Ottawa, prolonged trade tensions with Washington have encouraged the Carney government to pursue trade deals with India, the Mercosur bloc, the United Arab Emirates, Philippines and ASEAN.

But Ottawa’s determination to diversify markets for exporters risks inflaming trade tensions with Washington, no matter who occupies the White House. On Capitol Hill, China and Chinese transhipment is viewed as a problem.

Carney’s focus on diversifying trade and deepening relations with the European Union, guided by the creation of “associate membership” status for Canada, has raised eyebrows among opposition parties and former Canadian and American diplomats.

“Even after a couple of years of attempted diversification, more than 70% of Canada’s exports still go to the United States,” former Biden-era U.S. Ambassador to Canada David Cohen told a parliamentary committee this week.

“This is a long road, and how far Canada is going to be able to get with diversification is an open question,” he said.
