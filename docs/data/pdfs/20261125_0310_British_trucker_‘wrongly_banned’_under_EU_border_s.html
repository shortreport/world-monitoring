British trucker ‘wrongly banned’ under EU border system
By Sophie Inge · Nov 24, 2026, 7:09 PM · View in your browser

LONDON — A British truck driver has been wrongly banned from the EU because of failings in the bloc’s new digital border controls, according to the haulage firm that employs him, raising concerns many more may be affected.

The driver was barred from traveling in the Schengen area after being falsely accused of exceeding the EU limit of 90 days in any 180-day period, their employer said, leaving them unable to work or vacation in the bloc for a year.

With drivers frequently reporting inconsistent border control checks across the continent, the Road Haulage Association trade body fears thousands more drivers could be at risk of being wrongly banned.

The problem is linked to the EU’s Entry/Exit System (EES), which digitally records when non-EU nationals enter and leave the bloc. The system, which was supposed to be fully operational by April 10 this year after four years of delays, is designed to replace physical passport stamps with an EU-wide database supported by biometric data.

But it has already been beset with a litany of technical issues and haulage firms fear discrepancies in how border authorities record drivers’ movements could lead to further wrongful accusations of overstaying.

Sarah Boyson, a director at E M Rogers Ltd, the Northampton-based haulage firm which employs the banned driver, told POLITICO there were “very significant inconsistencies” in the way the checks were being carried out across different ports.

While smaller ports such as Gothenburg in Sweden and the Hook of Holland were found to have the most stringent controls, busier ports — particularly Calais and Dover, where French authorities carry out checks — are less consistent, according to Boyson.

For example, some drivers traveling through Dover and Calais late at night reported that passports were not being scanned and biometric checks were not being carried out. In some cases, drivers were checked either entering or leaving the Schengen area, but not both.

Over the past month alone, Boyson said well over half of the firm’s 62 drivers reported a partial or missing check by authorities in EU member states.

“If you’re recorded going in but not coming out, then you’ve got that open journey which is still recording days,” she explained.

Missing data

The discrepancies are leaving drivers unable to reconcile the digital records with their own travel logs.

“What the drivers are finding is that when they’re checking online, it’s not matching with their own records that they’re taking, and that there is data missing,” Boyson said. “And it does seem to be very widespread.”

In the case of the banned driver, Swedish authorities determined they had spent 92 days in the bloc. The company’s own records showed they had spent just 68 days there.

The firm said it did not want to identity of the driver out of concern for their privacy but has chosen to speak out for fear it could happen to more employees as well as those in the wider industry.

In the end, the company chose not to appeal, citing the cost and uncertainty of challenging what it considered to be an official EU-wide record with its own internal data.

“We were advised we’d have to go to a Swedish immigration lawyer, and then the burden of proof would be on us to prove that we hadn’t overstayed,” Boyson said.

The firm could provide the driver’s manual records, ferry crossings and vehicle-tracking data, but was unsure whether those would be sufficient to overturn the authorities’ decision.

The firm said it was able to reassign the driver to another domestic role. But Boyson warned that other drivers would not necessarily be so lucky.

“If it happened on a more regular basis, people would be losing their jobs because … we can’t absorb the cost of reallocating people to a lower paid job and still pay them that higher rate,” she said.

Impact on trade

Alastair Gunn, the Road Haulage Association’s trade policy lead, said the organization was concerned there “may well be multiple cases” of drivers being wrongly banned.

The impact goes beyond the firms and the drivers affected, he pointed out.

“It also begs the question: what damage is this going to be doing to U.K.-EU trade?” Gunn said. “Which supply chains are going to have to find other ways of coping if the drivers that they rely upon are no longer available for some reason or are restricted in how much work they can do?”

A European Commission spokesperson claimed the EES system was “working well” across member states one year in. They said that there were “strong safeguards in place to protect travellers from being wrongly identified as overstayers,” adding that member states have a “clear obligation” to register all border crossings falling within scope of the EES and to complete any incomplete entry and exit records.”

They pointed out that travelers have the right to request the correction of inaccurate data and challenge any incorrect record under the EES legal framework and Schengen Borders Code.

The Commission is “in close contact with member states on this matter and has been calling on Member States to correctly use the EES and register all border crossings as a matter of priority, underlying the potential impact on travellers,” the spokesperson continued.

The negative impact of EES on hauliers was recognized in the Commission’s Visa Strategy published earlier this year, where it noted that some third country professionals who heavily rely on mobility between member states, including truck drivers servicing EU businesses, “may require access to different member states for more than 90 days within 180 days, without needing long stay or residence in the EU.”

British hauliers’ concerns follow last week’s U.K.-EU summit which saw the U.K. and EU agree deals on agrifood, emissions trading and youth mobility. There was no mention of the EES rules in the summit joint statement.

“Unsurprisingly, our key concern was again overlooked,” Gunn said, pointing out that “the vast majority of supply chains are affected” by the EES rules, from the transportation of SPS goods, military logistics and traveling musicians.

“The EU’s own visa strategy now looks better attuned to the needs of British hauliers than the UK-EU Joint Statement,” he added. “This needs to be addressed in future negotiations as a win-win supporting economies [on] both sides of the Channel.”

A U.K. government spokesperson confirmed they were aware of inaccuracies in some EES records affecting British nationals, including incomplete records and that they had raised the issue with the European Commission.

“British nationals should check the latest FCDO Travel Advice before travelling,” they added. “If they believe their EES record is incorrect, they should raise this with the relevant authorities and follow the guidance set out on Travel Advice.”

The French and Swedish governments did not respond to requests for comment.
