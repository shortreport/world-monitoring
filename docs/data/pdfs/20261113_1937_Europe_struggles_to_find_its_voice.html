DRIVING THE DAY

WHO SPEAKS FOR EUROPE? European Council President António Costa is emerging as the front-runner to represent the EU in eventual Russia-Ukraine peace talks. But resistance from Europe’s biggest powers and deep divisions over the role of top diplomat Kaja Kallas threaten to derail the efforts to secure the bloc a seat at the negotiating table.

As EU leaders gather in Dublin today to thrash out who should speak for Europe, Playbook’s conversations with leaders, diplomats and officials reveal a growing consensus around Costa — though the bloc’s most powerful capitals might not accept being represented by Brussels at all.

Look no further: If you ask Estonian Prime Minister Kristen Michal, the answer is obvious: it should be Costa and Kallas. “Putin does not have to like them,” he told me in an interview in Dublin. What’s important is they are “accountable” to the EU and will presumably hold their positions until 2029.

The pair makes sense on paper: Costa’s role means he represents the EU on the world stage, while Kallas handles matters of foreign policy. The Baltics, Luxembourg, Poland, Italy and others support Costa taking the lead, diplomats and officials told Playbook.

But while most diplomats we spoke to are on board with Costa, some are far less keen on Kallas, who is seen as too hawkish on Russia. “That’s a joke, right?” a European diplomat (granted anonymity for obvious reasons) said, adding that Kallas is “probably the worst person you could pick.”

Then again: Her hawkishness is precisely what makes her the right pick, a second senior European diplomat argued. “It would make much more sense,” they told my colleague Zoya Sheftalovich. “That’s what Putin would do. He would pick whoever you don’t want to talk to.”

Both Kallas and Costa have signaled they’re up for the job. Kallas told reporters in Dublin yesterday that she and Costa were chosen for their roles in 2024, two years after the invasion of Ukraine, on the understanding that they would be the ones talking to Russia someday. And Costa stressed in his letter to Italy’s Giorgia Meloni on this topic that the EU is represented by its institutions.

Non and nein: That might not fly with France and Germany, my colleagues Jacopo Barigazzi, Giorgio Leali and Hans von der Burchard report. The EU’s two biggest countries will oppose any format that means giving up their seat at the table, a diplomat and an EU official said.

Wild card: Another name that has come up is that of Jonathan Powell, the U.K.’s national security adviser (and chief of staff to Tony Blair when he was PM), a European diplomat in Britain told my colleague Esther Webber.

Lower your expectations: The summit today is unlikely to result in an envoy being formally selected but will result in an “understanding” around the leaders’ table, a senior EU official said. “The individual or the structure itself, I think, is something that we need to tease through a little bit more,” Irish Foreign Minister Helen McEntee told me.

Whatever happens, you’ll want to tune in today: “It has endless possibilities. You could even have a TV show about it,” Michal joked, adding that the topic had already come up briefly among leaders on Thursday. “But we are saving the best part for tomorrow.”

YOUR SUMMIT CHEAT SHEET

WHAT TO WATCH TODAY: Artificial intelligence may be the official focus in Dublin, but leaders have a packed side agenda — from Ukraine’s place in peace talks to the race for the top job at the European Central Bank, Gerardo Fortuna reports. Here are five things on our radar:

1 — AI frontier: Jensen Huang’s fall has been a reminder that Europe has largely been a spectator in the global AI race, Pieter Haeck writes. Leaders will debate whether Europe should build frontier models to rival U.S. and Chinese developers or focus on applications. António Costa wants Europe to become “a leading and sovereign player” across the AI value chain — the obstacle is funding computing power and infrastructure. Mistral CEO Arthur Mensch and AI pioneer Geoffrey Hinton will address leaders.

2 — The Ukraine envoy question: Costa agreed to put the issue formally on the agenda after Giorgia Meloni called the EU’s approach “all over the place.” The bloc remains deeply divided over who it should choose as its representative — yet the task of choosing has become increasingly urgent after U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff said Europeans would be invited to the next round of Ukraine negotiations.

3 — Ukraine’s €70 billion gap: While peace talks gather pace, Kyiv is also facing a massive funding shortfall next year. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz wants Ukraine financing linked to talks on the EU’s budget, which is being resisted by governments wary of tying the two together.

4 — The MFF: The EU’s next long-term budget is another issue not formally on the agenda, but with the clock ticking, each meeting is a potential arena to narrow the gulf between the warring camps’ positions ahead of a new negotiating document expected around Nov. 21. Merz is set to discuss the budget with Macron, though no breakthrough is expected.

5 — ECB jobs bargain: Macron and Merz are also expected to discuss the race to succeed Christine Lagarde. Spain’s Pablo Hernández de Cos and former Dutch central banker Klaas Knot are leading contenders. The contest also covers two other senior ECB posts, with Paris and Berlin both coveting the chief economist job, according to two officials.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Much of the prep work for today’s informal leaders’ meeting will have been done by sherpas … but what are they? Sherpas are personal representatives (or chief negotiators) who carry out behind-the-scenes work for ministers, heads of state and government ahead of international summits. Named after the Himalayan mountain guides who lead climbers up peaks, political sherpas are meant to guide leaders through difficult diplomatic terrain.

CANBERRA INSISTS THE TRADE DEAL WILL HOLD

EXCLUSIVE — IGNORE THE NOISE: Canberra is “absolutely confident” the EU-Australia free trade agreement will get through Australia’s parliamentary processes, despite the opposition coalition threatening to bring it down, Australia’s Industry Minister Tim Ayres told Zoya Sheftalovich.

The trade deal is “unambiguously” in the interests of Australian businesses and workers, he said. “You’d have to have rocks in your head to oppose it.”

Damage control: The bullish comments come after Australia’s opposition’s trade spokesperson Matt Canavan, who previously called the country’s agreement with the EU the “worst trade deal ever,” again took aim at the agreement, criticizing its terms for farmers and climate commitments. In response, the European Parliament’s Australia delegation Chair Seán Kelly warned that if Australia reneges on the deal, it could be “buried” for decades, with Brussels having already shut the door to reopening negotiations.

The worrying math: Canavan’s opposition coalition cannot kill the deal on its own, but it could help block the laws needed to put it into effect in Australia’s Senate.

History lesson: Negotiated over eight years, the deal will cut tariffs and open market opportunities, while deepening the EU’s access to Australian critical minerals. Talks collapsed in 2023 over farm access and product names, but Donald Trump’s tariffs and concerns about reliance on China’s critical minerals gave the deal fresh geopolitical urgency earlier this year. The two sides reached an agreement in March.

On the Horizon: Ayres was in Brussels to formally sign up to Horizon Europe, which will give Australian researchers access to the EU’s €95 billion research program.
