DRIVING THE DAY

ANYWHERE BUT BRUSSELS: European Commission officials rushed to apply for a slew of plum jobs that are opening up in the EU’s diplomatic network in the coming months, including the head of its delegation to the United Nations in New York and head of its office at the OECD in Paris, two EU officials told Playbook.

The surge of applications — described by one as a “gold rush” — coincides with reports of employee malaise and looming budget cuts at the Commission, where staff were asked to conduct scenarios in which the EU’s overall development budget was cut by as much as 40%. It also reflects an ongoing turf war between Ursula von der Leyen’s European Commission and the European External Action Service (EEAS) run by Kaja Kallas over the future of EU foreign policy.

With budget cuts looming and staffers complaining about reduced autonomy in a highly centralized Commission, the appeal of ditching rainy Brussels for a far-flung locale where top jobs come with free housing and staff is more appealing than ever, the officials said.

Wish you were there? Some 30 key jobs in the EEAS’ global network of about 145 delegations are up for grabs in 2027, with standouts including head of the EU’s delegation to the tropical island of Mauritius, deputy head of delegation in Beijing and head of delegation in Saudi Arabia.

The juicy bit: Applicants include Commission staffers ranking as high as director-general — the highest level of EU civil servant below politically appointed commissioners — and senior national diplomats, the officials told me.

But it causes a headache: Managing the huge EEAS “rotation” is a key task facing new EEAS Secretary-General Kajsa Ollongren, who got started on Sept. 1 and has yet to replace the body’s outgoing head of resource management. The recruitment process is being overseen by acting officials, Elsa Fenet and Olivier Bailly.

Ollongren is about halfway through a tour of European capitals, with more than 15 stops planned, including a trip to Warsaw last Friday geared at gathering ideas on EU foreign policy.

New sheriff in town: The new secretary-general made a strong impression during her first appearances at Coreper (meetings of ambassadors), two EU diplomats said. She is part of a new EEAS leadership team alongside France’s former Ambassador to NATO David Cvach, who’s now deputy secretary-general for peace, security and defense, and Estonian senior diplomat Matti Maasikas, deputy secretary-general for geoeconomics and interinstitutional issues. “Ollongren is taking her time to see who she can trust and how the system works,” said one of the officials.

What’s next: With pre-screening of applications now done, formal job interviews are set to run through December and January.

The bottom line: The European Commission may be winning a turf war with the EEAS, but its employees are more eager than ever to trade the Schuman roundabout for the excitement of a foreign posting.

CLASH OVER GAZA FUNDING

GAZA CRISIS DISRUPTS PARLIAMENT BUDGET TALKS: The Ursula von der Leyen-backing majority in the European Parliament is at odds over whether to fund the reconstruction of Gaza from the bloc’s next seven-year budget — yet another difficulty in the negotiations that are exposing major differences between national governments.

Final countdown: This is one of the final hurdles to overcome in the Parliament, with lawmakers aiming to finalize a common position next Thursday on the Global Europe Fund, a €200 billion pot of money governing EU foreign aid. Another sensitive issue is whether to use EU money to build migrant return hubs abroad and discourage crossings to Europe.

Von der Leyen’s European People’s Party rejected an amendment from the Greens, seen by Playbook, to ring-fence €15 billion for Gaza in the next budget. The EPP argued that it’s premature to ringfence cash for Gaza before a clear plan to rebuild the territory is agreed, said a lawmaker with knowledge of the discussions.

But the Socialists and Democrats, the Left and parts of Renew are pushing in the opposite direction from the EPP — even though the final text is unlikely to commit funding. “The most basic obligation of the EU is to provide sufficient humanitarian funding for the people of Gaza,” Irish Renew MEP Barry Andrews told Playbook. Socialists and Democrats lawmaker Robert Biedroń said he was sure they’d find a “compromise that will guarantee a meaningful EU involvement in the reconstruction of Gaza. It’s a priority for the S&D Group.”

Sounding the alarm: Civil society groups CONCORD, EPLO and VOICE, which together represent 200 NGOs, warned that cuts to Global Europe would undermine the EU’s role in the world and its response to international crises.

Jean-Nicolas Beuze, UNHCR representative to the EU, told Playbook: “We should be careful about thinking spending less abroad automatically saves us money. Sometimes you save money today and end up paying much more tomorrow, when a faraway crisis has become much bigger and harder to manage.”

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner has presented plans to address sudden mass arrivals on the bloc’s borders, including a ramped-up role for Frontex, the EU’s border agency. But what does Frontex do? It has its own uniformed law enforcement officers (the Standing Corps) that can assist national authorities. It also coordinates joint operations and gathers and shares intelligence with EU countries, as well as sending back data to its base in Warsaw.

OI, URSULA!

PROTESTS ROCK THE SCHUMAN ROUNDABOUT: Noise from demos now regularly taking place on Brussels’ Schuman roundabout can easily be heard on the Berlaymont’s 13th floor — where Ursula von der Leyen works and often sleeps, two EU officials told Playbook.

Location, location, location: While protests have long been a feature of Brussels’ EU quarter (tractors and hay bales, anyone?), rallies are being staged more frequently on the roundabout since the completion of a renovation that’s managed to turn it into a flat, featureless disk. It’s now ideal for protesting — like Paris’s Place de la République or Maidan Square in Kyiv.

Over the past 10 days, Playbook observed — and heard — protests on the roundabout by climate activists (including an 8 a.m. “Rave against climate inaction”), animal rights activists (holding up placards with the face of Animal Welfare Commissioner Olivér Várhelyi) and steel industry advocates (featuring genuine-looking coffins).

Legal fine print: Unlike the immediate vicinity of Belgium’s Royal Palace, the Federal Parliament or the prime minister’s office — all “neutral zones” where protesting is illegal — there is no permanent restriction on protesting near EU buildings. Organizers must obtain an authorization from a local authority, in this case Brussels Mayor Philippe Close.

Brussels vs. the Berlaymont: “If the Brussels commune prefers, we can take this circus on the road” — i.e., to one of the bloc’s other locations, said a second EU official.
