New centrist “saviours” unlikely to save the centre
23 Nov 2026 04:16 EST
Discover more on the Portal
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:
  • The French presidential campaign is drifting towards a second round runoff between the extremes of right and left which would all but guarantee victory for the far right candidate Marine Le Pen; Eurasia Group’s odds of a Le Pen-Jean Luc Mélenchon runoff remain 20%.
  • A bid by the four-time Olympic Judo gold medallist Teddy Riner to emerge as a “saviour” candidate risks further muddling the already crowded race in the centre; another candidate—the centre-right mayor of Cannes, David Lisnard—has jumped to 9 to 11% of the first round vote, overtaking the former centrist favourite Edouard Philippe in one poll—but his tax-cutting, state-shrinking policies will make it hard for him to assemble the left-right coalition he needs to rise above his centrist rivals and Mélenchon in the first round on 18 April.
  • Eurasia Group is revising its basecase to give Le Pen a 45% chance of being the next President of the Republic (from 40% previously) while Mélenchon’s chances remain 10%; we are also reducing the chances of Philippe from 35% to 30%.

Efforts to secure a “primary of the centre” to prevent a runoff between the extremes will not succeed. Negotiations will resume this Thursday on a primary to thin and energise the crowded field of establishment candidates. The official centre-left candidate Raphael Glucksmann has refused to take part. So has Philippe. The breakthrough pro-market candidate David Lisnard, who overtook Philippe in a new Toluna-Harris poll today, is refusing to compete unless all his rivals do so. Some other deal may be possible, however. Philippe has agreed to meet other centre and right contenders next week. They will discuss a possible pact in which all would agree to withdraw in favour of the best-placed candidate in February—possibly after a debate or a mega opinion poll.

First-round polling remains daunting for all the candidates of the pro-European mainstream. So is the bleak and angry national mood which demands radical change but rejects any suggestion that voters must share the pain of reducing France’s large debt load.

An independent run announced last week by Riner is unlikely to be the final addition to a sprawling field of 30 or more candidates. Riner, a popular figure, immediately polled at 4-6% but is having difficulty defining his campaign.

The former Socialist President François Hollande is also expected to offer himself as a “saviour” candidate in the new year. Speculation continues on the plans of the retiring ECB president, Christine Lagarde, who is attracting almost as much support as Philippe.

Polling numbers in the last month have not shifted dramatically but make depressing reading for all of France’s mainstream candidates—with one exception. Le Pen has slightly strengthened her position and commands 33-35% of the first-round vote, despite the dismissive reception for her unrealistic economic programme.

Mélenchon, meanwhile, has profited from the social unrest in October to rise above Philippe in the polls. He now occupies second place on 17% to 20% of the round one vote (but is seen as losing to Le Pen by at least 66% to 33% if he reaches the run-off on 2 May).

Philippe has fallen five points since September to 15%—and even to a worrying 10% in the Toluna-Harris poll today. The other centre and centre-right candidates Gabriel Attal and Bruno Retailleau are struggling on about 7%. Glucksmann has fallen two points to around 13%.

Lisnard, once seen as a maverick independent too liberal economically to succeed in France, is the only candidate other than Le Pen and Mélenchon who will be satisfied with the opinion polls. Lisnard has risen to 9 to 10% in recent surveys—and even reached 11%, level with Glucksmann and a point ahead of Philippe in the Harris poll today. He has the advantage of never having served in national government and having no links with the unpopular President Emmanuel Macron. Nonetheless, his tax-cutting, state-shrinking policies will make it hard for him to assemble the kind of left-right coalition that he needs to rise above his centrist rivals and Mélenchon in the first round on 18 April.

The race in the centre will remain muddled but a single, mainstream champion remains likely to emerge in the new year. Le Pen seems certain to top the poll in round one. Mélenchon is on course to match the 22% he scored in 2022. If he can turn out his passionate support amongst the young and in the multi-racial, inner suburbs—far from certain—he might even reach 25%.

The scattered mainstream can reach that figure only by concentrating support behind one candidate. Philippe believes that process is best left to an informal “primary of the opinion polls.” This judgement may be right. By January moderate voters (who are at least 40% of the electorate) will likely take fright at the extreme policies offered by Le Pen and Mélenchon and look around for a competent alternative.

Nonetheless, that does not necessarily mean that moderate voters will turn back to him. The anti-Macron mood in the country has turned out to be greater than he bargained for. As an ex-Macron-era Prime Minister with only sensible, gradual reforms to offer, he is struggling to be heard.

Five months is a long time in French electoral politics and certainties have a habit of dissolving in the new year. We believe that Mélenchon’s rise is more fragile than it seems and that Philippe, though wounded, is still the most likely candidate to prevent an “all extremes” run-off in May. Nonetheless, we are adjusting our basecase to increase Le Pen’s chances of being the next President of the Republic from 40% to 45%; we reduce Philippe’s chances from 35% to 30% and hold Mélenchon’s chances at 10%.
