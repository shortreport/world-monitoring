Fight over Cook will not prevent Fed tightening cycle
17 Nov 2026 11:24 EST

Fight over Cook will not prevent Fed tightening cycle. Fed governor Lisa Cook’s lawyer said she won’t attend the White House’s hearing on mortgage-fraud allegations, scheduled for 14 December. The language of the statement implies Cook and her legal team view the hearing as illegitimate.

  • This is a signal Cook will contest any dismissal after the 14 December hearing. This is likely to result in another draw-out legal fight in which Cook’s chances of prevailing will be lower than in its previous iteration. With the White House apparently following the letter of the Supreme Court’s rule on the legally required process, the fight is likely to revolve around questions of “cause,” which is no obvious win for Cook.
  • In combination with the White House mounting attacks on Fed board members for rate hikes, a fight over Cook will likely be a source of volatility in the relationship between the White House and the Fed next year. Markets may be underpricing this risk, seeing the current dispute as a repeat of Trump’s failed attempt to fire Cook last year.
  • Tensions over Cook and potentially other board members are unlikely to stop the Fed’s tightening cycle. Thanks to Democrats’ control of the Senate, Trump probably will not be able to replace any vacancies with a more friendly governor, and Fed chair Kevin Warsh—let alone other members of the board—is unlikely to respond to tensions over personnel by backing off the current rate path.

Answers to common questions about US-Canada relations after the midterms. Democrats' resounding midterm victory does not shift our view that negotiations on a limited US-Canada trade deal are likely to drag into 2027, as Prime Minister Mark Carney still must decide whether to spend political capital to secure a measure of economic relief.

  • Trump is unlikely to pivot to a more conciliatory posture. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s declining influence in the administration is a positive sign for a potential deal, though, as he has been a significant impediment to reducing auto tariffs; if he is removed it would strengthen voices more inclined towards a deal.
  • Democrats in the next Congress will pass symbolic measures aimed at opposing tariffs on Canada but have no viable path to reclaiming trade authority from the executive branch, leaving the White House in the driver’s seat.
  • Although some Republicans in a more empowered non-MAGA wing of the party will likely chart a more moderate course on the US-Canada relationship ahead of the 2028 election, Canadian public opinion toward the US is unlikely to significantly change under the current administration.
  • For more, please see Five common questions about US-Canada relations after the midterms, 16 November 2026.

Medicaid expansions would be a major fiscal hit. Democratic gains in Wisconsin, Texas, Georgia, and Kansas raise the prospect that those three states could finally opt into the Affordable Care Act’s Medicaid expansion, likely adding just under 2 million individuals in total.

  • In each state, Democratic governors need assent from the state legislatures. Wisconsin, where Democrats gained control of the state legislature, is probably the most likely to expand.
  • Expansion in the other three states will depend on whether Democrats can get enough Republican support for expansion. Some Republicans in these states have supported expanding Medicaid in various forms in the past; reeling from midterm defeats, state-level Republicans may now wish to get on board with expansion, while the fact that recipients will be subject to new work requirements starting in 2027 makes it an easier political argument to sell to their current voters.
  • Expansion would have major fiscal implications, since the federal government would cover almost all of the cost for new enrollees, who would make between 100% and 138% of the federal poverty line. A rough estimate is that expansion would cost roughly $180 billion over the ten-year budget window, or roughly 0.04% of GDP on an annual average. This would more than offset the savings from one of the major scheduled cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, limits on state-directed payments.

"If there is, they will pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before. I hope it's not, but we take it very seriously because they happen to be crazy." - President Donald Trump, responding to reports of an Iranian dirty bomb threat targeting Washington, DC. While Iranian threats to individual US leaders have been relatively common, they have increased in frequency and scope as of late, likely driving the ongoing trend of cabinet officials relocating their homes to military bases. Intelligence disclosures of this type are almost always intentional, given the risk posed to intelligence sources and methods, and likely reflect a judgement on behalf of the administration that this information will be politically useful for the President, most particularly in regard to the war in Iran.
