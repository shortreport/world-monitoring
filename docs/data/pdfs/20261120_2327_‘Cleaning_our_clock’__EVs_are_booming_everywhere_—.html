‘Cleaning our clock’: EVs are booming everywhere — except the US
By Sara Schonhardt, Zia Weise · Nov 20, 2026, 3:26 PM · View in your browser

ANTALYA, Turkey — Electric vehicles are flying off showroom floors from Thailand to Turkey. In the U.S., not so much.

The trend showcases how the Trump administration is making the U.S. a global outlier on electrification. Running cars on electrons instead of gasoline is among the most visible steps nations are taking to reduce fossil fuels at the climate talks in Turkey.

Many countries are offering tax breaks, rebates and other subsidies to accelerate EV sales and manufacturing. Those types of incentives were repealed under President Donald Trump, putting the U.S. in the slow lane on EV uptake.

“One in four new passenger vehicles sold in Australia this year has been electric,” Australia’s climate and energy minister, Chris Bowen, said Wednesday at the climate conference. “Five years ago, it was just one in 50.”

Worldwide, EVs are expected to account for one-third of all car sales this year, up from 5% in 2021, according to the International Energy Agency. The U.S. lags behind those trends following Trump’s moves to cancel a Biden-era program that offered $7,500 rebates to EV buyers. Sales surged last year as American motorists raced to dealerships ahead of its expiration, before slowing down.

Electric cars account for 6% of new sales in the U.S. so far this year, compared to 27% in Germany and nearly 50% in Vietnam, according to the energy research firm Ember.

The shift comes as countries are putting electrification at the center of the climate talks in Antalya, with the presidency pushing a target to accelerate the shift to EVs, heat pumps and electrified industrial processes. Proponents say it can cut greenhouse gases and insulate people from sudden spikes in oil prices, like those being felt from the Iran war.

But scaling up EVs comes with geopolitical risks. Critics say it could cement China’s position as the global leader of EV manufacturing. That has led many countries to put tariffs or other limits on Chinese imports, though none quite like the U.S.

Trump has maintained a 100% tariff on Chinese EVs, even as he unwound regulations and incentives that were meant to push domestic manufacturers to make more electric models. Some analysts say those moves will set the U.S. back at a time when everyone else is moving forward.

“The United States is at the leading edge of AI because we see that that is the future. How is it possible that we are not seeing the future of renewable electricity, that we are not seeing the future of electric vehicles,” said Jules Kortenhorst, co-chair of the Energy Transitions Commission, a global think tank. “The country will look back at this era and blame the current administration for the foolish decisions made to reverse the momentum that we saw out of the Biden administration.”

China produces about 70% of EVs globally, and its exports have skyrocketed since the Iran war began in February, amounting to more than $12 billion in exports in September, according to Ember.

Many of those cars are going to Asian countries such as Thailand and Vietnam. Canada made a deal to import a small number of Chinese EVs amid an ongoing trade war with Washington. Prime Minister Mark Carney said Wednesday that the dispute is boosting China’s EV edge.

Shrugging off China

In the European Union, governments are less welcoming of China’s cars. The bloc has imposed tariffs in a bid to protect its legacy car manufacturers. It’s also weakening its clean car legislation, but that hasn’t stopped EVs from booming on the continent.

New registrations of gasoline-powered cars are on the decline while EVs made up almost 23% of all vehicle sales in the first nine months of the year, up from 17% last year. The share has been ramping up as fuel prices remain high due to the Iran war. EVs accounted for 30% of sales in October.

Sales are also soaring in the United Kingdom — overtaking hybrid and petrol cars in October — even as the country considers softening its EV targets.

No place, it seems, is eluding the shift quite like the U.S. In Southeast Asia, EV sales doubled last year, and in Latin America they rose by an eye-watering 75%, according to the IEA.

Back in the U.S., where big gas-powered vehicles dominate the market, EV sales crept to their highest levels in the past year during the third quarter. It was driven in part by high gas prices, according to Cox Automotive.

That has left American automakers “balancing two realities,” said Stephanie Valdez Streaty, an analyst at Cox.

“The need to build the vehicles U.S. consumers want today, including gasoline-powered vehicles and hybrids, while continuing to invest in the technologies they will need to compete tomorrow,” she said in an email. “Pulling back from future market investment could leave U.S. manufacturers playing catch-up as electrification continues to advance globally.”

The risk of the U.S. getting left in the dust isn’t lost on some American officials.

“Our greatest economic competitor is China, and they are cleaning our clock on low-carbon green growth,” California Gov. Gavin Newsom told reporters here last week. “I don’t know that we will have a functioning automobile manufacturing economy in the United States in a decade unless we wake up to that competition.”

Turkey’s Togg

U.S. car manufacturers have pivoted away from making EVs under the Trump administration by shifting back to gas-powered vehicles and repurposing EV battery plants into making batteries for electric storage.

Some countries are pushing their own models forward, despite the competition from China. Turkey is one of them.

It used its role as host of the climate talks to display the newest models from Togg, Turkey’s domestic EV maker and a pet project of the country’s president, Recep Tayyip Erdoğan. In the summit’s green zone — an exhibition space for companies — attendees inspected the cars as video advertisements played on repeat.

One shows a Togg sedan driving through pristine forests with mountains peaking in the distance. “We are redesigning the future,” the ad says, “because we believe in this road and this journey.”

EVs are at the heart of COP31’s flagship target — increasing electricity to account for 35% of final energy demand by 2035.

It comes as the world is rapidly electrifying sectors such as transportation, industry and technology. About 23% of energy use is currently provided by electric generation. That can offset higher-polluting fuels like oil.

China’s EV fleet is avoiding around 1.5 million barrels per day of oil consumption, according to the IEA, which says it’s increasingly affordable to power cars with electricity. Ethiopia, which is hosting next year’s climate talks, has banned imports of gasoline- and diesel-powered vehicles. Roughly 5% of its total car fleet is already electrified, compared to around 2% in the U.S.

“This is actually an African country, and they are charging ahead … of course by leveraging Chinese products and technologies,” said Li Shuo, director of the China Climate Hub at the Asia Society Policy Institute. “That’s the future.”
