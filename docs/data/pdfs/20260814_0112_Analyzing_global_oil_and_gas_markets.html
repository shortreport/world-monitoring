Analyzing global oil and gas markets

13 Aug 2026 12:07 EDT Listen to the recording
  • Eurasia Group assigns 60% probability to US-Iran de-escalation by mid-September, with a partial interim arrangement reopening the Strait of Hormuz (SoH); oil prices expected to fall to $65–$80/barrel on a deal.
  • European gas storage is at ~60%, below the EU's 80% target; Eurasia Group puts 65% odds on Europe clearing winter without severe shortages, but the 35% risk scenario is meaningful and driven by stacked vulnerabilities.
  • The SoH crisis is accelerating permanent structural shifts — alternative pipeline routes, electrification, and demand reduction — that will durably reduce the strait's role in global energy flows by 2028–2030.

Iran De-escalation: Base Case and Oil Price Outlook

Eurasia Group's base case — 60% by mid-September — is a partial, interim US-Iran arrangement that reopens the SoH. The deal will not be durable. It will not replicate even the June agreement in strength. Key structural obstacles persist: mines in the central waterway will take months to clear, limiting transits to the Iranian northern route and the Omani southern route; Iran will continue pressing for recognition of its control over the strait and for mandatory ship tolls; and the risk of re-escalation tied to Iran's nuclear program, ballistic missile development, and the Israel-Hezbollah front remains acute.

Under de-escalation, oil prices are expected to fall into a $65–$80/barrel range for the remainder of 2026, down from the current $75–$95 range. Flows through the strait will recover, but transits will not return to pre-war levels in 2026. Speakers estimated that in a de-escalation scenario, transits recover to 30–50% of pre-war levels by year-end.

---

Crude Supply: China and the Global Balance

Chinese oil imports have fallen from 11 million barrels per day (mb/d) to as low as 5 mb/d. China has drawn on inventories estimated at 1.5–1.8 billion barrels — significantly above prior analyst estimates of 1.2–1.3 billion barrels — and likely retains enough stock to sustain reduced import levels through year-end without materially cutting consumption. A full recovery in Chinese imports to pre-war levels is not expected in 2026, partly because some older refineries have permanently shut. This demand restraint is a key factor keeping prices from spiking further.

Approximately 5 mb/d is currently transiting the strait via three channels: ships using the Iranian coastal route with transponders on, ships transiting dark, and a shuttle service transferring oil ship-to-ship in the Gulf of Oman. These flows are showing up in Gulf of Oman export data rather than SoH transit data.

---

Refined Products: Tighter Than Crude

Refined product markets are structurally tighter than crude. US gasoline prices have exceeded $4/gallon — described as the first time this has occurred this late in the year in nominal terms. US refineries are running at maximum throughput, with a significant share of output being exported. Diesel is also priced elevated relative to crude, driven by Russian refinery outages from Ukrainian strikes, Persian Gulf refinery disruptions, and Houthi attacks on Saudi facilities including the Jizan refinery. US gasoline and diesel inventories are at the low end of the historical range, with further draws expected through fall refinery maintenance season. Speakers do not expect outright shortages but anticipate product prices will remain elevated even as crude falls.

---

European Gas: Tight But Probably Manageable

Northwest European gas storage stands at approximately 50%, well below the EU's 80% target. Eurasia Group's base case projects storage reaching 65–70% by November 1 — below target but likely sufficient. The firm assigns 65% odds that Europe clears winter without severe shortages or emergency measures; the 35% risk scenario encompasses a range of emergency responses, not necessarily residential curtailments.

Key upside risks: a cold shock, additional SoH disruptions, and unplanned French nuclear outages. In a shortage scenario, gas prices could reach €65–€85/MWh; in a benign scenario, €30–€50/MWh, with the base case between those bands.

The EU's Russian liquefied natural gas (LNG) ban takes effect 1 January 2027. Speakers see near-zero probability of reversal. Russia's Yamal LNG is poorly positioned to redirect to Asia given Northern Sea Route seasonality. Turkey, Egypt, and South America are the most plausible alternative markets for displaced Russian volumes.

---

Yambu Disruption and Houthi Escalation

Houthi attacks have disrupted Saudi exports from the Yambu terminal on the Red Sea. Pre-conflict loadings ran at 4–4.5 mb/d; current data suggests a drop to as low as 2 mb/d, though dark tanker traffic makes precise measurement difficult. Speakers estimate the net disruption at 1–1.5 mb/d. At least one berth may have sustained damage. Even absent further escalation, the Houthi-Saudi conflict is expected to persist through year-end with its own independent drivers. Yambu loadings are expected to recover toward the 4–4.5 mb/d range over the next several weeks as shipping reroutes, barring further infrastructure damage.

---

Structural Shift: The Strait's Declining Centrality

Speakers emphasized that crises of this magnitude do not resolve back to the status quo. By 2028, Eurasia Group estimates roughly half of flows that previously transited the SoH will move through alternative routes. By 2030, the strait's share of global crude flows could fall below 50%. The UAE is reportedly planning to double Fujairah pipeline capacity; Saudi Arabia is discussing an East-West Pipeline 2; an Iraq-Syria-East Mediterranean pipeline corridor is also under consideration. Europe may cease relying on the SoH for crude entirely by 2030.

However, speakers noted countervailing risks: the Bab el-Mandeb's strategic importance will increase as alternative routes depend on Red Sea transits, strengthening the Houthis' leverage. Iran retains the ability to strike alternative pipeline infrastructure with missiles and drones. Electrification creates new dependencies on critical minerals and grid vulnerability.

Key Watch Items

  1. US-Iran interim deal by mid-September: Whether an arrangement materializes, its scope, and whether it includes any recognition of Iranian toll/fee demands in the SoH.
  2. SoH transit recovery: Whether transits recover toward 30–50% of pre-war levels or remain suppressed due to mines, re-escalation risk, or shipping confidence.
  3. Chinese import recovery: Any meaningful rebound in Chinese crude imports from current ~5 mb/d would tighten the supply-demand balance and push prices toward the higher end of the $65–$80 range or above it.
  4. Northwest European gas storage trajectory: Whether storage reaches the projected 65–70% by November 1; any shortfall materially raises the 35% shortage-scenario odds.
  5. Yambu infrastructure damage: Extent and duration of damage to the Yambu terminal; sustained disruption above 1.5 mb/d would tighten product markets further.
  6. Russian LNG ban implementation (January 1, 2027): Confirmation that the EU does not soften the ban and how displaced Yamal volumes are absorbed by Turkey and other non-EU markets.

What Clients Are Asking

Iran's toll and fee demands: Clients asked whether Iran's claims about charging fees for SoH passage have any legal or historical basis. Speakers said Iran's demand for mandatory per-ship tolls has been rejected by Oman, the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), and the US as contrary to international law; the Omani counterproposal models the voluntary, cost-covering fee structure used at the Strait of Malacca. Iran is expected to keep pushing, making this a persistent source of instability regardless of any interim deal.

European shortage risk — why 35%?: Clients questioned whether a 35% probability of shortages is too high for what seems like an extreme outcome. Speakers clarified that the 35% scenario covers a wide range of emergency responses — primarily industry demand reduction and government-mandated purchases — not residential curtailments, which are considered very unlikely given the multiple policy escalation steps available before that threshold is reached.

Venezuelan crude supply: Clients asked about the visibility of Venezuelan oil hitting the market following the post-Maduro political shift. Speakers said Venezuelan output recovery has broadly met expectations — a slight increase but no significant near-term spike — given geological constraints, infrastructure deficits, and the time lag on upstream investment.

Refined product price outlook given crude de-escalation: Clients asked whether the $65–$80 crude range assumed meaningful SoH flow recovery, and what happens if transits stay near current levels. Speakers confirmed the range does assume a flow recovery; re-escalation scenarios — driven by Iran's nuclear/missile posture or the Israel-Hezbollah front — could push prices above that band. The administration's desire to move on from the Iran confrontation is the primary factor anchoring the de-escalation base case.

Qatari LNG recovery timeline: Clients asked about the pace of Qatari LNG supply restoration. Speakers said at least one LNG train has likely sustained damage requiring a full rebuild; it is expected to be faster for Qatar to bring new planned production to market by early 2027 than to fully repair damaged trains. Once SoH shipping resumes, Qatar could return to near pre-crisis production levels using new capacity while repairing older trains.
