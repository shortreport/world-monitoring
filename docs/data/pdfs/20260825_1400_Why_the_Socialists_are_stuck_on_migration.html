DRIVING THE DAY

SOCIALIST STALEMATE: As Europe’s Socialists head back to work, the feud over migration between Spain’s Pedro Sánchez and Denmark’s Mette Frederiksen is looming large over the center-left.

Will the Danes be ostracized for taking a migration position that’s further right than many Socialists are comfortable with? Will the spat spill over into the European Parliament? Could it even threaten Iratxe García Pérez’s grip on the Socialists and Democrats group?

Or will the Socialist family instead be gripped by political paralysis?

The Ceuta crisis exposed a wider split on migration that goes to the core of the Socialists’ political values. On Aug. 1, a day after tens of thousands of migrants crossed into the Spanish exclave, Frederiksen joined other EU leaders in accusing Sánchez of undermining the security of the bloc’s borders. Last week, Spanish MEP Javi López and former Italian government minister Peppe Provenzano wrote to the Party of European Socialists (the Europe-wide umbrella group of center-left parties) attacking Frederiksen for aligning with right-wing Italian PM Giorgia Meloni rather than showing solidarity with a fellow Socialist government.

But nobody really wants the fight. According to nine S&D MEPs and officials who spoke to Playbook about the Ceuta crisis, the center-left is effectively frozen by competing national and European interests.

“We need to develop a strategy and stay united,” Vytenis Andriukaitis, an S&D MEP and member of the PES presidency, told Playbook. He argued that migration has become part of a “far-right paradigm” designed to deepen divisions within the socialist family and society more broadly.

“Our approaches on this topic in our Socialist family are a little bit different,” Andriukaitis acknowledged. Their priority, he said, should be to maintain a humane approach to migration without deepening divisions in their own ranks.

The row is likely to reach PES bigwigs before the October European Council, per another PES presidency member. In the European Parliament, the S&D’s leaders are expected to meet in the second or third week of September, two officials said.

Will they do anything? “This could well be one of those ‘we don’t talk about it’ issues,” one group official said.

“The debate in the PES has nothing to do with the group,” an MEP said, granted anonymity to discuss internal dynamics, as were others Playbook spoke to. Another MEP dismissed the letter from López and Provenzano as “the kind of thing people do in the heat of the moment.”

Two other thoughts popped up frequently in my chats:

1. Don’t mess with the Nordics: “No one wants a real fight with the Danes because then all the Scandinavians will go wild,” one socialist MEP told me. Frederiksen may annoy her Socialist Mediterranean cousins, but Nordic solidarity is still strong.

2. The Italians won’t storm the palace: One possible spillover scenario is that García — a close ally of Sánchez — faces a leadership challenge. But four group officials pointed out that Italy’s delegation is the only one with the numbers to take on García — and Democratic Party (PD) leader Elly Schlein has little incentive to grab the S&D wheel.

Schlein is trying to hold together an anti-Meloni opposition alliance at home with the 5Star Movement (affiliated with The Left) and Greens. Taking over the S&D could leave the PD clashing with its coalition partners every plenary. “We need to focus on elections,” as one Italian working with the Socialist group put it. “We don’t care about [group] leadership.”

The bottom line: Europe’s Socialists have plenty to fight about — but in Brussels, they have even more reasons not to.

BIG AND BREAKING

TAKK, DONALD: The Pro-EU camp in Iceland’s referendum has been boosted by Donald Trump’s Arctic saber-rattling, my colleague Carlo Martuscelli reports from Reykjavík ahead of Saturday’s vote.

CLIMATE BILL COMING DUE: After a summer of fires, drought and brutal heat, EU governments need to spend on climate-proofing now or they’ll pay considerably more later, the Commission’s climate chief Wopke Hoekstra tells Zia Weise in an exclusive interview.

THE 400-YEAR-OLD BIG TECH SHIELD: An obscure 17th-century Irish law is making it harder for Europeans to collectively seek damages from tech giants accused of breaking European rules.

KYIV SCRAMBLES FOR CASH: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy appealed to European allies at yesterday’s coalition of the willing meeting for help plugging a €23 billion hole in his country’s defense budget.

DRAGHI ASSEMBLES HIS AVENGERS: Mario Draghi is setting up the Rhine Group with Stripe founder Patrick Collison to bring together policymakers, economists and tech heavyweights to push the former ECB chief’s competitiveness agenda. It’ll be run by economist Luis Garicano. But Poland’s minister for EU funds Jan Szyszko blasted the lineup for including just one representative from the 13 countries that joined the EU after 2004 — former Estonian President Toomas Hendrik Ilves.

BUDGET BASH

LEADERS ON TOUR: European Council President António Costa and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz are embarking on a week of shuttle diplomacy as the EU seeks a deal on its next seven-year budget before the end of the year, Gregorio Sorgi and Zoya Sheftalovich write in.

Ships in the night: Costa kickstarts a tour of the EU’s 27 capitals today in Bratislava, seeking to pave the way for a budget deal in December ― before a crucial election year that will see France, Italy, Spain and Poland head to the polls. Merz will gather frugal Northern countries in Berlin on Thursday.

Slovakia first? Robert Fico is the EU’s current problem child, so Costa’s early visit could be read as an effort to sweeten him up. But a senior EU official on Monday insisted in response to a question from POLITICO that the schedule reflects the need to group countries geographically while working around leaders’ availability. (Costa was in Kyiv on Monday for the coalition of the willing meeting.)

Berlin has taken an aggressive stance in the budget talks, demanding cuts of “several hundred billion” euros from the Commission proposal, which is worth almost €2 trillion. To build a common front, Merz has invited like-minded allies including the Netherlands, Finland, Austria and Denmark to his Thursday summit.

Pas vous, Manu: French President Emmanuel Macron wasn’t invited — despite Merz vowing just over a month ago, after hosting him in Berlin, that Paris and Berlin would work together on this issue and others.

Costa-to-Costa: The Berlin summit coincides with Costa’s tour of the capitals, which will take him to Slovakia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Czechia this week. Costa intends to visit every EU country except Sweden, which holds an election in September, to better understand where each EU leader stands on the budget and other priorities such as migration and competitiveness.

Grand finale: Costa will head to Ireland toward the end of his tour, to brief PM Micheál Martin on his takeaways. The rotating Irish Council presidency must produce a new budget negotiating document before an October leaders’ summit.

TAKING AIM AT ROMANIA

EXCLUSIVE — COMMISSION URGED TO WITHHOLD FUNDS: The center-right European People’s Party and liberal Renew Europe groups are asking Commission President Ursula von der Leyen to withhold €770 million of EU money from Romania due to rule of law concerns, according to a letter seen by Max Griera.

Big guns: EPP chief Manfred Weber and Renew leader Valérie Hayer asked von der Leyen “to make it unequivocally clear” that the money — part of the EU’s post-pandemic recovery cash pot — won’t be released unless Bucharest drops plans to amend a law that could lead to the head of a liberal party being barred from office.

In a nutshell: The Social Democrats (PSD), with far-right party AUR’s support, introduced an amendment to a law on integrity in office that could see USR party leader and Timișoara Mayor Dominic Fritz barred over a past conflict of interest case. Weber and Hayer say its retroactive application breaches EU law and Romania’s constitution — and accuse PSD of deliberately targeting Fritz.

“European taxpayers’ money must never become a blank check for political power plays,” Hayer told Playbook. Weber added that “if legislation is tailored to remove a political opponent, and EU funds are used as leverage to push it through, this is no longer just politics — it is a rule-of-law concern.”

Hoping for a sign: In the letter, they urge the Commission to send “a signal” before Romanian President Nicușor Dan signs off the law, and before the Aug. 31 deadline for reforms that would release the EU cash.

What this is really about: There’s been a month-long deadlock since PSD torpedoed a national government coalition with the help of the far right. PSD did not reply to a request for comment.
