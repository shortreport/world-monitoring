Disappointing COP31 confirms shift to smaller coalitions
24 Nov 2026 16:08 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 
 
  Listen now · 6 min
  • The 9-20 November UN climate summit (COP31) ended without parties adopting its draft political text, the "Antalya Pact," as divisions on fossil fuel language and discordant leadership exposed the obstacles to consensus-driven climate diplomacy.
  • The failure has limited practical consequences, as existing climate commitments remain intact, several notable decisions were adopted outside the pact, and energy security concerns, market forces, and smaller coalitions will continue driving the energy transition.
  • The outcome confirms our view that climate action is moving from multilateralism to minilateralism—action taken through smaller coalitions and bilateral deals—though this shift risks excluding major emitters and weakening the UN's credibility.

UN debates are regressing from the pace of phasing out fossil fuels to whether they should even be mentioned. Despite parties agreeing to "transition away from fossil fuels" at COP28, the draft Antalya Pact did not explicitly mention it, instead proposing to accelerate deployment of carbon capture. This was a concession to fossil fuel producers, led by Saudi Arabia and Russia. China and India were prepared to accept the text, but the EU, the UK, Norway, Pacific island states, and several Latin American countries refused anything silent on fossil fuels. Both sides refused to budge; the Iran war's disruption of Hormuz shipping elevated interest in electrification while increasing sensitivity to further pressure on oil exports.

The split presidency arrangement and text-heavy negotiations hindered progress. Under the compromise reached after more than a year of competing claims to host COP31, Turkey hosted and its Minister of Environment, Murat Kurum, held the presidency, while Australia's Minister for Climate Change and Energy, Chris Bowen, became president of negotiations with exclusive authority over the talks.

The mandate was ambiguous from the outset. Bowen claimed the full powers of a COP president, including drafting the political text, while Ankara said that the final decision rested with its presidency. The two ministers co-authored the final draft, so neither could serve as a neutral broker once it was rejected. At the close, Bowen cited "unbridgeable red lines" and Kurum declined to introduce new text. The last-minute brokering that a unified presidency normally provides was not possible.

The hosts promised a "COP of implementation" but ran a conventional, text-heavy negotiation that complicated progress. Turkey built its presidency around a voluntary Action Agenda. Australia, however, pursued a broad and ambitious political text. The two tracks collided. Folding voluntary goals such as the 35x35 electrification target into the negotiated draft exposed them to the consensus rule and pulled attention from mandated items and the technical tracks where implementation happens. Whether future presidencies focus on advancing deliverables through coalitions and national plans, or fold them into political texts, will shape progress on implementation.

Whether minilateralism can catalyze climate action remains to be seen. The 35x35 pledge does not require new electricity to be low-carbon, and the Santa Marta process on transitioning away from fossil fuels has yet to bring in the largest producers, such as Saudi Arabia, Russia, and the US. Near-term tests of the minilateral model will include the UN Water Conference (December), the High Seas Treaty COP (January 2027), and COP32.

The practical impact of COP31's failure to adopt its political text is very limited. While COP31 is the first summit since Copenhagen in 2009 to end without an overarching political outcome, this comparison inflates Antalya's importance. Copenhagen collapsed under high expectations, as heads of state gathered to decide on a successor to the Kyoto Protocol. Conversely, few expected a breakthrough at COP31, with the US absent, energy insecurity, and a split presidency. Moreover, unlike Copenhagen, which failed in its core mandated task, the Antalya Pact was an add-on that Australia staked its reputation on, making the drama over its rejection largely self-inflicted.

A missing declaration does not undo the commitments in the Paris Agreement, the COP28 global stocktake decision, or the COP30 call to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035. In addition, parties adopted decisions on the Just Transition Work Programme, the Adaptation Fund, measuring progress on adaptation, the Climate Finance Work Programme, and carbon markets, even if several were largely procedural and pushed key questions to 2027. Markets and energy security concerns will also continue driving the transition. The oil price shock from the Iran war, together with renewables growth, is set to cut global fossil fuel emissions by 0.4% this year, the first decrease since the pandemic.

The larger cost is reputational, as perceived failed summits erode the UN's credibility. These failures strengthen the hand of countries preferring to act unilaterally or minilaterally, and weaken least developed countries, which rely on the UN process for leverage over concessional finance.

Adapting COPs to a GZERO world will be a prolonged and uncertain process. With no country or bloc willing or able to lead, any party can block an outcome because COPs rely on consensus-based decision-making. This risk is heightened by the new geopolitical environment in which countries are increasingly favoring pragmatic decision-making over values-based ideals. The EU has said it will propose changes to the consensus model in favor of majority voting. However, changing voting rules will require broad agreement, which will be challenging.
