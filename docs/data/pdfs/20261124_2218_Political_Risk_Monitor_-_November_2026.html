AI use is most widespread in advanced economies with modest growth prospects. The UAE, Singapore, and Ireland lead in adoption, but they rank among the slowest-growing economies in 2026, while fast-growing markets like Ethiopia, India, and Vietnam remain below the 15.6% median. AI adoption is rising fastest where the technology has the greatest scope to boost slow growth. In the US, AI-related investment has supported GDP growth, but the buildout faces headwinds on multiple fronts. Data centers were a prominent issue in the midterm elections, and several incumbents who supported them lost. Inflation, deficits, and price pressures will also keep upward pressure on Treasury yields (see: Inflation and deficits will keep upward pressure on Treasury yields, 19 November 2026), raising the cost of continued investment. Beyond adoption, policymakers must also weigh domestic spillovers and global sourcing, because AI networks shape countries’ exposure to external shocks.

Trends in global political stability

In October, Eurasia Group’s Global Risk Index (GPRI) declined further, with a sharper drop year-on-year than month-on-month. Security was the main drag, reflecting continued disruption in the Middle East. Society scores and, to a lesser extent, economic scores also fell year-on-year, though both have been edging up month-on-month. Government scores improved broadly as election-related uncertainty eased in many countries we cover.

Trajectory updates

These maps present Eurasia Group’s updated short-term (six-month) and long-term (24-month) political trajectories. In October (since the last report) and November:

  • Brazil: The short-term trajectory shifted from positive to neutral after President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s surprise election victory. Lula will make constructive gestures toward fiscal reform, but he is unlikely to follow through. As his support erodes, he will pivot to taxing the wealthy and the private sector to fund middle-class benefits, which is why the long-term trajectory is closer to negative territory than the short-term outlook (see: Surprise Lula victory sets up a difficult post-election outlook, 25 October 2026).
  • Chile: The short-term and long-term trajectories were downgraded from positive to neutral as President Jose Antonio Kast’s political capital has weakened and policy upside is limited. With approval ratings at new lows and limited leverage in congress, the administration will be confined to incremental measures on employment, investment, and security (see: Downgrading trajectories to neutral on weaker Kast and limited policy upside, 27 October 2026).

EG geopolitical trends

  • After months in improving territory, the purchasing power parity (PPP)-GDP-weighted average of Eurasia Group’s political trajectories fell back below zero in October, returning to declining territory for the first time since late 2025. The reversal reflects the erosion of buffers that have absorbed geopolitical shocks, including spillovers from the Iran conflict. Even so, our basecase remains resilient growth through 2027, supported by stable trade flows and steady demand. However, persistently high long-term rates leave less room for error if political conditions deteriorate further.
  • Volatility in political stability among the 89 countries tracked by Eurasia Group’s GPRI fell further in October, declining to 12.8 from 13.8 a year earlier. The trend captures a period of notable resilience in global political conditions, but that stability appears increasingly fragile.

Notable year-on-year movements

  • China’s political capacity has weakened as deeper centralization around President Xi Jinping, who is almost certain to secure a fourth term, has created decision-making bottlenecks that limit the government’s flexibility. Macroeconomic vulnerability has also deteriorated, as weak domestic consumption and a widening gap between producer and consumer price inflation points to deeper structural fragilities despite strong export growth.
  • Egypt’s political capacity and macroeconomic vulnerability have both worsened. Cairo’s deeper involvement in regional conflicts is straining the government’s capacity to address the country’s structural problems. Regional turmoil is also raising food and energy costs and threatening Gulf remittance and investment flows. The end of the IMF program this year will add to liquidity pressures.
  • Ghana’s political capacity has weakened as the administration shifts from fiscal consolidation to pre-election infrastructure spending, risking the credibility President John Mahama’s government built through a sharp reduction in the deficit. Macroeconomic vulnerability has remained stable, as gains from fiscal consolidation are offset by heavy reliance on gold exports.
