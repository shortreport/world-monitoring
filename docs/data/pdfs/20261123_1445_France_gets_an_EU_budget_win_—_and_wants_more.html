DRIVING THE DAY

COSTA HANDS FRANCE A WIN: Paris is emerging as the main beneficiary from the latest round of talks on the EU’s long-term budget, according to four EU diplomats who spoke to Playbook over the weekend. The proposal from European Council President António Costa secures subsidies for agriculture and puts forward a digital tax — both policies Paris supports.

Hopes and dreams: In a hint that a deal on the seven-year Multiannual Financial Framework may be within reach, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz cautiously welcomed the proposal — which cuts the top-line number by €44 billion compared with last month’s proposal — as a “first step” toward a wider agreement, Gregorio Sorgi reports. EU ambassadors delve into the fine print during a Coreper gathering today, ahead of the leaders’ summit on Thursday.

But but but: There’s a lot of grumbling behind the scenes — not least from the French themselves. The Elysée Palace called the proposal “deeply disappointing” and said it “doesn’t provide a basis for an agreement,” partly because it doesn’t sufficiently guarantee farmers’ incomes.

That’s rich, say the so-called frugal states that want a smaller budget. “French criticism of [Costa’s] efforts to arrive at an affordable budget is particularly surprising,” said a diplomat from a net-payer country. “Has Paris decided yet who should foot the bill for France’s contribution to the EU budget — the ESM [European Stability Mechanism] or the ECB [European Central Bank]?”

What that means: France is in no position to dictate terms, given the state of its public finances. Ouch.

Another battleground to watch is the digital tax, which repurposes a French-led proposal from 2018 to impose levies on tech giants such as Google and Meta. Back then the initiative failed amid opposition from the U.S. and tech-friendly countries like Ireland.

There’s little to suggest that dynamic has changed: Ireland’s Prime Minister Micheál Martin has spoken out against the tax, while Sweden’s Europe Minister Jessica Rosencrantz said last week that any new EU-wide tax “won’t solve the financial equation.” The proposal risks “opening Pandora’s box” because of the potential reaction from U.S. President Donald Trump, a second EU diplomat said.

The tech industry is up in arms. “The digital levy must be kept out of any MFF deal,” said Daniel Friedlaender, head of office for the CCIA tech lobby in Brussels. A digital tax “tells companies to look elsewhere.”

Then there’s Ukraine: Costa’s proposal ring-fences €100 billion for Ukraine, stating that interest payments on current and future loans for Kyiv won’t come from this envelope. But that leaves open the question of how the interest will be repaid, including a possible extra vehicle just for Ukraine, an EU official said.

The bottom line: Expect the gloves to come off in the budget negotiations over the next few weeks. “Now is when we really start slugging,” said a third EU diplomat. Whether Costa is able to broker a deal before year-end will be seen as the defining test of his term in Brussels.

THE FAR RIGHT’S BIG PLAN

PATRIOTS’ PATH TO POWER: The far-right Patriots for Europe group is focused on winning national elections before attempting to reshape Brussels in 2029, Co-President Kinga Gál told Max Griera.

Pulling the levers: Gál, a close ally of former Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán, has led the group alongside French National Rally leader Jordan Bardella since October, after serving as first vice president since 2024. While Bardella draws the attention, Gál does the legwork.

First, the capitals: Gál said the priority is helping member parties win elections to increase their number of EU leaders. She hopes that Vox enters government in Spain this month, Marine Le Pen wins in France and Poland’s Law and Justice returns to power.

After that, “we have to see” how to strengthen ties between the right-wing forces in the European Parliament ahead of the 2029 EU election, Gál said. The ideal would be “to grow into a huge group.” The populist right bloc is currently split into three groups: the Patriots, Giorgia Meloni’s European Conservatives and Reformists and the AfD-dominated Europe of Sovereign Nations.

Taking back power: Gál didn’t dismiss AfD boss Alice Weidel’s proposal to replace directly elected MEPs with delegates appointed by national parliaments. “These ideas, they all have their own validity,” Gál said. “It’s important to see how we conceive the future of Europe.”

Summit school: The far right is in training for power in Brussels, too. Before EU summits, Czech PM Andrej Babiš briefs fellow Patriots on the agenda and battle lines. They then close ranks to show “that we are behind him on many of the important issues.” On the EU budget, they agree on protecting funding for farmers and regions while opposing EU “own resources” and “sending more money to Ukraine.”

Will they seek to bring down Commission President Ursula von der Leyen if they were to gain influence in the Council? “Yes. We tried to do it here in the Parliament,” Gál said, referring to two failed no-confidence motions.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

While talks on the next EU budget continue, five countries in the current period get a rebate … but what is that? A rebate — officially a budgetary correction mechanism — is a way to compensate certain member countries for their financial contributions. It was decided in 1984 that the U.K. would get some of its budget contribution back. In the current budget period (2021-2027), Austria, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden get rebates. It’s too early to know if there will be rebates in the 2028-2034 budget.

EUROPE’S DEPORTATION DILEMMA

RWANDA PLAN WILL FUEL VIOLENCE, CONGO SAYS: Plans by Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands to set up a deportation hub in Rwanda risk worsening one of Africa’s largest forced displacements, the Democratic Republic of the Congo’s Foreign Affairs Minister Thérèse Kayikwamba Wagner told Hanne Cokelaere.

Rwanda has been accused of supporting the M23 rebel group, which controls large swathes of the North and South Kivu provinces in Eastern Congo, where fighting between the Congolese military and multiple rebel groups has forced millions of people from their homes. The U.S., U.N. and EU have called on Rwanda and its army to halt support for M23 and to withdraw troops from Congo, though Kigali has repeatedly denied helping the rebel group.

Congo fears that money for the deportation hub could be redirected to finance more violence.

“It is puzzling to see that a country that is at the heart, not just of instability, not just of conflict, but also of displacement and forced migration, is perceived to be a safe haven and … a credible partner to uphold international law,” Wagner said. She called on the EU countries to “go back to their notes” and rethink their choice of partner.

The EU is already “quite timid” on potential sanctions on Rwanda, according to Wagner. “I think it’s going to be even more difficult now,” she added.

FAREWELL TO A BRUSSELS LEGEND: Tijn Sadée, the longtime European correspondent for the Dutch public broadcaster NOS, has died, aged 62.

“Tijn was an incredibly experienced, funny, and simply lovely colleague,” my Berlin Playbook colleague Hans von der Burchard recalls. “I’ll never forget how, during the last EU budget negotiations in July 2020, we were waiting together for Angela Merkel and Mark Rutte on a late Saturday night outside the EU Council building (back then because of Covid-19) and passed the time joking around.
