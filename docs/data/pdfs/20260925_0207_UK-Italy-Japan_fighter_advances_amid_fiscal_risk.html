UK-Italy-Japan fighter system will advance amid fiscal risks
24 Sep 2026 13:04 EDT
Discover more on the Portal
  • The UK’s Defense Investment Plan (DIP) now locks in £8.6 billion over four years for the country’s joint Global Combat Air Programme (GCAP) with Japan and Italy, backed by major contracts and growing allied interest, with political and financial costs making reversal unlikely but not impossible.
  • GCAP is emerging as the only viable export-oriented sixth-generation fighter program for partners excluded from US and Chinese systems or seeking to diversify away from Washington, but it is not a substitute for Europe’s failed Future Combat Air System (FCAS), especially for Germany.
  • Even as Canada and India consider further involvement, the program’s long-term trajectory still depends on a politically fragile, GDP-linked UK defense spending path and a deteriorating fiscal position that will be stress-tested by London’s Autumn Budget.

Stronger UK financial commitment moves GCAP from concept to implementation. After months of Treasury–Defense Ministry conflict that caused serious strain within former prime minister Keir Starmer’s government, the DIP was finally announced on 30 June, setting an £8.6 billion path for GCAP over four years. It has since moved from concept to committed program, with Edgewing receiving a contract for advanced concept and assessment and BAE Systems signing a £708 million extension to its contract for sovereign UK combat air technologies.

The UK has now invested £5 billion in GCAP trilateral project structures and active industrial baselines, with another £8.6 billion planned. This level of financial commitment makes cancellation materially more costly—in cash, credibility, and alliance terms—than prior to the DIP announcement, but still conceivable if the UK government remains under sustained fiscal stress. Italy has locked in €8.8 billion for GCAP through 2037, and Japan embedded its contribution within a five-year defense buildup program, leaving the UK—as program lead and the last to confirm its funding—as the primary point of vulnerability.

GCAP is the only export-oriented sixth-generation fighter in development. China is flying prototypes, and the US is pushing its Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) program, but neither is open to industrial partners. And earlier this year, the joint France-Germany-Spain FCAS fighter element collapsed over Airbus–Dassault leadership disputes (see: Franco-German divisions likely to end FCAS fighter, not full program), leaving Germany and Spain searching for alternatives.

Still, GCAP is not a viable substitute for the failed European FCAS program. FCAS was conceived as a “system of systems”; GCAP’s architecture and work-share are built around a specific fighter concept designed to meet the requirements of the UK, Italy, and Japan. The UK and Italy prioritize NATO interoperability and expeditionary operations (with Italy highly cost‑sensitive); Japan focuses on range, payload, and stealth for Pacific maritime competition with China.

Germany’s stated openness to joining GCAP comes with conditions—in particular, significant work‑share and leadership stipulations—which risk reproducing FCAS‑style tensions. Eighteen months after FCAS collapsed, German officials have yet to offer any strategic direction on a successor. In that vacuum, a consortium of eight German companies is already developing an "anchor program" open to external partners, further crowding the replacement field along with GCAP and a potential French-led FCAS successor.

German participation would require a complete renegotiation of the GCAP agreement. Supply chains have already been distributed, and there are substantive military specification mismatches: Germany does not share the UK's and Japan's longer-range requirements. German export policy constraints would also complicate GCAP’s exportability and partner mix. Leonardo CEO Lorenzo Mariani’s confirmation that new partners must join by the end of 2026 (a hard stop by mid-2027, before the architecture locks) sharpens Berlin’s timeline.

Other potential partners include Canada and India. Canada has taken an observer status to diversify away from US dependence, and India has joined as a lower‑access “Dialogue Partner,” while simultaneously pursuing France’s own fighter program.

The UK fiscal outlook remains GCAP’s most significant vulnerability. The UK’s macro backdrop has worsened: Its £23.6 billion fiscal headroom has fallen by about £10 billion since March, hit by the US–Iran conflict and Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s initial £2 billion in spending pledges. Former defense secretary and current Chancellor John Healey must now find £4.7 billion to fund the DIP’s first year in the Autumn Budget he is set to present on 28 October. He is likely to present the additional spending as a low‑key stability exercise rather than a defense surge (see: Budget will bring Burnham's big promises up against fiscal reality, 2 September 2026).

The DIP was not sufficient to meet NATO’s 3% of GDP defense target or the ambitious 3.5% by 2035 path; both will be deferred to a spring spending review. This will include a timetable for reaching 3%. Eurasia Group’s 35% probability of early UK elections in 2027 means a renewed LabouNr mandate could reframe DIP and GCAP again on Burnham’s terms. Although GCAP is more politically and financially anchored than it was six months ago, its future still depends on an increasingly rocky UK fiscal path and political consensus that has yet to solidify.
