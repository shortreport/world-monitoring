DRIVING THE DAY

UKRAINE’S ALLIES TO SPAR OVER FUNDING AND AIR DEFENSE: Ukraine’s Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha is set to press allies in Ireland this week to deliver urgently needed air defenses and fill a funding gap that has led to a $27 billion shortfall at Kyiv’s defense ministry.

The call will prompt the allies — the 27 EU members plus Norway, Canada and the U.K. — to thrash out how best to meet Kyiv’s needs as Russia shifts its tactics to near-continuous bombardment.

Emergency, again: The near-daily drone and missile attacks have put Ukraine at the top of the EU’s back-to-school in-tray. The funding problem has become unavoidable after Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskyy raised the alarm about the cash needed to keep his defense ministry ticking over.

Finger-pointing: Greece and Spain, which lag other allies in the numbers of interceptors offered, are set to be at the sharp end of calls to boost air defenses. Spanish PM Pedro Sánchez vowed in March to give Ukraine five Patriot interceptors but Greece has given none. PM Kyriakos Mitsotakis has even boasted that Athens (which has just inked a €3 billion air defense deal with Israel) has “resisted” pressure to deliver Patriots.

Gearing up: Foreign and defense ministers “will definitely speak up about this … It’s not fair for some countries to bear most of the burden,” said an EU diplomat who was granted anonymity to talk about the delicate negotiations.

In addition to the defense ministry issue, Ukraine faces a €45 billion budget shortfall next year, which was meant to be met by non-EU Ukraine allies when European leaders agreed on a €90 billion loan. Despite strong financial aid from Norway and the U.K., contributions from other G7 Ukraine backers including Canada and Japan, along with the IMF, aren’t yet filling the final third.

Up-front money: Zelenskyy is calling to bring forward part of the EU loan, but the response from EU countries has been muted. Some want to reopen discussions on the use of Russia’s frozen assets, in what one diplomat said was a signal to the Commission to come up with new proposals to address Belgium’s legal concerns. But even those wanting to revive that debate agree it’s a tough sell.

Pressed on bringing forward the loan, diplomats from three so-called frugal countries left the door open. “We are a frugal country when it comes to the MFF, but for support to Ukraine we are completely generous and flexible,” said one of them.

ALSO IN FOCUS: EEAS reform, to be discussed Wednesday during a special “Gymnich” session of the bloc’s foreign affairs ministers. Expect proposals from top diplomat Kaja Kallas on how to revamp the foreign policy branch — as well as grumbling from countries saying this isn’t the time to change the way the EU works.

The bottom line: Ukraine is back to being a flashing red emergency for the EU and other allies during this rentrée.

EU TRADE CHIEF’S MESSAGE TO CHINA

OCTOBER DEADLINE FOR CHINA: The EU is exploring ways to persuade Beijing to rein in its exports to the bloc as the European Commission comes under pressure to reduce its €1 billion-a-day trade deficit with China — and to show EU leaders it can deliver results.

Adjusting the balance: “Mechanically … what’s needed is the increase of European products to China … We also need to look at how to manage better the Chinese exports to the European Union,” EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič told my colleague Camille Gijs Monday.

Why it matters: National leaders want the Commission to show results from its engagement with Beijing by mid-October. Two months ago, EU members urged Brussels to come up with new tools to reduce the trade deficit, as manufacturers of chemicals and cars closed plants and cut jobs, under pressure from cheaper Chinese imports.

Tick tock: Šefčovič will speak with his Chinese counterpart Wang Wentao by videoconference in mid-September before visiting Beijing in early October, he said. EU negotiators are in China this week for talks that are “very open, very tough,” the commissioner said, with “all topics … on the table.”

Changing tack: Brussels is edging toward a more interventionist China policy — and taking a page from Washington’s book. Rather than relying primarily on traditional trade defenses once Chinese goods reach Europe, the approach increasingly focuses on a broader management of trade flows between the two sides.

“We are working with very concrete proposals. Let’s see how our Chinese counterparts would react to them,” Šefčovič said. If there’s no action in response to the EU’s concerns, then the bloc would look to make full use of its trade defenses and shut off its markets, he said.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Today and tomorrow, Wicklow, in Ireland, will host a Gymnich. But what does that mean? A Gymnich is an informal meeting of EU foreign affairs ministers (informal in an EU context means a less rigid agenda, with no formal decisions made or votes taken). There are two such meetings held each year, hosted by the country holding the rotating presidency of the Council of the EU and chaired by Kallas. The name comes from Schloss Gymnich, a castle in Germany where the first such meeting was held, in 1974.

THINK TANK TROUBLE

BATTLE FOR THE BRUSSELS BUBBLE: The team behind MCC Brussels won’t be silenced. The EU’s most talked-about right-wing think tank, which lost its funding from Budapest following Viktor Orbán’s defeat, may be shuttered, but the group’s director is already planning his next move.

Watch this space: Frank Füredi quit as MCC director yesterday, telling Playbook the move was the first step in “the process of mobilizing support [for] eventually launching a new initiative” with alternative funding.

What happened: The group, which set out to challenge “mainstream” thinking on climate, migration and gender issues, was almost entirely reliant on a multimillion-euro grant from its Hungarian sister organization. That dried up when Prime Minister Péter Magyar took over in May.

Gracious hosts: MCC Brussels was the organizer of events on populism, transgender rights, and “the future of the West,” and also a “Freedom Ball” for the city’s right-wing movers and shakers, complete with a symphony orchestra and champagne reception. But it had its membership of the EU’s Transparency Register suspended in a dispute over registration and it has been accused by Magyar of being part of Orbán’s network of influence.

Daddy Trump? The U.S. government has publicly said it wants to support MAGA-friendly partners in Europe — Reuters reported overnight that the Trump administration had earmarked $4 million to bolster right-wing media. It’s this kind of support that Füredi’s new initiative could tap into. “I’m happy to get money from anywhere, Americans included,” he said, while adding that “we don’t want strings attached.”

“And that’s the problem, I think potentially, with the Americans … they are lovely people but whether they are going to simply allow us to work in the way that we’ve been accustomed to [remains] to be seen,” he said.
