DRIVING THE DAY

MONTREAL DISPATCH: At today’s EU–Canada summit, Brussels and Ottawa are expected to announce a deal to make it easier for businesses to trade online — the first concrete result of their push to bring their economies closer.

“This was one of the most intense negotiations,” EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič told Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich on the sidelines of the summit in Montreal yesterday, describing the talks that began this spring to make trade easier for businesses.

Keeping in step: The digital deal is part of a broader effort to align trade policies. Canada is concerned it could be excluded from the EU’s “Made in Europe” preference for public procurement, while Ottawa is developing its own Buy Canadian procurement framework. The EU is working with Canada to ensure neither scheme harms the other, Šefčovič said.

The wider push goes beyond trade: Officials have been exploring cooperation on critical minerals, education, skilled workers and Arctic security — while trying to avoid stoking the ire of Washington and Beijing.

The name game: The summit is meant to mark a closer partnership, but the label for that relationship has become a sticking point. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen restated her proposal to make Canada the EU’s first “associate member” again yesterday, which an EU official said she is expected to repeat today. But Germany is pushing back against the term, which it says is wrong for a country that isn’t seeking EU membership.

Berlin previously floated an “associate membership” of the EU for Ukraine, but President Volodymyr Zelenskyy rejected that as a “half-measure.”

Berlin has instead opted to use the phrase “Alliance for the Future” to describe the EU–Canada relationship. But associate membership and the proposed Alliance for the Future are separate ideas, a senior EU official said. Associate membership would be a bilateral arrangement bringing Canada closer to EU “policies, programs and governance,” where the alliance would be a broader “common space for economic prosperity” with trusted partners including Japan, South Korea and Australia.

What’s in a name? “This new alliance is not a marketing exercise. The name doesn’t matter,” Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said yesterday when asked about the disagreements over the term.

Next up: With areas of deeper cooperation expected to be laid out in more detail today, the full College of Commissioners and the Canadian government are due to meet in six months to do take stock of their progress, ahead of the next EU-Canada summit in Brussels.

Heated rivalry: The diplomacy is in full swing, with von der Leyen taking a walking meeting with her right-hand man Björn Seibert, chief diplomatic adviser Simon Mordue and other aides yesterday before tucking into a St-Viateur bagel — picking a side in Montreal’s long-running rivalry with Fairmount. She was also given a Canadiens jersey bearing the name of Slovak forward Juraj Slafkovský. Watch the bagel diplomacy on Instagram.

SOUNDING THE DEATH KNELL

DON’T BURY US: European coffin makers fear they will be left lifeless, becoming the next casualty of Europe’s industrial decline as cheap Chinese imports threaten one of the continent’s oldest sectors.

Knocking on DG TRADE’s door: Coffin manufacturers are forming a coalition and have met Commission officials, including those from its trade department last week, the initiative’s organizers told Playbook. The move comes after MEPs raised the alarm with Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič last year, who invited the industry to file a complaint.

“The EU takes robust action to protect its industries from unfairly traded imports,” a Commission spokesperson told POLITICO. But about the discussions with coffin makers, mum’s the word — talks over potential trade defense complaints are confidential, the EU executive says.

A grave situation: Cheap imports have helped bury a quarter of Europe’s coffin makers over the past five years, with their ranks shrinking from 120 to 90 companies, according to Folco Ciulli, a consultant preparing the trade complaint. Smaller firms couldn’t compete, he said, while survivors have picked up only half of their lost production.

But cheap imports alone don’t prove dumping. Selling a product in Europe below its normal value, usually its price on the Chinese market, is where the definition could be triggered. The manufacturers must also show that the imports are harming European producers. The coalition, which has yet to choose a name, is still gathering evidence — a process that could take another six months.

The mystery of the missing coffins: Mathematician Emiliano Inguscio, hired by the industry to organize the data, said he found an unexplained gap in expected supply and demand for coffins in Italy last year, with around 30,000 fewer coffins being supplied to the market than anticipated. And demand is predictable, he explained, as it’s closely tied to … mortality.

Assemble your own Likkista: The discrepancy could be because some Chinese coffins evade proper identification by customs, the manufacturers say. Their suspicion is that some coffins arrive flat-packed, IKEA-style, and declared as wooden components, or that some are imported as packaging for other goods.

From EVs to RIP: The case shows how Europe’s trade tensions with China are spreading beyond cars, steel and chemicals. In January, Brussels slapped duties of 56% to 60% on Chinese candles. Between candles and coffins, the EU is one trade dispute away from regulating the your entire funeral.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Belgian Deputy Prime Minister Frank Vandenbroucke said the European Union should use its Anti-Coercion Instrument against the U.S. over Washington’s push to raise EU drug prices … But what is the ACI? Sometimes called the “trade bazooka,” it allows Brussels to impose or increase customs duties, restrict exports or imports through quotas or licenses and impose restrictions on trade in services. It can also curb access to public procurement, foreign direct investment, intellectual property rights and access to the bloc’s financial markets. But it’s never been used.

BRUSSELS IS CHILL ON SPANISH POLL

NO BUDGET PAIN IN SPAIN: The outcome of the Spanish election next month is unlikely to undermine the EU’s plan to conclude negotiations on its next seven-year budget by the end of December, five EU diplomats and officials told Gregorio Sorgi.

Tranquilo: “The Spanish elections will not affect the approval of the Multiannual Financial Framework,” an official from the center-right opposition Partido Popular, which is leading in the polls, told Playbook’s Madrid colleagues. “The PP is following the negotiations closely and remains in contact with its fellow [European People’s Party] members across Europe to understand what lies ahead for the MFF.”

Meanwhile, Spanish PM Pedro Sánchez has signaled to fellow leaders that he will handle the budget negotiations until the end of the year, regardless of the election result, the officials told us. António Costa is aiming to strike a deal during a summit that starts on Dec. 17 — days before the new Spanish parliament convenes for its first session on Dec. 23.

Even if Sánchez loses the election, there’s nothing stopping him from approving the budget in December as caretaker leader. Sweden and Latvia are currently represented in the European Council by interim prime ministers.

Sánchez and PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo are at odds on most subjects, but they share similar priorities on the EU budget. “The budget should prioritize the Common Agricultural Policy, cohesion funds and adapting to today’s challenges,” the PP official added, broadly echoing the current government’s position.

Nada cambia: “After all the political dispute, things will settle and the Spanish interests in agriculture and cohesion are not going to change,” said an EU diplomat with knowledge of the discussions.
