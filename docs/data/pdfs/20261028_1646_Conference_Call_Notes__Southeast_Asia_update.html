Indonesia: MSCI response and reform credibility

Indonesia's authorities have moved beyond general reassurances in response to the MSCI threat of a double downgrade to standalone status—a development that caught markets and officials off guard. The government's response is organized around four tracks: ownership transparency, free float reform, index composition, and market enforcement.

Specific measures include: new Financial Services Authority (OJK) disclosure requirements for shareholders holding more than 1%; a framework to identify stocks with highly concentrated shareholdings; a phased-in 15% minimum free float requirement; and the exclusion of 80 stocks from the Indonesia Stock Exchange (IDX) Jakarta Composite Index effective 2 November. The IDX is also expanding its oversight committee to include independent experts, academics, and foreign investor representatives. On enforcement, OJK emphasized it had handed out approximately 2.1 trillion rupiah ($116 million) in administrative fines across several cases, including DSSA.

The immediate watchpoint is implementation quality. The next MSCI review benchmarks are concentrated around November. MSCI has focused on the actual impact of reforms, not just their announcement.

Prabowo's public posture—defiant and nationalist—is not an accurate guide to the government's private behavior. On the MSCI issue, domestic political considerations will shape his rhetoric, but market credibility constrains what his administration can actually do. He will not publicly back down from a foreign institution, but the government will continue implementing capital market reforms in parallel. Investors should watch what the government does, not what Prabowo says.

Resource nationalism remains a structural watchpoint. The 8–9% cut to gas exports to Singapore reflects a broader policy direction: Prabowo's administration is increasingly prioritizing domestic industrialization and energy security over export volumes. The Danantara Sumberdaya Indonesia (DSI) currently covers coal, palm oil, and ferro alloys. Nickel and refined fuels are potential expansion candidates, and a revision to the oil and gas law is under active monitoring. Singapore-Indonesia bilateral ties are not seen as materially deteriorating; both sides have strong incentives to manage tensions through diplomatic channels.

On the domestic political front, Prabowo's relationship with former president Joko Widodo (Jokowi) is souring. Key Jokowi-linked figures are being sidelined through cabinet reshuffles, though the separation remains managed rather than public. Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka survived a recent court challenge, and his impeachment probability is assessed as very low, given the breadth of Prabowo and Jokowi's parliamentary coalition. Gibran can still obtain a qualifying diploma (via Indonesia's "Package C" program, completable in under three years) and is positioned as Prabowo's main rival in 2029. Other names emerging as potential 2029 contenders include West Java Governor Dedi Mulyadi, whose polling has already exceeded Prabowo's in some surveys; former presidential candidate Anies Baswedan, though polling lower than Dedi; and Agus Harimurti Yudhoyono, son of former president Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono and currently a coordinating minister in Prabowo's cabinet, who could run independently or as vice president.

Philippines: Impeachment math and 2028 stakes

The Senate's decision to recalibrate the impeachment threshold—from two-thirds of 24 senators to two-thirds of senators present—has lowered the bar for convicting Duterte-Carpio from 16 votes to 14. The current read: approximately six senators are very likely to vote in Duterte-Carpio's favor, approximately nine are very likely to vote against her, and five remain wavering. All 14 would need to vote against her for conviction. The situation is fluid.

Prosecutors have dropped three of four original charges against Duterte-Carpio, consolidating the case around a corruption-related allegation involving the improper accumulation of funds. This is a deliberate tactical choice—a narrower case is seen as more likely to succeed.

Short-term policy implications of conviction are limited. The more consequential outcome would be a Senate vote to permanently bar Duterte-Carpio from public office, removing her as the early frontrunner for 2028. In that scenario, the Duterte family would likely field one of her two brothers—one a congressman, one the mayor of Davao—as a presidential candidate. President Ferdinand Marcos cannot seek re-election due to the one-term limit and has not yet identified a preferred successor. A Duterte-family candidate winning in 2028 would shift Philippine foreign policy away from its current strongly pro-US orientation toward a more balanced posture, with less firm alignment with Washington and some tilt toward China. On reforms: energy security and power sector reform remain near-term priorities, constrained by limited fiscal space.

Malaysia: State elections and Anwar's survival strategy

Anwar's PH coalition is expected to lose the Melaka state election on 14 November—this would mark a fourth consecutive state poll defeat over the past year. Barisan Nasional (BN) is expected to retain Melaka, benefiting from incumbency, popular local leaders, and recent electoral momentum.

In Sarawak, elections must be called by December. The regional Gabungan Parti Sarawak (GPS) coalition is expected to hold its dominant position, bolstered by strong state-rights sentiment and its track record of extracting concessions from the federal government. Sarawak is key to Malaysia’s energy security and as a source of government revenue as it holds more than 60% of Malaysia's gas reserves.

The GPS-led Sarawak state government has been pushing hard for its oil and gas company, Petros, to gain a larger share of the sector, at the expense of national oil company Petronas. Mediation between Petronas and Sarawak is ongoing alongside a court case over oil and gas rights. A negotiated profit-sharing agreement is the most likely outcome, but a worst-case scenario—Sarawak winning full oil and gas rights in court—could trigger similar demands from Terengganu and other states as well as negatively affect federal revenue.

The state election outcomes will not impact the federal parliament. Anwar is expected to maintain majority support and remain prime minister. His response strategy involves two levers: higher public spending (the recent budget includes increased cash handouts and minimum wage increases) and expanded lawfare against political rivals, including the opposition Perikatan Nasional (PN) coalition (which faces a deregistration threat).

The general election is due by February 2028 but is expected to be held in late 2027. Anwar will wait unless he loses majority parliamentary support before then—in which case a snap election becomes more likely.

Key watch items

  2. Indonesian resource nationalism expansion: Monitor whether DSI-related restrictions expand beyond coal, palm oil, and ferro alloys to nickel, refined fuels, or other commodities; watch for oil and gas law revision.
  3. Philippines Senate impeachment vote: Track the five wavering senators and whether ongoing corruption cases against them influence their votes; a conviction verdict would restructure the 2028 presidential field entirely.
  4. Anwar's parliamentary majority: Watch for defections from BN or other coalition partners in Malaysia that could prompt a snap election call before the anticipated late-2027 window.
  5. Malaysia Sarawak offshore oil and gas dispute: Monitor the mediation track between the Petronas and Sarawak state on oil and gas rights; a court victory for Sarawak is the tail-risk scenario.
  6. Indonesia 2029 election positioning: Track Dedi's national profile-building and any moves by political parties currently within Prabowo's coalition to signal independence.
