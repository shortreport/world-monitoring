Nvidia scandal speeds up US curbs on China’s chips access
17 Nov 2026 08:05 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 

summary
  • The Nvidia scandal will pose threats to the company Nvidia and its suppliers, hurt the countries that host China’s computing power, and could threaten the US-China truce in 2027.
  • Oil oversupply will have a limited price impact through early 2027, as continued risk premiums are likely to set a floor in the $80–$100 range.
  • Democrats’ resounding midterm victory does not shift our view that negotiations on a limited US-Canada trade deal are likely to drag into 2027.
  • Further fiscal measures are likely to follow in Brazil, while Vice-President Geraldo Alckmin looks likely to be the next finance minister.
  • Countries and sectors in this edition of the Daily Brief include the US, Brazil, Canada, China, Zambia, energy, oil, geoeconomics, and global macro.

the top line
Global Macro, US, China—The Nvidia “spy” scandal will accelerate US-China decoupling
  • Washington will move faster to stop Chinese firms from using US AI chips, especially chips they rent abroad. The Nvidia scandal has tilted the Trump administration toward China hawks and given three bipartisan chip bills a likely place in next month’s defense bill.
  • Beijing will likely hold off through the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, but could hit back hard as Chinese firms begin to lose chip access. Curbs on chipmaking tools under the MATCH Act would provoke the harshest response, likely mineral export limits, and put the 2027 truce at serious risk.
  • Southeast Asian data center hosts, Nvidia, and US, Japanese and Dutch toolmakers will bear most of the costs of tighter controls. EG’s basecase of managed US-China stability through next year still holds.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: globalmacro@eurasiagroup.net.
Oil—Risk premiums set near-term price floor even as oversupply builds
  • Crude markets are heading toward an oversupply of up to three million barrels per day by the first quarter of 2027 as Middle East exports recover alongside production growth in other regions, especially the Americas.
  • While supply increases have already lowered prices from wartime highs, the building oversupply will probably have limited further impact through early 2027, as continued risk premiums are likely to set a floor in the $80–$100 range.
  • Middle distillate markets will remain tight amid refinery outages in the Middle East and Russia.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: ecr@eurasiagroup.net.
United States, Canada—Five common questions about relations after the midterms
  • Democrats’ resounding midterm victory does not shift our view that negotiations on a limited US-Canada trade deal are likely to drag into 2027, as the decision on whether to spend political capital to secure a measure of economic relief from a trade deal ultimately still rests with Prime Minister Mark Carney.
  • Democrats in the next Congress will pass symbolic measures aimed at opposing tariffs on Canada but have no viable path to reclaiming trade authority from the executive branch, leaving the White House in the driver’s seat.
  • Although some Republicans in a more empowered non-MAGA wing of the party will likely chart a more moderate course on the US-Canada relationship ahead of the 2028 election, Canadian public opinion toward the US is unlikely to significantly change under the current administration.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: unitedstates@eurasiagroup.net.
Brazil—Update conference call
  • Last week’s fiscal package was a first step, and further measures are likely to follow, potentially including a constitutional amendment.
  • Alckmin remains the leading candidate for finance minister, but the choice of chief of staff may be more consequential for medium-term fiscal discipline.
    • The market selloff over a possible appointment of National Development Bank President Aloizio Mercadante overstates the risk: his nomination is unlikely, and he has publicly backed the fiscal course correction (see QuickTake, 16 November).
  • The incoming senate will be more conservative and opposition-leaning than the current chamber, making revenue-raising measures very difficult to pass and requiring negotiation to advance fiscal reform.

eg in brief

US, Energy—Permitting legislation remains on track: The Senate’s 73-25 cloture vote on the motion to proceed on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act suggests it can pass the chamber. However, the decisive battle will be in the House, with party lines uncertain. Trump endorsed the bill on 8 November, though a day later said he would not approve wind turbines even if it becomes law. Despite these travails, the bill remains moderately likely to pass in the lame-duck session. If the bill slips into 2027, its prognosis in Congress would become more negative. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: ecr@eurasiagroup.net.

Zambia—Mines and agricultural output will determine budget credibility: President Hakainde Hichilema has sufficient political capital in his second term to push ahead with revenue-raising measures and spending restraint aimed at reducing the budget deficit by 2.9% of GDP. Despite positive developments in key sectors and in government, agriculture and mining remain vulnerable to climatic, operational, and geopolitical shocks, creating persistent risks to budget credibility. However, those potential shocks would probably not derail progress under a future IMF arrangement. IMF board approval is likely by early in the first quarter of next year. Read more here.
