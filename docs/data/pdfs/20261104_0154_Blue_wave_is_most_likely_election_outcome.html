Blue wave is most likely election outcome
3 Nov 2026 11:51 EST
Discover more on the Portal
  • Blue wave is most likely election outcome
  • Florida, Kentucky, Georgia the best early signals tonight
  • Trump will likely continue Donroe Doctrine despite midterm defeat
 

Blue wave is most likely election outcome. Eurasia Group continues to see Democrats the favorites to flip both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections tonight.

  • A flip of the House is near-certain, with Democrats likely to take around a 25-seat majority. That is a figure both large enough to be a workable majority—much larger than Republicans’ current majority—and for likely speaker Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) to isolate the Democratic Socialists of America faction, which will likely number under 10 seats in the next Congress.
  • In the Senate, the most likely outcome is a Democratic majority with 52 seats, but a majority ranging from 51 to 54 seats wouldn’t surprise us either (nor would Republicans barely hanging on, likely with 50 seats). Winning 52 seats would be much more comfortable for Democratic leadership than would 51 seats, as Senator John Fetterman (D-PA), under fire from his own party, could vote with Republicans on some key issues even if he doesn’t outright defect.
  • We do not see the White House as having viable pathways to challenge the election results (see White House has no good options for midterm disruptions, 27 October 2026). The Supreme Court’s rapid dismissal of a suit over California voter rolls is a strong indication that the court system is not willing to play ball with tenuous election challenges. The most significant legal risk post-election concerns a potential challenge to Alaska’s Senate result, which could metastasize should the race go to a ranked-choice tabulation, but even this would be extremely unlikely to alter the result.

Florida, Kentucky, Georgia the best early signals tonight. Early-reporting states aren’t definitive indications of the result, but strong Democratic results in Florida, Kentucky, Georgia, and other early-reporting states tonight would bolster Democrats’ outlook in Senate races across the country.

  • All of those states, in the eastern time zone, close polls relatively early and count quickly. We will be watching all three—along with Virginia and Indiana—for indications that pollsters’ predicted 11-point swing in the national environment from 2024 is indeed playing out on the ground. If the swing in those races looks more like 7 or 8 points than 11, expectations for a Democratic Senate majority will fall.
  • Senate races could remain close throughout the night. In Michigan, Democrats normally dominate in late-reporting Wayne County; if the race is close, it wouldn’t be surprising to see Republicans hold onto an early lead until well into Wednesday morning. Similarly, the likely close race in Maine will probably see Democrats gain somewhat as tallies come in later from the cities and towns in the south of the state; the result probably will not be clear until past midnight.
  • Alaska will be the biggest uncertainty tonight. Its polls don’t close until midnight on the east coast, its numerous remote towns can take days to report, and its rules on absentee ballots are unusually generous in allowing for late arrival. By midday Wednesday, likely under 80% of the vote will be counted, which will leave too much of the vote outstanding for a close race to be called. If neither candidate receives over 50% of the vote, the race won’t officially be called until the state tabulates ranked-choice results on 18 November.

Trump will likely continue Donroe Doctrine despite midterm defeat. Expected Republican losses in both houses of Congress will not have a meaningful impact on President Donald Trump’s approach to Latin America. The region will remain a priority because of domestic politics, geopolitical considerations, and Trump’s growing focus on his legacy. Most relevant policies also depend on executive action.

  • That approach includes the full Venezuela sanctions and licensing framework, as well as trade policies affecting the region. The Trump White House will continue to support allies through the Treasury Department, the US International Development Finance Corporation, multilateral institutions, and security assistance, while using sanctions authorities to pressure adversaries.
  • The administration’s country-specific priorities include delivering economic gains in Venezuela, securing symbolic change in Cuba, and targeting cartels in Mexico and elsewhere in the region. That issue will remain important to the Republican base through 2028.
  • Even so, Democratic control of Congress would probably bring heightened scrutiny of Trump’s regional policies. Investigations would likely focus on Venezuela oil deals and revenue management. A Democratic victory would also constrain the administration’s ability to confirm appointees.
  • Cabinet changes after the midterms could also shape policy, as a relatively small group of decision-makers direct the administration’s regional policies. Those changes could have significant implications for both Venezuela and Argentina (see: EG Update, 31 August 2026; Latin America Update, 28 October 2026).

Quote of the Day

"You can consider it barter. You can consider military aid or cash. What we give them — $4 billion of things and money — and they don’t need it. And I was approached by Bibi Netanyahu, and we’ve been talking about it for a couple of years because it’s a lot of money." - President Donald Trump, saying that he will end foreign military aid for Israel. The President's comments on Fox & Friends were characteristically off the cuff and wandering, and their timing indicates they were more so meant to energize the American political base rather than to serve as a genuine announcement. On the other hand, this proposition has repeatedly been floated by both Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Such a move could deliver a political win to Trump and Netanyahu, serving to assuage concerns about strategic autonomy in the righter-most wings of both countries, before a more fractious break could come under the next US administration.
