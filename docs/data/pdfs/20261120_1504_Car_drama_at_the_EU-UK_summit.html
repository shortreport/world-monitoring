DRIVING THE DAY

EU-UK SUMMIT KICKS OFF: British Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives in Brussels today for meetings with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa, along with a broader delegation of U.K. ministers and EU commissioners. Top of the agenda for the automotive industry is whether the U.K. will secure its status as “Made in Europe” in the Industrial Accelerator Act.

The deets: The summit officially kicks off at 10:30 a.m. before the leaders’ meeting at 11 a.m. Burnham, von der Leyen and Costa will hold a press conference at around 1:25 p.m. You can read the developments as they happen on our live blog.

Why it matters: Roughly 60% of automotive exports from the U.K. are destined for the EU, making any potential exclusion from the IAA a critical issue for the industry. But any agreement on the U.K. obtaining Made in Europe status hinges on whether London will work to prevent Chinese circumvention of EU tariffs.

Scale of the problem: The EU is concerned that Chinese automakers could use the U.K. to circumvent the IAA rules or other trade measures. Their worries are not unfounded. After Brussels slapped duties on made-in-China EVs, the automakers shifted their focus to the U.K. One in three EVs sold in the U.K. today is Chinese. Car imports from China by the U.K. rose 93% year-over-year in the first eight months of this year, according to a report from Rhodium Group.

Don’t get your hopes up: A draft joint statement scooped by POLITICO suggest Britain isn’t ready to commit to raising tariffs on Chinese EVs. Read more on that here.

On the sidelines: The Brexit trade agreement isn’t officially on the agenda but is expected to be discussed on the sidelines. Automakers on both sides of the Channel have lobbied hard for new rules of origin on vehicles and batteries to be delayed by at least three years. Barring action, the new local content requirements will go into effect on Jan. 1. Catch up on that here.

MARITIME

GLOBAL SHIPPING INDUSTRY UNITES ON IMO: Nine major maritime groups representing the global commercial fleet are urging governments to agree on a decarbonization framework at the upcoming International Maritime Organization talks, as warnings grow that continued stalemate, weak enforcement and fragmented rules could ultimately raise costs across global supply chains.

Why certainty matters: Lars Jensen, CEO of maritime analytics firm Vespucci Maritime, boils the industry’s position down to three things: stability, enforceability and uniform application. Ships typically operate for 20 to 25 years, he notes, so regulatory uncertainty raises the financial risk of ordering new vessels — and ultimately the cost of moving goods.

And enforcement matters too: Jensen argues that rules that cannot be enforced risk rewarding operators that ignore them, while penalizing shipping lines investing to cut emissions. He also points to the ballast water convention as a cautionary example of what can happen when global rules take too long to implement and national regimes fill the gap.

Who’s behind it: The joint call comes from BIMCO, CLIA, IBIA, ICS, Intercargo, Interferry, Intertanko, IPTA and the World Shipping Council ahead of the organization’s Marine Environment Protection Committee 85 meeting from Nov. 30 to Dec. 4.

The core ask: The groups want rules that are practical, fuel- and technology-neutral and sensitive to the cost and availability of cleaner fuels, while still providing clear incentives for investment in zero- and near-zero-emission technologies. They also want flexible compliance options while those fuels remain scarce or commercially unviable for much of the existing fleet.

CARS

COUNCIL VOTING ON HYBRID SAFEGUARD MEASURES: Member countries are expected to vote today in favor of the Commission’s proposed safeguard measures to curb imports of hybrid vehicles, three people familiar with the discussions told POLITICO. The measures will include specific quotas for China, the United Kingdom, Japan, South Korea, the U.S., Mexico, Turkey, Morocco and Serbia.

Catch up: The Commission opened a safeguard investigation into hybrids on Oct. 30 following a deal with China that would see Beijing curb its vehicle exports. Chinese brands have been flooding the EU with the hybrid models since the EU slapped duties on made-in-China electric vehicles in 2024. Tomorrow’s vote will be on provisional measures.

Country quotas: Typically, if a country accounts for more than 5% of imports into the EU in the previous year on a particular good, they would get a country-specific quota. Nine countries are expected to receive their own quotas. Any unused quota could be carried over from one quarter to the next, one of the people told POLITICO.

Deadlines: The measure will be published on Nov. 24 and go into effect on Dec. 1, the two people said. But it will not contain a shipping clause that would allow vehicles already en route to the EU to enter under the old regime. So any vehicles already shipped that arrive on or after Dec. 1 would be included in the quota. Borderlex first reported the shipping clause.

SCOOP — IRISH COMPROMISE ON CORPORATE FLEETS: The latest compromise from the Irish Council presidency on corporate fleets would allow EU member countries to give financial support to all vehicles, even if they are not zero or low-emission, according to a draft obtained by our colleague Jasper Bennink. A previous version would have limited public funds to only zero or low-emission vehicles. While financial support would be available to all powertrains, the compromise would require support to be more favorable to zero and low-emission cars.

AVIATION

UPCOMING REVISION OF SAF CRITERIA GIVES HOPE TO FEEDSTOCK ALLIANCE: Part of the sustainable aviation fuel sector is looking to the upcoming revision of EU sustainability criteria for SAF production as an opportunity to make intermediate crops — such as fast-growing oilseed crops — and crops grown on severely degraded land eligible for SAF production. Fuel suppliers and companies in the agricultural and seed sectors have joined forces in the Advanced Feedstock Alliance (AFA) to pursue this common goal.

Looking forward: The Commission is expected to open a consultation on the revision of regulation 996 from 2022 around the winter break, SAF industry representatives told Tommaso. The sector is awaiting this opportunity to complete a regulatory process that began more than two years ago.

Nearly there: Back in March 2024, the Commission expanded the list of feedstocks for SAF production to allow the use of “intermediate crops” and “crops grown on severely degraded land.” But this decision must be reflected and further specified in the sustainability criteria under revision to provide producers with the necessary framework for certifying these crops as eligible SAF feedstocks.

Fault line: The industry is pushing for less restrictive criteria that would allow these crops to qualify as SAF feedstocks. But environmental NGOs, as well as some agriculture consultancies, warn that this opening would have a limited impact on feedstock availability while creating greater risks of fraud and competition with food production.

How it works: Agricultural and chemical company Nufarm, an AFA member, has a clear model in mind: “We contract the farmers to grow our intermediate crop … [which] needs to be an off-season crop, it cannot be a main crop,” said André Paula Santos, Nufarm’s head of EU policy. He’s convinced that intermediate crops could provide farmers with a viable new source of income. “Agricultural land is being abandoned throughout Europe, because it’s not profitable,” he said, responding to critics concerned about changes in land use.

No competition: According to Hinse Boonstra, public affairs manager at Bayer, another AFA member, growing intermediate crops or crops on degraded land would not compete with food production. “The goal is that we have a regulatory framework that is supportive of these value chains,” Boonstra said.

Need for feed: Asked whether SAF produced from intermediate crops would be competitive in the increasingly crowded green fuels market, Nufarm’s Paula Santos pointed to the EU’s growing demand for sustainable aviation fuel. “The pool is so big and the challenge is so great that we’re going need all available technology solutions feedstocks,” Paula Santos said.

Better bio than synthetic: Turning to competition between bio-based SAF and synthetic aviation fuel, or e-SAF — whose first ReFuelEU mandate takes effect in 2030 — Alexander Kerst of BP, another AFA member, noted that “HEFA [SAF from animal fats or vegetable oil] is the most commercial and economic of all the technologies to produce SAF.”
