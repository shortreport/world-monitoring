DRIVING THE DAY

ARCTIC TUG-OF-WAR: As the European Commission reviews its Arctic policy ahead of an expected September update, China has launched its second consecutive large-scale Arctic expedition. Analysts are warning Europe needs to start paying more attention to the icy region, where countries are competing over shipping, energy and security priorities.

The Chinese mission: Officially, China’s four-month expedition will study climate change, the Arctic Ocean and geological processes around the Gakkel Ridge, located in the Arctic Ocean between Greenland and Siberia. But Beijing has also steadily expanded its Arctic footprint beyond research, increasing investments in Russian Arctic LNG projects and completing more than a dozen container voyages through Russia’s Northern Sea Route last year.

Across the Atlantic: The United States has also ramped up its own Arctic posture, expanding its icebreaker fleet and, last month, introducing bipartisan legislation that would require foreign government research vessels — including Chinese ships — to obtain U.S. authorization before conducting marine scientific research in or near America’s Exclusive Economic Zone and extended continental shelf.

Why it matters: The Arctic’s importance lies in the strategic leverage it offers. As sea ice retreats, the Northern Sea Route could emerge over the coming years as a seasonal alternative linking Europe and Asia for commercial shipping. Melting ice is also opening access to vast reserves of oil, gas, critical minerals and fisheries. The resulting competition over trade routes, resources and security is transforming the Arctic into a geopolitical flashpoint, with Russia militarizing the region, China expanding its economic and scientific presence, and NATO strengthening its northern defenses.

Europe’s blind spot: Arctic analyst Malte Humpert argues Brussels still views the region too narrowly. “I don’t think Europe is paying enough attention to the Arctic, period,” he told Martina, arguing it should be fully integrated into the EU’s economic security, sanctions and defense agenda rather than treated as a standalone environmental policy. Updating the EU’s Arctic strategy every five to seven years, he argued, will not be enough.

Missing the bigger picture: “Even within the EU’s sanctions regime against Russia, the Arctic has taken a backseat, with no broad measures targeting Arctic oil and gas infrastructure, Chinese firms building these assets for Russia, or the shipping companies and operators involved,” Humpert said. “For Brussels, the Arctic appears to continue to exist in a vacuum rather than as an integral part of Russian and Chinese economic and security considerations.”

Dutch reality check: A series of policy briefs published last week by the Clingendael Institute argue the Arctic should no longer be viewed primarily through the lens of commercial shipping. The think tank says the Northern Sea Route’s importance lies “less in the cargo it carries than in the strategic options it creates” for Russia, and calls for Arctic developments to be integrated into broader EU and NATO thinking on economic security, sanctions, navigation rights and defense.

Brussels responds: Asked about the issue, a Commission spokesperson told Martina the EU is updating its Arctic policy because the region has “garnered significant global attention due to its unique environment, strategic importance, and role in the international dynamics.” The spokesperson said the revised strategy aims to promote “peace, stability and sustainable development,” and said the review remains ongoing.

AVIATION

NO FULL EES PAUSE: Despite ongoing problems with the EU’s Entry/Exit System, an EU official ruled out a blanket suspension of the new biometric border checks for non-EU citizens, arguing that “the system doesn’t work if you have full suspension in one or the other entry or exit points.”

Targeted fixes: The official acknowledged that around 20 of the EU’s 1,500 border crossing points are experiencing significant delays and said the EU is working with member countries to improve staffing, infrastructure and implementation. They argued the bottlenecks stem from how the system is being operated rather than from the Entry/Exit System itself.

Meeting industry reps: During a meeting with representatives of the aviation sector on Tuesday, the Commission acknowledged airlines’ concerns over the rollout and said it is working closely with national authorities to address remaining operational issues. According to a Commission spokesperson, that refers to ongoing efforts to improve border checks, upgrade national IT systems, expand the use of automation — including the EES pre-registration app — and deploy additional Frontex personnel where needed.

Summer flexibility: Member countries will continue to be able to temporarily suspend the collection of biometric data during periods of severe congestion under the existing emergency provisions. But the EU official made clear there are no plans to extend those flexibilities beyond the summer travel season.

MEPS PUSH FOR TOUGHER AVIATION ETS: A group of senior Socialist MEPs is urging the European Commission to toughen the EU’s carbon market rules for aviation, arguing the bloc should move ahead with expanding the Emissions Trading System if the global CORSIA offsetting scheme fails to deliver.

In a letter seen by POLITICO, S&D Vice President Mohammed Chahim, Environment Committee coordinator Tiemo Wölken and former ETS negotiator Javi López remind Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra that the last reform of the EU ETS requires the Commission to expand the system to international aviation if CORSIA, the International Civil Aviation Organization’s global carbon offsetting scheme for international flights, was not sufficiently strengthened by the end of 2025.

The trigger: The lawmakers argue those conditions have now been met. CORSIA has neither been strengthened nor reached the threshold of countries representing at least 70 percent of international aviation emissions, they write, urging the Commission to activate Article 28b of the ETS Directive and ensure aviation emissions are priced more comprehensively.

Private jets too: The MEPs also call for emissions from private jets to be brought into the EU ETS, arguing aviation should not benefit from exemptions as the bloc prepares its next overhaul of the carbon market.

STRONGER AIR PASSENGER RIGHTS FINALLY APPROVED: The European Parliament on Tuesday gave its final plenary backing to a sweeping overhaul of EU air passenger rights, introducing new obligations for airlines on compensation, family seating and ticketing.

Blaming the lobby: Asked why the reform took so long, center-right EPP lawmaker Andrey Novakov pointed to the aviation industry’s political clout. “Airlines are profiting a lot. This is an industry that is generating billions annually,” he said. “I believe they have huge influence over governments, which makes the negotiations really difficult.”

Who pays? The Parliament estimates the new rules will add around €1.80 to the average ticket price. If fares rise by more than that, Novakov argued, the reasons should be sought elsewhere— from fuel prices and carbon costs to airlines’ own pricing decisions.

CARS

EU’S NEXT EV BATTLE IS ON VANS: Europe is at risk of repeating the mistakes it made in the passenger car market by easing emissions rules for electric vans just as Chinese manufacturers prepare a major push into the sector, Transport & Environment argues in a new briefing.

China catches up: The NGO warns Europe’s industrial lead is coming under growing pressure. Chinese manufacturers are expected to offer 18 of the 64 electric van models available in Europe by 2027, while European manufacturers continue to lag behind in dedicated EV platforms — 18 percent of production compared with 49 percent in China — limiting their ability to compete on cost and performance.

Don’t touch the targets: T&E argues the Commission has already made significant concessions in its automotive package by weakening the 2030 and 2035 CO₂ targets for vans. Further flexibilities — including weaker targets, allowing manufacturers to spread compliance over more years, greater recognition of biofuels and more favorable treatment for plug-in hybrids — would, it says, slash electric van sales to just 27 percent by 2030 and hand Chinese competitors an advantage.

Corporate fleets: The report also calls for tougher targets under the proposed Clean Corporate Vehicles Regulation, arguing large companies should lead the transition because they already account for 75 percent of electric van purchases. It recommends raising the Commission’s proposed EU-wide target from roughly 37 percent to 46 percent zero-emission light commercial vehicles by 2030, excluding plug-in hybrids from the rules and preserving the proposal’s “Made in Europe” provisions linking public support to European-made electric vans.

MARITIME

MEPS TO DEBATE MARITIME DRUG TRAFFICKING: The European Parliament will today hold a plenary debate on the growing threat of maritime drug trafficking, after the European People’s Party put the issue on the agenda amid mounting concern over increasingly sophisticated smuggling networks operating along Europe’s Atlantic coast.

Why now: The debate was requested by Portuguese European People’s Party MEP Ana Miguel Pedro following a series of violent incidents linked to drug trafficking in Spain and Portugal, including the deaths of law enforcement officers during maritime interdiction operations. The MEP argued criminal organizations are becoming better equipped, more aggressive and increasingly able to exploit weaknesses in Europe’s maritime surveillance and enforcement capabilities.

The political ask: Pedro is calling for the EU to strengthen the legal framework governing maritime enforcement and provide security forces with better vessels, equipment and operational tools. The EPP also wants the Commission to include new measures to combat maritime drug trafficking in its next annual work program, arguing the Atlantic coast is becoming one of Europe’s main entry points for transatlantic cocaine.
