DRIVING THE DAY

PARLIAMENT OPTIMISTIC ABOUT CAR PACKAGE: The mammoth package on auto sector regulations is still in the early stages of negotiations with the European Parliament proposing hundreds of amendments. Legislators are optimistic they can get it across the line before the end of the year.

On the docket: The European Commission put forward the Automotive Package at the end of 2025, giving automakers flexibility on meeting emission standards and allowing all powertrains past 2035 so long as they meet European preference requirements and offset the extra emissions with things like green steel and alternative fuels. What counts as Made in Europe, meanwhile, is being defined in the Industrial Accelerator Act.

Amendment frenzy: The ENVI Committee is the lead negotiator for Parliament on the CO2 standards bill with TRAN giving an opinion. Yet in TRAN alone, MEPs have tabled over 500 amendments. In ENVI, the amendments number into the thousands, Jens Gieseke, TRAN’s rapporteur on the file, said during a hearing on Wednesday. “That really reflects the political situation on the ground, and it sends a message at the technical level: We mustn’t repeat the mistakes of the past.”

Tick tock: Legislators are under pressure to get the package negotiated before the year’s end — an optimistic timeline. Despite that goal, legislators still aim to take their summer break. “The conversations are complicated. There’s not much time in hand, so I would make an appeal to colleagues: Have a nice summer break and then in September, we’ve got to get into this … we need to get it done by Christmas,” Gieseke said.

A pipe dream: The European People’s Party aims not to partner with the far-right and instead work with the center and the left, although that may slow talks. “I’m prepared to be cooperative. And we’ve got the democratic forces in the center of our politics,” Gieseke said, but he warned that any “new conditions” from other groupings are “really not helping and it doesn’t make the whole process any easier.”

CARS

CHINA’S ‘PIRATE’ BUSINESS MODEL: Top White House trade adviser Peter Navarro has taken aim at what he called the “pirate business model” driving Chinese electric vehicle maker BYD’s rise to global market leadership. Only the United States has so far resisted BYD’s “plundering global car markets,” he writes in an op-ed for POLITICO, calling on the West to unite.

Knowledge extraction: “The likes of Volkswagen, GM, Ford, BMW, Mercedes and Toyota spent decades teaching China how to build world-class cars in exchange for market access,” writes Navarro. “And BYD sat at the very center of this knowledge extraction.” BYD did not respond to a request for comment.

Catch up: China’s industrial strategy in the 1980s required foreign companies to set up joint ventures with domestic firms in exchange for access to the market. The EU wants to take a similar approach through the Industrial Accelerator Act.

U.S. hard line: The U.S. has already banned Chinese connected cars from the market, but regulators are going a step further and mulling a bill that would also ban Chinese components. The Senate committee in charge of the bill is voting on it later this month. The ultimate goal is to wait for Washington to relax its restrictions, Navarro argues, and then “BYD and its Chinese peers will flood the U.S. market with subsidized vehicles priced to kill Detroit.”

Breach points: While Western CEOs thought they were selling cars, Beijing was buying time, know-how and dependency, Navarro says, arguing that BYD then weaponized those advantages. Read the whole op-ed here.

BYD SNAGS FORMER HUNGARIAN FOREIGN MINISTER: Meanwhile in Hungary, former Foreign Minister Péter Szijjártó announced he is taking a senior executive role at the Chinese automaker. Szijjártó worked under former Prime Minister Viktor Orbán, who curried favor with Beijing by opening the country to investments from battery companies and automakers like BYD.

MILITARY MOBILITY

MILITARY MOBILITY TALKS KICK OFF: EU lawmakers and member countries will hold their first talks on the Military Mobility Regulation today, formally launching negotiations on legislation designed to speed up the movement of troops, military equipment and supplies across the bloc.

Ceremonial start: According to the draft agenda seen by POLITICO, the first meeting will focus on introductory remarks from the Parliament, Council and Commission, an exchange of positions between the co-legislators and a mandate for technical-level negotiations, rather than detailed article-by-article bargaining. The technical teams will take over negotiations in the autumn.

Commission heavyweight presence: Defense Commissioner Andrius Kubilius and Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas are both expected to attend the opening session, according to a person close to the negotiations.

AVIATION

EPP PUSHES BACK AGAINST COMMISSION’S ETS PLANS FOR INTERNATIONAL AVIATION: The Commission has yet to formalize its proposal to extend the Emissions Trading System to long-haul flights departing from the EU, but it is already facing resistance from the Parliament’s largest political group. European People’s Party ENVI coordinator Peter Liese told reporters on Wednesday: “In a draft of the Commission there was: Every outgoing flight should be covered by the EU ETS to Washington, to China, to Latin America. This will create a storm.”

What’s at stake: The assessment due Friday will analyze how CORSIA aligns with the Paris Agreement’s climate goals and whether participating countries account for at least 70 percent of international aviation emissions, one of the legal conditions for maintaining the ETS exemption. If the U.N. scheme falls short, the EU could propose extending the ETS for aviation starting in 2027.

Not popular with conservatives: He called the move “not a smart solution” and said he doesn’t know of anyone in his center-right grouping backing the idea. The EPP’s coordinator in TRAN, Jens Gieseke, also described it as “the wrong signal at the present time,” warning that it could “become a major flashpoint for foreign policy, security, and international trade.”

Suggesting alternatives: Gieseke told Tommaso that the ETS should be extended to hubs near the EU which compete with European airports, and not to all departing flights.

Angering Trump: The possible extension of the ETS is angering airlines and fueling concerns about fresh trade tensions with the U.S. and China, whose airlines could also be affected by the proposal. Donald Trump is very hostile to climate change efforts that impose any costs on U.S. business. “A Trump administration would almost certainly view such a move as an extraterritorial regulatory measure rather than purely a climate policy,” said aviation consultant Linus Benjamin Bauer. We’ll have the full story later today, so watch your inbox.

MARITIME

SHIPOWNERS LAY DOWN ETS RED LINES: European shipowners are urging the Commission to use Friday’s overhaul of the EU Emissions Trading System to boost investment in clean fuels and technologies, avoid double carbon pricing and safeguard the competitiveness of the bloc’s maritime sector.

Top demand: The European Community Shipowners’ Associations says Brussels should commit to reviewing — and ultimately withdrawing — the shipping ETS once a global carbon pricing system agreed at the International Maritime Organization is fully in place, preventing companies from paying twice for the same emissions.

Spend the money: The lobby also wants member countries to earmark the roughly €9 billion in annual ETS revenues generated by shipping to help close the price gap between conventional and sustainable marine fuels, which it says remain around four times more expensive, and to support clean shipping technologies.

Other asks: The shipowners are also calling for broader support for energy-efficiency technologies, automatic ETS derogations for islands, outermost regions and ice-class vessels, simpler reporting rules aligned with FuelEU Maritime, and any measures to support EU ports to preserve a level playing field across the shipping sector.

PIT STOPS

After declaring that it was closing the Strait of Hormuz, Tehran on Wednesday threatened to shut additional energy export routes including the Bab el-Mandeb gateway to the Red Sea, reports France24.

An agreement ending border controls between Gibraltar and Spain went into effect on Wednesday, giving Spain responsibility for Schengen controls at Gibraltar’s airport and port and taking down a border fence that’s stood since 1908, the Guardian reports.
