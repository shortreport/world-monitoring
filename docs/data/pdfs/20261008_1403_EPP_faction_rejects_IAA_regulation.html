DRIVING THE DAY

IAA AMENDMENTS BREAK PARLIAMENT: MEPs in the three committees in charge of leading negotiations on the Industrial Accelerator Act had until Wednesday evening to submit amendments to the European Parliament’s draft proposal on the regulation. They delivered — 5,300 of them, to be precise — and managed to choke the institution’s software system in the process.

Road bump: While Berlin is negotiating a broad deal, two of its MEPs — Christian Ehler and Angelika Niebler, along with a Swedish MEP Jörgen Warborn — are threatening to derail the regulation by submitting an amendment to reject the IAA entirely. At least 24 EPP members support the effort, according to a copy of the amendment submitted late Wednesday that was seen by POLITICO. The list spans five nationalities — German, Swedish, Romanian, Bulgarian and Irish — and includes Germany’s Markus Ferber and Parliament’s Vice President Sabine Verheyen, also a German with the EPP.

Indignant reaction: The move by the three EPP lawmakers triggered strong reactions from MEPs shepherding the file through Parliament. Anna Cavazzini, co-leader on the file for the Greens, had harsh words for Germany’s Christian Democrats, saying their “lack of leadership is a slap in the face for every steelworker, every car worker and every engineer whose job is on the line.” Renew’s Christophe Grudler said he was “surprised” but that his door remained open for discussions.

Franco-German grand bargain heats up: While German MEPs threaten to derail the regulation, Berlin is negotiating a grand bargain with Paris on the IAA and emission targets for automakers. German media reported on Wednesday that a deal had been reached, but a spokesperson for President Emmanuel Macron’s office told our colleague Giorgio Leali late on Wednesday evening that “negotiations are still ongoing.”

Catch up: As POLITICO scooped in July, the two capitals have been in negotiations for a quid pro quo deal on weakening emissions targets for automakers in exchange for tightening the list of the countries that would qualify as Made in Europe under the IAA. But the talks have stalled.

Hoping for a deal: MEPs hoped a Paris-Berlin deal would shake up Parliament. “The Franco-German proposal arrives right in the heat of parliamentary negotiations among democratic parties regarding the automotive package,” wrote German MEP Tiemo Wölken from the Socialists and Democrats.

Internal divisions: German Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s coalition government is split on the issues, with the Christian Democrats calling for scrapping the EU’s 2035 car emissions ban, while the Social Democratic Party wants stricter emission targets and for the IAA to favor Made in Europe.

Head in the sand: German S&D lawmaker Jens Geier was scathing about Merz’s Christian Democrats. “His people seem to have no idea how to tackle this complex challenge. They are tossing the proposal in the trash, effectively rejecting the future of European industry and its workforce.” The EPP’s Jens Gieseke hit back, telling POLITICO that “we do not throw anything in the trash.”

ARCTIC

BRUSSELS BREAKS THE ICE: International Partnerships Commissioner Jozef Síkela is in Reykjavík today for the final stop of his Arctic tour. He’ll talk about transport links, infrastructure resilience and climate protection in today’s speech ahead of the EU’s new Arctic Strategy, due to be published on Oct. 20.

Arctic wishlist: Síkela is expected to frame the EU’s approach in his speech around five broad priorities: supporting local and Indigenous communities; building a stronger relationship with Europe’s northern neighbors; boosting security and resilience; stepping up investment; and focusing on climate action.

Transport watch: The Arctic is becoming more relevant for transport as retreating sea ice opens northern shipping lanes. That puts a premium on resilient ports, icebreaker capacity, satellite coverage and other infrastructure, another area where the EU could deepen cooperation and channel cash from its Global Gateway infrastructure investment scheme.

Climate backdrop: The region is warming around four times faster than the global average, accelerating sea-ice loss and putting pressure on ecosystems and local communities. Shipping is a massive part of that debate, with environmental groups pushing the International Maritime Organization to tighten rules on black carbon emissions that worsen Arctic melting.

Why now: Brussels last overhauled its Arctic policy in 2021, before strategic competition in the High North intensified following Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

AVIATION

RYANAIR UNDER FIRE OVER EU OWNERSHIP RULES: Lufthansa and Air France-KLM are clashing with Ryanair over whether the low-cost airline meets the requirements to operate in the EU. The European Network Airlines Association, which represents the two legacy airline groups and their subsidiaries, asked the Commission and the Irish Aviation Authority earlier this year to assess whether Ryanair complies with EU ownership and control rules, which require airlines to be majority-owned and effectively controlled by EU nationals or entities to hold an operating license in the bloc.

Who owns Ryanair? “Since Ryanair publicly announced changes to its share ownership rules on 7 March 2025, there are doubts about the airline’s compliance” with these rules, ENAA said in a statement.

Below the threshold: As a result of those changes, by the beginning of 2026, “only 30% of Ryanair’s issued share capital was held by EU nationals,” ENAA added, citing Ryanair’s own annual report.

The timeline: ENAA filed its formal complaint with the Commission in March and with the Irish authority in June, the lobby told Tommaso. The Commission, however, could not confirm that it has received the ENAA request. The dispute remained under the radar for months, but surfaced Monday, after ENAA took the Irish authority to the Irish High Court, accusing it of being “reluctant to engage on the matter,” according to the lobby. Further hearings before the court are expected between December and early 2027.

See you in court: Ryanair hit back, saying ENAA, “whose members cannot compete with Ryanair’s low fares, is instead trying to misuse the EU ownership and control rules via the Irish High Court to restrict competition and choice for EU air travel.”

Defense strategy: “Ryanair fully complies with EU ownership and control requirements and will vigorously oppose ENAA’s contrived claim which seeks to ground Ryanair by suspending its air operating licence,” the carrier added in an emailed statement. Ryanair has previously stressed that its non-EU shareholders do not have voting rights.

Backing the request: When asked about the complaint against Ryanair, a Lufthansa spokesperson confirmed to Tommaso that ENAA “like any association, represents the views of its members.”

Fireworks today: The CEOs of Europe’s biggest airlines will appear together in Brussels today at lobby Airlines for Europe.

COMMISSION SETS 2029 DEADLINE FOR EU-QATAR AVIATION DEAL REVIEW: The Commission will carry out an evaluation of the EU-Qatar air transport agreement “by the end of 2029,” DG MOVE told the European Cabin Crew Association in a late July letter seen by Tommaso. The union on Wednesday rejected what it called a “ridiculously long timeframe.”

Some context: Parliament criticized the EU-Qatar agreement in April over the allegations of corruption involving a senior Commission official, who was fired in January. MEPs called for the agreement to “be evaluated and, if necessary, suspended.”

Trust the process: The cabin crew association called for “an immediate investigation yielding a written report before the end of this year.” But the Commission indicated that any decision would come only after the evaluation, noting that the Berlaymont “continues to have confidence in the integrity of the negotiating process and its outcome.”

MARITIME

HORMUZ SHIPPING FINDS A WAY: Even as attacks continue in and around the Strait of Hormuz, more ships are getting through using escorts, night sailings and other workarounds. That means trade is gradually recovering without waiting for a political deal, though risks and transport costs remain high.

Still dangerous: Another tanker was attacked in the strait this week, with 12 seafarers reportedly injured. The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations center reported 12 attacks over the past week.

The market adapts: Global commodities analytics firm Kpler has now shifted its base case away from the idea that Hormuz needs a comprehensive political deal before shipping can recover. Instead, it expects a slower, uneven normalization.

Crude is recovering, but at a cost: Kpler says Middle East crude flows have recovered, but that does not mean conditions are back to normal. Security risks are still pushing up freight costs, meaning buyers could regain access to Gulf oil but at much higher prices.
