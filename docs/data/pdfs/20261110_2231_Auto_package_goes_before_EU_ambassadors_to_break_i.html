Auto package goes before EU ambassadors to break impasse
By Jordyn Dahl · Nov 10, 2026, 2:30 PM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — The Irish Council presidency is asking EU country deputy ambassadors to give political guidance on the Automotive Package after talks stalled at the working-party level.

The presidency laid out a number of guiding questions for Coreper I diplomats to discuss, including views on compensation mechanisms to offset a newly proposed 2035 emissions reduction target for automakers, according to a note sent to diplomats ahead of Wednesday’s meeting and seen by POLITICO.

At a working party meeting earlier this month, the presidency proposed a compromise that would set a 90% emissions reduction target for 2035 compared to 2021 levels instead of the current 100% reduction. Automakers would have to offset the extra emissions with investments in low-carbon aluminum and steel and alternative fuels. An agreement on that approach never materialized.

The proposal is close to the European Commission’s initial December 2025 package that proposed a 90% reduction but limited offsets to alternative fuels and green steel.

France and Germany reached a compromise deal on the sidelines of the last European Council meeting that saw Paris agree to a 90% unconditional emissions reduction target in exchange for stricter criteria for Made in Europe for the car industry under the Industrial Accelerator Act.

The Franco-German deal helped broker a draft agreement in the European Parliament, with committees backing an 87.5% reduction target with various offsets.

But talks among member states remain at an impasse. Some capitals are “preferring to stick to the 100% target” while others disagree over the offset mechanisms, the presidency note says.

Diplomats will also be asked to weigh in on whether to establish a new category that would recognize vehicles running exclusively on alternative fuels.

Countries are similarly split on freezing the utility factor — the estimated share of total distance that a plug-in hybrid electric vehicle runs on battery power versus its internal combustion engine.

In contrast with the Franco-German deal and the draft Parliament position, the Irish presidency proposal did not establish a new vehicle category in exchange for maintaining the current utility factor until 2030.

Current lab-based measurements overestimate how often the vehicle is driven on the electric motor, while the Commission wants to update the calculation with real-world data, which shows the combustion engine is used more often. Carmakers and their national allies want to keep the lab-based utility factor as long as possible, as it allows them to report artificially low fleet-wide CO2 emissions and avoid billions in fines without forcing an expensive pivot to 100% battery-electric vehicles.

However, some countries are “strongly opposed” to keeping the current utility factor, the note says.

Diplomats will also be asked to give their views on the automotive components of the IAA, focusing on whether vehicles from Switzerland and the U.K. should be included as Made in Europe.
