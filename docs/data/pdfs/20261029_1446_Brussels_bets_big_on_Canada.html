DRIVING THE DAY

HERE WE GO: Canada and the EU are racing to lock in closer ties at a summit today, with both sides hoping Donald Trump is too distracted by the U.S. midterms to retaliate, five EU officials and two Canadian diplomats told me.

It’s a full house: European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, European Council President António Costa and a small army of commissioners and EU officials are descending on Montreal for a grand two-day talkfest with Prime Minister Mark Carney, who wants to create an alliance of middle-power democracies as a counterweight to Washington.

Trump’s shadow looms: While von der Leyen has been at pains to clarify that Brussels and Ottawa’s alliance is “not against anybody else,” Brussels is not naïve and knows the U.S. is watching, a senior EU official said. Trump has already vowed to lash out in the form of “serious tariffs” if the two sides get too close for his comfort.

But that’s not stopping the EU, which is seizing this rare moment to secure expansive new ties with over 40 million Canadians and their €2 trillion economy, and won’t let the prospect of American retaliation get in the way, another senior EU official said. Carney knows he won’t get another chance like this and is pushing for “structural changes,” two Canadian diplomats said.

Now or never: Ottawa wants to lock in concrete gains before Europe’s political priorities shift, a Canadian diplomat said. “It’s very fortunate we’re doing this summit now and not in six months,” the diplomat said, referring to the French election, with far-right candidate Marine Le Pen leading the polls, and the seemingly unstoppable rise of the far right across Europe.

The stakes couldn’t be higher: Brussels and Ottawa will commit themselves to cooperate on trade, energy, security, education, the Arctic and more, according to a joint statement obtained by POLITICO.

A note on terminology: Their pact will not be called “associate membership” after that term was dropped from the summit’s joint statement due to opposition from Germany … although von der Leyen was notably still using it to describe the relationship in a statement — even after Berlin had made sure it got cut — and social post on Wednesday night.

The real prize: The EU–Canada partnership could serve as a model for other countries seeking to join a Carney-style alliance with the bloc at the center, a third senior EU official said.

Bigger fish to fry: Another EU official predicted the U.S. president would be too focused on the Nov. 3 midterms to care about what’s happening up north. His Republican Party is polling badly in key races and is expected to lose the House.

Managing expectations: Trump won’t be bothered, a fifth senior EU official predicted, but only because the EU–Canada summit will mostly just produce “some nice pictures,” which won’t be enough to “trigger” the president.

Who’s who: Here are the key players on either side of the Atlantic at today’s summit, from Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich and POLITICO’s Ottawa Bureau Chief Nick Taylor-Vaisey.

What to watch: Von der Leyen will deliver a keynote on EU-Canada relations at 2:50 p.m., Brussels time, with Costa addressing the Montreal Council on Foreign Relations at 6 p.m. and both EU leaders joining Carney at a business forum at 7 p.m. View it live here. More details below, in Agenda.

TRUMP-PROOFING THE ICC

OSLO VOWS TO KEEP ICC AFLOAT: Norway stands ready to provide material help to the International Criminal Court to ensure “active business continuity” as it faces the threat of U.S. sanctions, Norwegian Foreign Minister Espen Barth Eide told Nick Vinocur.

It takes a village: “We are in close contact with the Netherlands, with Germany, Iceland, with a number of like-minded countries looking for ways not only to speak up for the ICC, but to ensure business continuity, whether it’s economic continuity or helping them continue to operate,” Eide said in a phone interview.

Weak spot: The United States does not provide funding for the ICC. But the court is heavily dependent on U.S. service providers for payments and IT systems, which would be shut off if the sanctions come into effect, with potentially dramatic effects on its ability to operate.

Warning: Eide didn’t spell out how Norway or others could help the ICC (EU chief diplomat Kaja Kallas extended a similar offer of operational support when she visited The Hague earlier this month). But he did argue that Europe should develop alternatives to U.S. technology if these are “weaponized to punish things important to Europe.”

Make it in Europe? “The only answer,” he added, “is to supplement U.S. technology with European alternatives,” while a “longer-term” response is to “boost European autonomy in data platforms.

Zoom out: The United States, which has already sanctioned 13 senior officials from the ICC, has announced sanctions against the court itself, but these won’t apply for another six months. Meanwhile, the Netherlands is looking to convince the U.S. not to follow through with its sanctions threat — while supportive nations such as Norway, along with the EU, try to prepare for a worst-case scenario.

The EU has delayed the deadline to apply to build AI data centers … but what is the plan and why a delay? MIND THE BUDGET GAP

UKRAINE LOANS OPEN €35B BUDGET HOLE: The EU’s next seven-year budget will have to find at least €35 billion to repay old loans to Ukraine, as leaders race to agree fresh funding for Kyiv during an upcoming summit, Gregorio Sorgi reports.

Summit twins: The Commission has doled out €171 billion in loans to Ukraine since 2014, with most disbursements coming after Russia’s full-scale invasion in 2022, the Commission wrote in a note seen by Playbook. EU leaders will gather in Brussels for a special summit on Nov. 26-27 to negotiate the next seven-year budget and commit themselves to raise a further €45 billion for Ukraine in 2027.

The EU will repay last year’s €90 billion loan after the war ends, but the interest rates are already weighing on the next budget. The EU stands to repay €35 billion in borrowing costs from 2028 to 2034 — or €41.9 billion, if interest rates rise by 1%, according to the note sent to EU governments.

“The Union budget is equipped to address these costs and has sufficient financial capacities within the headroom [financial cushion],” a Commission official told Playbook. But the EU executive wrote in the note that “a source of coverage [for the borrowing costs] is to be defined.”

EUCO showdown: This sets the scene for tough discussions, as Hungary, Czechia, Slovakia and Bulgaria insist that they don’t want to service the borrowing costs for any future loan — creating annoyance among Ukraine’s allies.

DASHBOARD

Many EU citizens think highly of Canada — but that doesn’t necessarily mean they think of the country as a key ally. In a September POLITICO Poll, independent pollster PF Global asked respondents in France, Spain and Germany to pick their country’s most important allies. Canada ranked lower than other EU countries and also consistently behind the U.S. Results from a June POLITICO Poll, however, showed that many Europeans hold Canada in high regard. Large shares of respondents in France and Germany said Canada has the happiest citizens and is the best place to live.
