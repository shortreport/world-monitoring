Shipping powers head into talks divided over emissions plan
By Martina Sapio · Nov 23, 2026, 6:00 AM · View in your browser

Global talks on cutting shipping emissions open this week in London with no deal in sight, as the U.S. rejects the plan outright and even the EU, its biggest backer, is softening its stance.

Two people involved in the negotiations, granted anonymity to speak about sensitive matters, said they don’t expect an agreement, let alone a vote, at this year’s International Maritime Organization talks, and see October 2027 as a more realistic deadline.

Over the next two weeks, first in a technical meeting that starts Monday and then in the Marine Environment Protection Committee, national delegates will try to narrow their differences over the IMO’s Net-Zero Framework, or NZF.

The NZF aims to spur a shift toward using cleaner fuels to cut shipping’s greenhouse gas emissions to net zero by 2050. It would increase the financial burden on polluting ships over time. Fees collected would go to help fund the shift to greener fuels and support developing countries.

Adoption was due in October 2025, but countries voted to postpone it for a year after pressure from the U.S. and Saudi Arabia.

The battle lines are familiar.

On the one side, the U.S., Russia, Liberia and petrostates either want no deal or a very weak one, worrying that environmental fees will saddle the shipping industry with high costs and harm economic growth. On the other, the EU and its allies are pushing for something like the 2025 NZF, although Brussels is now softening its approach in an effort to reach a deal.

The EU softens

The EU strongly supported adopting the framework in 2025, but Greece and Cyprus — both big shipping countries — abstained from the IMO vote that ultimately delayed the Net-Zero Framework from being adopted, breaking EU unity.

Ahead of this week’s talks, the bloc still wants the plan to keep both of its main parts: a global fuel standard that forces ships to steadily cut their emissions, and a price on greenhouse gases. It also still backs the goal of shipping having net-zero emissions by around 2050.

But a new November draft EU position, obtained by POLITICO, shows a softening stance that would allow emissions cuts to ramp up more slowly in the early years and leave room for a range of 2040 targets, which could be revised after a review by 2035.

The shift followed pressure from Greece, Cyprus, Malta and Italy, which account for three-quarters of EU shipping. In September, they called for “realistic, globally applicable and cost effective measures.”

But even the new EU position isn’t enough for the Union of Greek Shipowners. Last week, the industry association published a statement warning the EU against taking an overly rigid stance that “is steering the IMO process to a new dead end, effectively torpedoing the negotiations by depriving them of the flexibility necessary for any chance of success.”

Environmental group WWF has urged EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas to ensure member countries maintain a unified position in support of adopting the framework.

The U.S. says no

The U.S. continues to “firmly oppose” the NZF, Laura DiBella, chair of the U.S. Federal Maritime Commission, said earlier this month. “The NZF cannot be adopted, will never be enforced, and has to be off the table,” she said.

Russia’s submissions argue that the draft could sharply increase shipowners’ costs and that cleaner fuels and technologies are not sufficiently available. They also question whether the fund and pricing mechanism would deliver the intended emissions cuts, and call for a trial period to test the rules before ships face penalties.

Liberia, which operates the world’s largest shipping registry by gross tonnage and which has traditionally mirrored U.S. positions, questions the fund’s scale and accountability, the legal mechanics of payments and whether essential implementation guidelines will be ready in time. It also calls for a market-responsive fuel target that could tighten as cleaner fuels become more affordable and widely available.

New ideas

Trying to bring the camps together is an informal group of more than 25 countries, including the U.S., Saudi Arabia, Japan, Brazil and several EU countries. Dubbed the “risotto group” after their first dinner meeting, where they ate risotto, the group cannot submit a proposal itself, but its members could bring forward a joint text if they reach agreement, the two people involved in the negotiations said.

Two proposals to watch come from Japan and Norway as they aim to bridge the differences between the two camps.

Japan said in its submission that it would let ships contribute directly to approved projects instead of paying into a central IMO fund — a way of assuaging U.S. and other countries’ concerns.

Norway’s two papers would replace mandatory remedial payments with voluntary contributions, a gentler approach that leaves the framework’s structure in place. They would also add a technical multiplier to reward zero- and near-zero-emission fuels.

External pressure is also rising on the talks. The NZF is based on the IMO’s 2023 greenhouse gas strategy, which is due for review in 2028. If negotiations drag on, countries could end up reopening not only the rules but also the longer-term targets they were designed to meet.

“Clear targets, effective enforcement and meaningful incentives for cleaner fuels must work together,” said Joe Kramek, president and CEO of the World Shipping Council, an industry lobby. The council warns that further delay, or a framework centered on an open-ended “experience-building” phase, would prolong uncertainty for investors.

Fuel producers want a “technology-neutral framework” that focuses on rewarding fuels that cut emissions, said Felicia Mester, managing director for Europe and IMO at the Global Methanol Alliance. “Anything less will leave it with the same uncertainty and a growing patchwork of regional regulations.”

Climate campaigners warn that an IMO deal is crucial.

“Failing to adopt a robust framework would keep global shipping in its costly state of limbo, reliant on unreliable fossil fuels, while leaving the poorest countries even more vulnerable,” said Alex Springer, deputy director of shipping at green NGO Transport & Environment.
