On the Ground in China: Political and Economic Outlook

1. Political Calculus: Stability Above All

The dominant theme from Eurasia Group's on-the-ground research in Beijing, Shanghai, and Hong Kong is that Chinese policymakers are operating in a near-total risk-aversion mode ahead of the 21st Party Congress, expected in October 2027. Every official calculation — domestic and foreign — is filtered through the imperative of a smooth leadership transition.

This risk aversion is structural, not cyclical. The fifth plenum's tightening of lifelong accountability for officials has created a strong disincentive for any bold or innovative policy moves. The logical outcome: officials will do the minimum prescribed by Xi Jinping and nothing more. Private entrepreneurs have read this correctly — the prevailing business posture is to keep a low profile and wait to see if Beijing recalibrates after the Party Congress.

2. Succession: No Signal, By Design

There will be no visible succession planning at the 21st Party Congress. Xi's consolidation of power — illustrated by People's Daily imagery placing him above Jiang Zemin and Deng Xiaoping, second only to Mao — makes designating a successor politically untenable. In Xi's political logic, naming a successor immediately splits official loyalty between present and future leadership. The house view is that the public will not be able to identify any potential successor from what emerges in October 2027.

Approximately half of provincial-level leaders have already been rotated; the rest will turn over through next year, along with new military commanders and ministry-level officials by October 2027.

3. Chip Controls and Tech Decoupling

The new Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) rule — expanding chip export controls to overseas data centers serving Chinese companies and extending restrictions to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil, while tightening rules on foreign foundries serving Chinese chip designers — is the most consequential near-term development. This is accelerating China-US decoupling regardless of the Jensen Huang/Nvidia episode that preceded it.

Chinese chip capacity is far from competitive: roughly four Huawei Ascend chips are required to match one Nvidia H200, at more than four times the cost and energy consumption. The gap to closing is estimated at two to four years on the short end, 8 to 12 years on the long end, depending on the metric. Chinese policymakers have no illusion about near-term catch-up but are confident in their ability to ration compute and develop efficient open-weight AI models in the interim.

China views the new rule as violating the terms of the trade truce, which runs to January. A truce extension is anticipated but rising uncertainty given the incoming Congress and growing anti-China sentiment. If Trump is unable to exercise a presidential veto — which requires a two-thirds congressional majority to override — Chinese retaliation could exceed current expectations. Rare earth export controls remain the "nuclear option" and are not anticipated; a slow-approval mechanism is more likely than an outright ban.

4. Domestic Economy: Structural Shifts, Policy Inaction

Meaningful fiscal stimulus is unlikely before October 2027. The Central Economic Work Conference in December is expected to reaffirm existing support mechanisms: infrastructure front-loading across six network categories, consumption subsidies via trade-in programs, and modest direct subsidies. One watch item is whether Beijing broadens consumption support to the services sector (e.g., vocational training, leisure), but fiscal constraints and the political cost of underperformance make this a low-probability near-term move.

Positive structural dynamics identified: China's electrification and digitalization advantage (no power-supply constraint on AI data center buildout, unlike the US); rising domestic brand competitiveness displacing foreign market share; and a generationally better-educated youth cohort entering the workforce despite record unemployment. Gig work is absorbing urban job creation but is not assessed as a social stability risk at this stage.

On the renminbi: the central government's long-term preference is for a stronger yuan, but near-term policy favors stability with a slight weakening bias. A target of around 6.8 by year-end was cited, with 6.6–6.7 seen as too strong given pressure on small exporters, many of whom have been running at a loss since September.

5. Foreign Policy: Managed Tensions, No Escalation

China's foreign policy posture mirrors its domestic one — avoid surprises, avoid escalation, preserve optionality through the Party Congress. This applies to both the US-China and China-Japan fronts.

On Japan: the diplomatic impasse with Prime Minister Takaichi is structural so long as she remains in office and does not withdraw or apologize for her remarks on Taiwan. Beijing's original expectation that she would be voted out has not materialized, and diplomatic pressure may paradoxically be boosting her domestic standing. One pragmatic adjustment: China's Foreign Minister received a Japanese trade promotion delegation in early October — a signal to the business community that commercial engagement can continue despite the diplomatic freeze. This is being monitored as a calibrated adjustment, not a thaw.

On the Miami summit (Trump-Xi): expectations are low. China has criticized the BIS chip rule as economic coercion but has kept its response reserved and will not escalate before Trump acts.

6. Multinationals and Hong Kong

The operating environment for multinationals in China is getting harder and is not expected to improve through 2027. Key pressures: stricter data security compliance, tighter capital outflow controls, reputational risk (especially for US and Japanese firms), and ODI restrictions that effectively banned approvals for sensitive/digital-related overseas investments before December 1. Regulatory coordination across Chinese agencies (tax bureau, customs, SAFE) has improved markedly, raising enforcement risk.

Hong Kong's role as a financial gateway remains intact, and its IPO market continues to be the primary access route for Chinese tech companies. The demographic shift — mainland immigration stabilizing the housing market — is reshaping the city. The regulatory environment is tightening in parallel with the mainland, and the 1% scenario (direct intervention by Beijing with severe consequences) remains a tail risk that operators must price in.

Key Watch Items

  1. Central Economic Work Conference (mid-December 2026): Watch for any broadening of consumption support to the services sector and the overall fiscal posture signal for 2027.
  2. Trump's response to BIS chip rule: Whether Trump exercises a presidential veto — and whether Congress has the two-thirds majority to override — will determine the scale of Chinese retaliation.
  3. Miami summit outcome: Any US-China agreements or escalatory signals on trade, tech, or Taiwan in the near term.
  4. China-Japan diplomatic channel: Monitor whether Beijing extends pragmatic business-level engagement beyond the October trade delegation, and whether Takaichi's domestic position shifts.
  5. 21st Party Congress composition (October 2027): Watch for any unusually young additions to the Politburo or Politburo Standing Committee as the sole credible signal of succession planning.
  6. Post-Party Congress stimulus: Any policy pivot on fiscal stimulus or structural economic reform will most likely emerge only after October 2027; watch for early signals in the April and July 2027 Politburo economy meetings.

What Clients Are Asking

China-Japan diplomatic outlook: Whether the Xi-Takaichi handshake at APEC signals a potential thaw. It is bare-minimum diplomatic courtesy, not a turning point; the impasse is structural and tied to Takaichi's Taiwan remarks, which she is unlikely to retract.

Chinese chip catch-up timeline: How long will it take Chinese domestic chip producers to close the gap with Nvidia? Two to four years on the shortest estimate, 8 to 12 years on the longest, depending on the metric — with no illusions among Chinese policymakers about near-term parity.

Youth unemployment — urban vs. rural divide: Whether the geographic dimension of youth unemployment differs. Rural unemployment is structurally undercounted due to land system design, but underemployment is more severe in rural areas; the acute pressure point is urban fresh graduates, with over 12 million expected annually for the next two to three years.

Humanoid robotics — why overseas-first?: Why Chinese humanoid producers are targeting export markets rather than domestic ones. There is no established domestic commercial use case; roughly 90% of humanoids produced in mainland China are purchased by the Chinese government for R&D data collection, not the commercial market. The overseas market — particularly Japan and Europe, where labor shortages are acute — offers better near-term application cases.

December policy meetings — what to expect: The Standing Committee readout in the first week of December will preview the CEWC direction. Overall expectations are low — a reaffirmation of existing policy tools, not a new stimulus package.
