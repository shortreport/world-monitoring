DRIVING THE DAY

A MIXED BLESSING: The EU’s burgeoning friendship with Canada was already getting complicated before Donald Trump crashed the party overnight.

First came the will-she-won’t-she fuss over whether Commission President Ursula von der Leyen would attend Prime Minister Mark Carney’s speech in the European Parliament today.

Then came yesterday’s bombshell: von der Leyen’s proposal for Canada to become an “associate member” of the EU. A draft of the speech obtained by POLITICO before von der Leyen took to the stage didn’t contain the Canada line. And one diplomat said the version that capitals received didn’t have it either.

Nobody seems entirely sure what an EU “associate member” is, since that status doesn’t exist yet. “We’re entering terra nova,” Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said outside the chamber — which might have been a Canada joke, given that Terra Nova is the old name for Newfoundland. “We’re entering the unknown,” Hoekstra added.

Commission Executive Vice President Teresa Ribera conceded that the Canada move is first and foremost a declaration of intent — but called the idea “quite thrilling and interesting.” But MEP and EU law professor René Repasi said doing it “properly” would require treaty change. A more realistic route, he suggested, could look more like the European Economic Area. (Sorry, Britain: Associate membership isn’t on the cards in any post-Brexit reset.)

But here’s the really awkward bit: The idea wasn’t presented in advance to European capitals and even Ottawa seems to be distancing itself from the “associate” label, as Sebastian Starcevic and Max Griera report.

“We’re not there,” Jonathan Wilkinson, Canada’s incoming ambassador to the EU, told reporters in Strasbourg when asked about associate membership. “What we’re focused on is actually driving results.”

Stuck in the middle with you: Von der Leyen’s speech also floated the creation of a European Security Council that would bring together the leaders of Canada, the U.K., Norway and Ukraine. That’s an extension of Carney’s famed Davos speech in which he called for the world’s “middle powers” to stand together against autocrats, as Nicholas Vinocur reports.

But the European Security Council would sit outside of NATO — which might cause a problem with the military alliance.

Cue the Carney-val: Von der Leyen’s speech yesterday was reassuring — doctors won’t be replaced by AI; housing is a right; Europe is less vulnerable to another energy crisis — but hardly a barnburner. The verdict among those Playbook spoke to in Strasbourg was that it was all rather flat. Expectations are higher for Carney’s address today. One MEP from von der Leyen’s own political family said it could make the Commission chief’s unconvincing performance look even worse.

COSTA ON TOUR

COSTA’S BIG BUDGET DEBRIEF: European Council President António Costa is in Dublin today to debrief Irish Taoiseach Micheál Martin on his tour of EU capitals.

Costa has visited 24 leaders to bring them closer to an agreement over the EU’s next seven-year budget before an informal end-of-year deadline. His input will be crucial for Ireland, which holds the rotating presidency of the Council of the EU and plays a key role in steering the discussions.

Dublin will put forward a new negotiating document, or negobox, with revised figures that will set the tone for a leaders’ meeting on Oct. 15, which will last for two days and touch on other issues including competitiveness, enlargement and Ukraine.

Appetizer: After making it to Dublin on a delayed Ryanair flight, Costa will share with Martin the findings of his meetings to help him strike the right balance in the negobox. The taoiseach has been carrying out his own budget consultations, including a call with French President Emmanuel Macron and a meeting with the Visegrád Group.

“It will be a moment to compare notes and chart the way for the October European Council,” said an EU official. Costa’s priority is to narrow down which EU-wide taxes — or own resources — could finance the budget with strong backing among countries.

Costa won’t find encouragement from a heated discussion on the size of the budget among the EU’s 27 ambassadors on Wednesday, which was “the most useless session so far,” according to one EU diplomat. “It was smoke and mirrors,” said a second diplomat, adding that countries caused confusion by using different metrics to calculate their budget contributions.

With friends like these: Germany renewed calls to slash the budget by hundreds of billions of euros — warning that otherwise there will be no deal by the end of the year, and EU payouts will be delayed after 2028, said one of the diplomats. France retorted that this would lead to the expiry of Germany’s discount on budget contributions, better known as a rebate.

KOSOVO WAITS

A BIG LETDOWN: While Canada got a song and dance about associate membership, the countries that have been in the EU’s waiting room for years got nothing concrete from von der Leyen’s big speech yesterday.

And they’re feeling forgotten. Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich spoke with Jeton Zulfaj, Kosovo’s chief negotiator with the EU, who lamented that four years after officially applying to join the bloc, Kosovo hasn’t even received candidate status. “There is no criteria left for us to fulfill,” Zulfaj said.

“We’re doing good as a country,” Zulfaj added, checking off a list of achievements and reforms. “What is lacking is unity from the EU. And this unity is not going to just miraculously happen if nobody looks for it, if nobody pushes it, if we are not on the agenda.”

Five EU countries — Cyprus, Greece, Romania, Slovakia and Spain — don’t recognize Kosovo’s independence, broadly due to concerns about territorial integrity and the precedent of unilateral secession. That leaves Kosovo’s EU path hostage to the bloc’s lack of consensus about its very existence.

Russia benefits: “We feel like [Kosovo] has been very much neglected,” Zulfaj said, adding that Moscow was attempting to stoke divisions and anti-EU sentiment in Kosovo. “You cannot be complacent about the region and then wonder why there is malign influence … The people there are waiting for the EU to deliver.”
