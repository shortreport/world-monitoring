DRIVING THE DAY

READY TO RUMBLE: The EU is expecting Pedro Sánchez to come to the European Council spoiling for a fight on migration, five diplomats and one official told Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich and me.

The gloves are off: “Of course” there will be a fight, said one senior EU diplomat, adding that it will likely get “quite bloody.” Another said they wouldn’t be surprised if there’s an “intense” debate. They were granted anonymity to speak frankly about their national leaders’ expectations ahead of the two-day summit.

The Spanish leader is expected to take on Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni over migration. Taking a stand over the issue is seen as something that will play well with their domestic voter base, two of the diplomats said.

Prime time: Sánchez is behind in the polls ahead of a snap election on Nov. 29, while Meloni faces a tough election next year as far-right challenger Roberto Vannacci gains momentum, one of those diplomats said. “It will be helpful for Sánchez to pick a fight,” according to the other EU diplomat. “And it might be helpful for Meloni too.”

The ground is laid: The clash comes as EU leaders — and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen — push for faster returns and tighter border controls. That has put Sánchez, whose government has pushed more liberal migration policies, at odds with the bloc’s harder line. The Spanish prime minister came under fire from Meloni and other EU leaders after the mass arrivals in Ceuta.

Sánchez will arrive prepared, two Spanish government officials told our Madrid Playbook colleague Ángel Villarino Perez. He has been briefed through meetings, weekend crisis sessions and ministerial discussions, they said, and will point to results on returns, border management and arrivals. He’s expected to defend his record by citing a 74% fall in arrivals on the Atlantic route, which Madrid attributes in part to cooperation with Mauritania and Senegal.

I think I’ve seen this film before: At the June European Council, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen steered discussion toward the EU’s new returns rules. Sánchez criticized the bloc’s hard-line approach, while Meloni backed Frederiksen and attacked Spain’s decision to regularize undocumented migrants.

When all is said and done: Diplomats expect the row to produce little tangible change. “They’ll say lots of things to each other, and then probably go for a drink after,” one said. A lot of work has gone into getting leaders on the same page on the migration pact, the EU official said, meaning they don’t expect the broader agreement to be derailed by a diplomatic dust-up.

PARLIAMENT’S GAME OF THRONES

PRESSURE COOKER: Tensions are high in the European Parliament as its midterm jobs reshuffle unfolds, Max Griera writes. The EPP, Socialists and Democrats and Renew groups elect their leadership teams next week, while MEPs are fighting over who does what.

Burn short, burn bright: With nobody emerging to rival Renew chief Valérie Hayer for her job, Belgian lawmaker Hilde Vautmans briefly threatened a bid — only to change her mind the next day.

She lashed out at colleagues at a dinner in Strasbourg last week for failing to challenge the group’s five-year French dominance and said she would run herself, according to four Renew insiders.

She backed down the day after. “She has no intention of putting forward her own candidacy,” her office said.

But it continues: Bids from Malik Azmani and Abir Al-Sahlani have kicked off a crowded contest for the vice chairs, with all eight incumbents also seeking reelection.

Meanwhile, over at the EPP: Manfred Weber is trying to calm his troops after the group descended into all-out war over its vice chairs. He tried to roll over all 10 incumbents, but surprise attempts by Hungary’s Eszter Lakos, Portugal’s Sebastião Bugalho and Czechia’s Danuše Nerudová have upset the balance of power, according to six MEPs and officials.

French guillotine: Weber is lobbying to save close ally François-Xavier Bellamy from being ousted, according to three of the people above, because he is the center-right group’s leading French figure ahead of the presidential election. But detractors point to the French delegation’s 26% rebellion rate against the group line, the highest in the EPP this term.

Weber’s chess: “France is the key,” one EPP MEP said, adding Weber needs people “like Bellamy who can deliver with the radical right on our content.” Bellamy led on the returns regulation, passed with far-right support. The offices of Bellamy and Weber did not respond to requests for comment.

Over in the S&D, Tuesday’s deadline to challenge Iratxe García passed uncontested, and today’s vice chair deadline also currently sees no challengers. To head off infighting, the group added a vice chair for its eastern delegations. Biljana Borzan is set to take the post, according to two S&D officials.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Today there’s a meeting of the European Parliament’s Conference of Presidents … but what is that? It’s the top political body in the Parliament, in which the president and the chairs of the political groups meet to set the legislative agenda, decide the responsibilities and membership of committees and delegations, and discuss relationships with other EU institutions and national parliaments. The CoP normally meets twice a month.

BRUSSELS’ BUBBLE OF FEAR

MEAN STREETS: First, EU officials were relocated to Brussels’ Northern Quarter, which many consider unsafe. Now, a fatal shooting near the Parliament has brought the concern closer to home, EU staff unions and lobbyists told me.

Right on the doorstep: The person was shot dead around 6 p.m. on Monday on Rue Wiertz, in Ixelles, which is near the Parliament, the European Committee of the Regions and the European Economic and Social Committee. A resident of the street — granted anonymity over fears for their safety — told Playbook they first mistook the gunfire for construction noise. Police cordoned off the street, collected evidence overnight and kept a patrol there until morning. Officers were reassuring but vague when asked if it was safe to stay, the resident said.

The working theory: Investigators suspect a possible settling of scores linked to organized crime, though that remains unconfirmed. “Organized crime in general is a threat for our institutions, but the incident is not related to the EU,” Brussels Public Prosecutor Julien Moinil told Playbook.

But it has still alarmed people who work nearby. “We have received many reactions of fear and concern, just as there have been almost every day for our colleagues in the northern neighborhood,” said Cristiano Sebastiani of the Renouveau et Démocratie EU staff union.

It’s a familiar worry: EU staff raised safety concerns about the Northern Quarter after a stray bullet hit an EU building there last year, outside working hours. In 2023, an EPP parliamentary assistant was shot and injured on Avenue de la Toison d’Or.

“The situation has gotten worse in a way that’s almost visible,” said a lobbyist who works 300 meters from Monday’s shooting. Incidents like this, they said, make “people get paranoid” and wonder whether their street could be next.

ANTIQUE HUNT: As autumn descends on Brussels, Playbook’s Sebastian Starcevic argues there are few activities cozier and more fall-coded than antiquing. Fortunately, Brussels is replete with shops full of hidden gems and curios, from tchotchke-hawking holes in the wall to pricey-but-fun-to-look-at galleries … and we’ve listed just a few of our favorites below.

Reliek, on Rue de l’Enseignement, is packed with a well-curated assortment of art, furniture and curios, with its owner driving across Belgium and France for the best estate sale finds. This means the offering changes week to week.

Go for a wander: Basically all of Rue Blaes in Brussels’ trendy Marolles district is an antique lover’s paradise, but don’t miss Passage 125, which sprawls out over four whole floors overflowing with a chaotic jumble of trinkets and oddities.

There’s a dog: Costermans in Sablon, which has been operating for almost 200 years, offers a more refined, museum-like experience, with Old Master paintings, chandeliers, furniture and sculptures. There’s also a lovely cafe and courtyard attached, where you can order the tuna melt and say hi to the sleepy German Shepherd.

A few honorable mentions: Les Petits Riens in Châtelain … Dandelion in Saint-Gilles … 10gallery in Ixelles … and the flea market at Place du Jeu de Balle.

POLITICAL ADVERTISEMENT

**A message from EFPIA:Across Europe, the growing use of mandatory rebates requires pharmaceutical companies to return money to the state when public spending on medicines exceeds agreed budgets. Mandatory industry refunds to governments have risen from around 12% to 24% of pharmaceutical revenues since 2018, reflecting a wider trend of structural underbudgeting. These policies operate like a tax, with no guarantee that savings delivered on existing or older medicines are reinvested in newer ones.It is unrealistic to expect greater investment in Europe and faster access to new treatments while simultaneously demanding the lowest possible prices and highest government clawback rates. Europe needs to make a choice: medicines budgets should evolve sustainably, taking account of economic and population growth, ageing, disease burden and opportunity costs. **
