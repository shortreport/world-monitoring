Funding will be a constant battle in Congress
19 Nov 2026 08:16 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 

summary
  • Funding will be a constant battle in Congress, with shutdown risks pronounced.
  • Inflation and deficits will continue putting upward pressure on Treasury yields.
  • Tech outages will undermine sector confidence.
  • Hong Kong's March 2027 executive election will likely favor policy continuity.
  • Countries and sectors in this edition of the Daily Brief include the US, Brazil, China, Hong Kong, geoeconomics, and geo-technology.

the top line
US—Funding will be a constant 2027 fight as deficits hover above 6% of GDP
  • Congress is likely to push the government funding debate into the new year with a short-term continuing resolution at the 11 December funding deadline, at which risks of a shutdown over Iran and Ukraine will be pronounced.
  • A “Cromnibus” combining full-year continuing resolutions (CRs) for some departments and regular appropriations for others is the likely outcome of the government funding fight in 2027, which will be one of Democrats’ few sources of real leverage over the White House.
  • Deficits will likely hover at or above 6% of GDP through 2030, with defense spending and possible delays in welfare-program cuts providing a modest fiscal expansion and rising bond yields posing the most significant fiscal risk.

US, Geoeconomics—Inflation and deficits will keep upward pressure on Treasury yields
  • Inflation and moderately higher deficits from fiscal compromises under divided government will keep upward pressure on Treasury yields and the implied Federal Reserve path, with shutdown and Iran escalation risks adding to volatility.
  • Hot inflation data and hawkish Fed statements have already pushed Treasury yields and the implied Fed path higher, breaking from the historical post-midterm pattern of falling yields and a steady Fed rate path, while equity moves will likely stay limited outside tech.
  • Democrats' congressional sweep fits the historical pattern of inflation shocks driving government turnover, a trend that is likely to persist globally.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: geoeconomics@eurasiagroup.net.
Geo-technology—Tech outages weren’t hacks, but undermine confidence in the sector
  • Mounting pressure for efficiency and growing cyber risk are forcing a greater volume of change on fragile, technical systems in the form of patches, capacity work, and overdue modernization.
  • Interdependence across shared infrastructure and configurations can turn a single point of failure into a cross-industry outage, amplifying technically trivial incidents.
  • Failures, including broadly impactful ones, will become increasingly common and degrade confidence in technological systems; public pressure for accountability and government intervention may build, even if governments lack a clear mechanism for intervention.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: geo-technology@eurasiagroup.net.
China, Hong Kong—Leadership selection will likely favor policy continuity
  • Hong Kong’s March 2027 chief executive election will formalize a decision Beijing makes behind closed doors, with a 75% probability that an administration candidate will win out, offering continuity in support for the international business environment alongside closer alignment with mainland priorities.
  • The incumbent John Lee remains the likeliest individual choice, although Beijing could also select another official, with either outcome likely preserving the balance between national security and economic priorities.
  • A business candidate (15%) would signal a stronger push to reassure investors, while appointing someone with a Mainland background (10%) would represent a precedent-breaking move, potentially accelerating institutional alignment with mainland China while disrupting Hong Kong’s established governance practices.
  • Read more here. Reach out to discuss: china@eurasiagroup.net.
Brazil—A short guide to Lula 4.0
  • Voter pessimism and a slowing economy suggest President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva's popularity will fall more sharply in 2027.
  • Lula will pursue a modest fiscal course correction with a credible economic team, but it will fall short of earnestly opening room for lower interest rates.
    • Centrist parties still hold the balance in the lower house, but the senate turns right in February. The opposition will work with Lula on measures to control spending, but will block tax hikes.
  • As popularity falls, Lula will lean toward taxing companies and the wealthy to fund middle-class benefits. The pairing of the finance minister and the chief of staff will set the pace of deterioration.
  • Lula 4 will keep non-alignment: modest stabilization with the US without a tariff deal, broader ties with China, and more Mercosur agreements with middle powers.
  • Expect a stronger state role in critical minerals, energy, and AI infrastructure. Platform regulation will be the main friction point with Washington.

eg in brief

US—Peltola win puts Democratic Senate supermajority within reach in 2029: Democrat Mary Peltola was called as the winner of Alaska's competitive Senate election last night, unseating incumbent Republican Dan S. Sullivan and finalizing the party's 56-seat majority in the upper chamber. Democrats will need four net pickups in 2028 to reach a filibuster-proof majority, which no party has achieved since 2009. Even if Republicans were to gain, they have essentially no path to reclaiming the Senate in 2028. The major question will be whether the national tide is as blue as it was in 2026 (a D+14 national environment). If so, 60 seats is a real possibility for Democrats: Holding their own states and winning those they did in 2026 (South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa, and Alaska) would put them at 62. Utah and Florida could be wild cards. More realistically, though, the national environment probably won’t be as strong for Democrats as in 2026, even if it leans blue thanks to President Donald Trump’s low approval ratings. Brazil—Senate victory shows administration’s negotiating room for fiscal adjustment: The Senate finished voting on Complementary Bill 299/2026 on 18 November, keeping the limits on companies’ use of tax credits by 49 votes to 31. That margin exceeded the administration’s expectations and preserves a package that could improve the 2027 fiscal balance by up to BRL 60 billion. Senators made only marginal changes, including a higher cap on Kandir Law transfers that does not affect the primary balance. The lower house will likely pass the bill unchanged in a vote expected on 23 November. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti led the negotiations and will likely remain in his post. The next phase hinges on the incoming finance minister, with Vice President Geraldo Alckmin the frontrunner. A constitutional amendment proposal is likely (65% odds), with final approval most likely next year. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: brazilresearch@eurasiagroup.net.
