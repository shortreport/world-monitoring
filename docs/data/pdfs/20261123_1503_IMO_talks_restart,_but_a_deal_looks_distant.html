DRIVING THE DAY

NO DEAL EXPECTED ON IMO SHIPPING DEAL: Major shipping powers are debating possible changes to the IMO’s Net-Zero Framework — an effort to cut greenhouse gas emissions from shipping. However, two people involved in the negotiations told Martina they do not expect an agreement — let alone a vote — as a result of the two weeks of talks that start today in London.

Restarting talks: The NZF aims to spur a shift toward using cleaner fuels to cut shipping’s greenhouse gas emissions to net zero by 2050. Adoption was due in October 2025, but countries voted to postpone it for a year after pressure from the U.S. and Saudi Arabia.

First, the technical work: This week’s discussions are focused on the technical groundwork, with the formal Marine Environment Protection Committee meeting following next week, when the political fight over the framework will move up a level.

The two sides: On the one side, the U.S., Russia, Liberia and petrostates either want no deal or a very weak one, worrying that environmental fees will saddle the shipping industry with high costs and harm economic growth. On the other, the EU and its allies are pushing for something like the 2025 NZF, although Brussels is now softening its approach in an effort to reach a deal.

Middle ground emerging: Japan and Norway are among the countries trying to bridge the gap. Japan proposes letting ships contribute directly to approved emissions-cutting projects instead of paying into a centralized IMO fund. Norway, meanwhile, would replace mandatory remedial payments with voluntary contributions at different levels and add a multiplier rewarding zero- and near-zero-emission fuels.

The EU is shifting too: Brussels still backs a technical fuel standard, an economic measure and net-zero shipping by around 2050. But a draft Council position obtained by POLITICO softens its previous line and says the bloc should oppose forcing a vote if negotiations reach an impasse.

Enter the risotto group: An informal group of more than 25 countries — including the U.S., Saudi Arabia, Japan, Brazil and several EU members — is trying to find common ground. Nicknamed the “risotto group” after its first dinner meeting, it cannot issue a proposal, but members could bring forward a joint text if they converge.

RAIL

EXCLUSIVE — RAIL INDUSTRY WANTS ITS OWN EU INDUSTRIAL STRATEGY: Europe’s rail suppliers are calling on Brussels to draw up a dedicated industrial strategy for the sector. According to documents shared exclusively with POLITICO ahead of their publication today, they warn that the bloc is losing ground in global rail markets while domestic producers face rising regulatory costs and heavily subsidized foreign competition.

Why now? The European Rail Supply Industry Association, or UNIFE, wants EU transport ministers to use their Nov. 30 meeting to start shaping the strategy. The group says European suppliers can now access only 56% of the global rail market, down from 61% in 2022, costing the industry around €97 billion a year in missed opportunities.

Made in Europe: In a letter to Commission Executive Vice President Stéphane Séjourné, UNIFE wants tougher use of the EU’s trade and industrial toolbox, including closing loopholes in the Foreign Subsidies Regulation and introducing European preference rules through the Public Procurement Act.

Follow the money: UNIFE is also pushing for a dedicated public-private rail research partnership in the next EU budget, alongside substantially more funding through the Connecting Europe Facility and European Competitiveness Fund.

The pitch: The sector says it is worth €62.6 billion annually and supports around 650,000 jobs, while playing a role in everything from decarbonization to military mobility.

AVIATION

NEW ANSP LOBBY WANTS COMPETITIVE AIRPORT SERVICES: A new lobby for air navigation service providers, or ANSPs, is seeking to liberalize airport air traffic control services across the EU. It also wants to include performance targets in agreements between airports and ANSPs and introduce consequences for persistent underperformance.

New in town: Traditionally represented by the European branch of global association CANSO, European ANSPs now have a new lobby: the Association of Private Air Navigation Service Organisations, or APANSO, established by two private players in the sector — Spain’s Skyway and Sweden’s ACR.

State of play: Article 11 of the Single European Sky reform, known as SES2+, says that countries “may” allow airport operators to procure air traffic services under market conditions. APANSO wants that “may” to become a “must,” said Raquel Martínez, CEO of Skyway and chair of APANSO.

The current provision has allowed Spain and Sweden to introduce competitive tendering, with contracts awarded to Skyway — which operates at airports including Alicante, Ibiza and Valencia — and ACR — which operates at 20 Swedish regional airports.

Sovereignty matters: Countries should still be allowed to choose ANSPs they feel best serve national interests, Martínez told Tommaso. This would allow them to keep major airports under the control of national service providers, but only after a competitive tendering process with transparent selection criteria. “That transparency will also force state-owned ANSPs to operate better,” Martínez said.

Competition pays? Martínez cited assessments showing that introducing competition in airport air traffic control services led to cost reductions of 50% in Spain and 30% in Sweden.

What’s stopping it? Most countries have so far chosen not to liberalize these services, Martínez said. She also cited service continuity and safety as two potential barriers for countries, even though privately controlled ANSPs must comply with the same certification requirements as their publicly owned peers.

Most ANSPs disagree. “Circumstances are very different between countries, which is why we believe this should remain a national decision,” said Enrico Parini, director of European affairs at CANSO Europe, the sector’s largest lobby. “Cost is important, but it is not the only consideration for airport air navigation services,” he added, noting that some airports operate primarily to provide connectivity.

Military concerns: “Others may be considered part of a country’s critical infrastructure, including for potential military use,” he added, arguing that these considerations help explain why most countries prefer to keep airport services under the control of national providers.

CARS

COUNTRIES APPROVE HYBRID SAFEGUARDS: A committee vote by member states on Friday approved the Commission’s proposed provisional measures to curb the import of hybrid vehicles (more on that here). Only Cyprus abstained.

Affordability concerns: “While recognising the need to address market distortions, Cyprus abstained over concerns relating to potential effects on smaller markets, consumers’ choices and affordability,” a Cypriot official told our colleague Camille Gijs.

EU PRODUCTION WOULD MEET BATTERY DEMANDS, REPORT SAYS: The Industrial Accelerator Act would set local content requirements for battery production. The industry has pushed back on the regulation, arguing it needs more time to build up domestic supply to meet current demand. However, a new report from the International Council of Clean Transportation — released today and seen in advance by POLITICO — says EU production would meet initial battery-origin requirements.

Reminder: The IAA aims to build clean technology supply chains to improve competitiveness and reduce dependency on China, particularly in batteries. Under the Commission’s proposal, three main battery components will need to be produced in the EU once the regulation is adopted. After three years, those requirements increase to five components, including cells and cathode active materials.

Not a problem: The report said that current battery cell, module and pack production capacities across the EU are “sufficient” to meet the demand for cars that are eligible for public funds.

…for now: The more stringent requirements, however, would require the EU to allow some trading partners to qualify as Made in Europe, the report found. The problem is the announced cathode-material capacity in the EU would cover only 62% of projected demand in 2030. The ICCT argues that some trading partners will be need to be included to meet the stricter targets, but could be restricted to Japan and South Korea.

MORE EUROPEANS CONSIDERING EVS: Consumers in six of Europe’s biggest markets — France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Spain and the U.K. — are considering buying an electric vehicle next, according to a new survey out today from Verian, a research consultancy. The impetus, they say, is the war in Iran.

All about €€: The war in Iran has dramatically increased fuel costs. In Poland, 37% of those surveyed planned to buy an EV next, while 34% in Germany intend to do so.
