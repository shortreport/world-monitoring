DRIVING THE DAY

NOT ANOTHER MEETING: Ursula von der Leyen’s pitch to launch a European Security Council is being greeted with profound skepticism in parts of the EU — including in Denmark, where Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, touted as a potential leader of the council, has signaled she wants nothing to do with the idea.

The Security Council was a key point in the Commission president’s State of the Union speech Wednesday, billed as a platform where Canada, Norway, Ukraine and the U.K. could discuss shared threats. It would be a safe space outside NATO at a time when Washington is often at odds with its allies.

But some EU officials and diplomats have responded with brutal feedback, telling Playbook that it would merely add more meetings to leaders’ schedules without fixing problems of confidentiality in the EU.

“Can’t see it coming to life in reality,” deadpanned one senior EU official.

An EU diplomat riffed on Europe’s seemingly ever-expanding list of discussion formats: “After Ukraine, Canada, Norway and the U.K. meet at NATO, they pop over to the European Council, grab a coffee with the Coalition of the Willing, join the UDCG [Ukraine Defense Contact Group], reconvene at the European Political Community, and then finally gather in the European Security Council to act decisively.”

“Clearly,” the diplomat added, “what’s been holding Europe back on the world stage all these years … [is] a shortage of meetings.”

Unlike the proposal to offer Canada “associate member” status, this initiative didn’t come as a surprise. European Council President António Costa pitched the Security Council to several leaders during a recent huddle in Finland, including Frederiksen. That fed speculation in local media that she could lead the group.

Which made sense: Tensions between Copenhagen and Washington over Greenland might make Denmark an obvious candidate for a group that doesn’t include the U.S.

But a Danish official doused the idea, telling Playbook: “The Danish prime minister is very into all things involving security in Europe, but we are quite skeptical about this [Security Council] construction. Basically, we have a lot of meetings.”

Another complaint: That EU meetings are ripe for eavesdropping. “The fact is that our defense officials don’t feel comfortable discussing sensitive matters in EU formats,” said a third EU diplomat. “Why would this be any different?”

The bottom line: EU diplomats can be harsh critics, especially when they’re speaking off the record. But when even Denmark isn’t interested — despite its interest in holding security discussions away from the U.S. — it suggests this could be another von der Leyen idea that struggles to become reality.

FISCAL FACE-OFF

THE €2 TRILLION OP-ED WAR: Germany and Spain have turned to separate POLITICO opinion pieces to spar over the scale of the EU’s next seven-year budget, setting the stage for fraught negotiations ahead of an informal end-of-year deadline.

EU governments are battling to preserve pet projects and curb the sums they send to Brussels. But after nearly a year of negotiations they’re still a long way from a deal, Playbook’s Gregorio Sorgi reports from Dublin after European Council President António Costa stopped there on his tour of 25 capitals yesterday.

The competing articles on our site this morning expose their widening differences:

Op-ed No. 1: Spain’s Economy Minister Carlos Cuerpo argues that the budget should rise to 2% of EU gross national income, while proposing a delay to post-Covid debt repayments to release €11 billion annually for agriculture, defense and competitiveness.

“The next framework should have a ceiling equivalent to 2% of EU GNI — enough to support both the economy and society,” Cuerpo writes.

Op-ed No. 2: Germany’s Friedrich Merz and the leaders of Denmark, the Netherlands, Austria and Finland push back against increasing the budget, insisting that additional money and borrowing are not the solution. “We cannot just add every new priority to every old one and send the bill to taxpayers,” they write.

The five countries are urging Ireland, which holds the rotating presidency of the Council of the EU, to remove hundreds of billions of euros from its next negotiating document — though officials anticipate there will be a compromise involving less severe cuts.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The U.N. General Assembly kicks off in New York next week. How does the European Union participate? Since 2011, the EU has had what’s known as enhanced observer status. That means it can participate in debates, make statements and proposals, and address the General Assembly. Crucially, the EU can’t vote, although all 27 member countries can, and the EU can help coordinate those votes.

EU STAFF ON THE MOVE

EUROCRATS GO NORTH: More EU officials are set to decamp to Brussels’ Northern Quarter after national capitals approved the rental of new office space earlier this month. That’s unlikely to be welcome news for hundreds of well-heeled Eurocrats who are reluctant to swap Schuman for the gritty Quartier Nord.

Leading by example: The EU’s own running costs have attracted increasing scrutiny during negotiations over the next seven-year budget, with EU governments heaping pressure on the Commission to save money.

The EU executive insists that it is tightening its belt. A Commission official said it is delivering on its promise to cut office space by a quarter between 2021 and 2030. Three EU agencies have already relocated to Boulevard Simon Bolivar in the Northern Quarter, with others set to follow suit.

The Belgian authorities have encouraged moving some EU buildings outside of Schuman to gentrify poorer neighborhoods. During a meeting of EU government officials earlier this month, the Belgian representative dismissed concerns around the Northern Quarter’s safety, noting that several Belgian government departments are based there.

Cristiano Sebastiani, president of the Renouveau et Démocratie trade union, slammed the move north as “entirely unjustified.” He told Playbook: “The health, safety and security of our staff must, now more than ever, remain the institution’s first priority.”

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Sunny intervals. High 21C.

UNWINDING AFTER SOTEU: The State of the Union week is tough on everyone — commissioners included. Luckily, two of them have found good ways to decompress …

Plough on: Agriculture Commissioner Christophe Hansen was in Tullamore yesterday for Ireland’s National Ploughing Championships, alongside Irish Agriculture Minister Martin Heydon and, naturally, plenty of farmers. The three-day fair drew around 243,000 people, with competitors judged on everything from the straightness and depth of their furrows to the neatness of the finish. Bonus event: a brown-bread baking contest.

Sex, drugs and streaming: Culture Commissioner Glenn Micallef gets an altogether different post-SOTEU outing today: Hamburg’s Reeperbahn Festival, centered on the city’s famous nightlife and red-light district — and the neighborhood where the Beatles cut their teeth.

He’s there on official business, obviously: Reeperbahn is also one of Europe’s big music-industry gatherings and a showcase for emerging artists. Micallef speaks at the European Streaming Economy Summit on the future of music streaming at 1:45 p.m.

SAKHAROV NOMINEES: The S&D this week nominated the International Criminal Court for the European Parliament’s Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought, saying it was defending the judges’ independence from pressure by the U.S. Other candidates put forward for this year’s prize include Tanzanian opposition leader Tundu Lissu (ECR); El Salvador’s President Nayib Bukele (ESN); Israeli NGO Standing Together (Renew); and Palestinian doctor Hussam Abu Safiya (the Greens and Left).

SPOTTED … at the speakeasy in Strasbourg on Wednesday night: Portuguese Socialists and Democrats MEP Isilda Gomes, who went on a night out to celebrate her 75th birthday … and left shortly after because the bar was too crowded — the most crowded Playbook has ever seen! Time for a new watering hole. Ideas?

Also spotted … at Acumen’s Champagne-fueled shindig at KBR rooftop terrace: ePURE’s Craig Winneker; CCIA’s Albane Vannier; Disney’s Thomas Spiller; EUCOPE’s Matthias Heck; Fourtold’s Victoria Main; Boldt’s Simon Taylor; Euractiv’s Josh Posaner, Thomas Moller-Nielsen and Magdalena Kensy; POLITICO’s Mari Eccles and Dominic Giannini.

OPEN HOUSE, BRUSSELS: It’s Brussels Heritage Days this weekend, meaning Saturday and Sunday offer that rare chance to snoop around places normally closed to the public. And if that doesn’t sound exciting, trust Playbook: There are some gems, including private estates and historic residences such as Domaine Allard in Uccle and Domaine Tournay-Solvay in Watermael-Boitsfort.

The EU district pépite: Even the EU quarter has hidden heritage. The building housing Lithuania’s permanent representation to the EU was once a grand private townhouse and later part of the Hôtel Dorlodot. It’s been heritage-listed since 1993. Bad news: Tours are already fully booked, the embassy said on X. Good news: You can still look up next time you walk past.

A SECOND RAY OF SŪN SHINE: The EU Quarter’s favorite Japanese coffee shop has opened its second location, this time in Ixelles. You’ll find the same top-notch sesame lattes and cold brews. Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich writes in to report the tuna onigiri and katsu sandos are just as good, though the bowls can be a bit hit and miss. Open Monday-Saturday, but sadly, SŪN No. 2 is closed on Sundays. Rue Defacqz 24.
