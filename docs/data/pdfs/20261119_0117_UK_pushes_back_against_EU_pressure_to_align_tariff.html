UK pushes back against EU pressure to align tariffs on Chinese cars
By Graham Lanktree, Jon Stone, Camille Gijs · Nov 18, 2026, 5:15 PM · View in your browser

LONDON — Britain isn’t ready to commit to raising tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles in exchange for access to EU supply chains ahead of a pivotal summit this week.

London has pushed back on wording in a draft joint statement, obtained by POLITICO, that could be interpreted as binding it to align with EU trade protections.

Brussels imposed tariffs of up to 35% on Chinese electric vehicle imports in 2024, following an anti-subsidy investigation.

Ahead of Friday’s U.K.-EU summit, Prime Minister Andy Burnham described Chinese over-production of EVs as a shared “problem” for the EU and U.K.

But the U.K.’s latest suggested wording in the draft statement, which remains subject to change, indicates London is resisting the EU’s efforts to force London to align with its tariff regime.

A U.K. official, granted anonymity to discuss the talks, said no decision had yet been taken.

The statement sets out that the two sides will immediately establish a Dialogue on Economic Security to coordinate on economic security and supply chains, and address economic dependencies through a “partnership for economic resilience” that has “the principle of trust at its core.”

Where the EU and the U.K. agree to cooperate to defend mutual supply chains like the automotive sector, according to Brussels’ wording added to the document, “the UK would be bound to ensure in its policies and national legal order the same outcomes as regards other international partners as the relevant EU measures.”

This would “ensure that the supply chain in question is protected in a consistent way across the EU and U.K. markets,” the EU’s wording states.

However, in wording suggested by the U.K., London removes the “bound to” phrasing, instead saying only that it would “aim to protect supply chains consistently across our markets and guard against potential risks of circumvention.”

As well as the discussion over tariffs, the U.K.’s access to the proposed Made in Europe program is likely to be conditional on a deal to lower tuition fees for EU students agreed within six months of the summit. The program is still going through the EU legislative process, so Brussels says it cannot make any assurances to the U.K. yet.

In draft wording that is not currently disputed, the two sides said: “We will notably work towards diversified and resilient steel and electric vehicles supply chains, and emerging technologies together. The EU and the U.K. commit to taking work forward on this partnership as a matter of priority, and to review progress, in particular in these sectors, every four months.”

Britain’s Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds said in mid-October that Britain wouldn’t be forced into following Europe in combating the surge in Chinese vehicles entering its market.

“When we make any trade decision in terms of trade defence tariffs in relation to the EU, that’s based on the U.K. interest — it’s not based on any other country asking for that,” Reynolds told The Guardian. “What is the fundamental nature of the UK automotive sector? It’s an export-led sector, so that’s what I’ve got to take into account.”

Mathieu Pollet also contributed to this report.
