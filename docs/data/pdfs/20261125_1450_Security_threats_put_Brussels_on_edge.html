DRIVING THE DAY

ARE WE SAFE? An attack on a European commissioner has triggered significant unease over personal security among the people working in the EU institutions, five officials in commissioners’ cabinets told Playbook.

Last week’s incident, when anti-pesticide activists threw a gray powder over Health Commissioner Olivér Várhelyi as he gave a speech, has added to a growing sense of vulnerability in Brussels, coming amid a major cyber breach and crime near EU buildings.

The temperature is rising: Officials say they were already anxious about increasingly intense activist campaigns — which have included posters of European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s face plastered around the city — and what they view as a lack of protection when prominent EU politicians appear in public.

“Our security during missions [trips] is excellent,” said one official granted anonymity to speak candidly. “The problem is at events.” Close protection officers hired by the Commission to protect senior officials and VIPs on premises and during missions aren’t usually deployed when commissioners appear at events in Brussels, the officials said.

The broader sense of insecurity is “linked to rampant crime, including shootings,” said Cristiano Sebastiani, president of the Renouveau et Démocratie staff union. Those fears are being compounded by the prospect of more EU staff relocating to Brussels’ Northern Quarter, a neighborhood with a reputation for being unsafe, he added.

It’s not just physical threats … The European Commission’s acknowledgment on Monday that names and email addresses were leaked in the cyberattack has only deepened the unease.

It doesn’t help that staff felt they were left in the dark during the cyberattack. “It won’t be easy for the administration to regain the trust of our colleagues,” Sebastiani said. “Good intentions won’t be enough.” Even those willing to accept the reasons for the silence between Oct. 22, when the cyberattack started, and Nov. 19, when Playbook broke the story, were “outraged to have discovered that even after confirming the information to the press, the administration did not deign to inform the staff,” he said.

Lessons learned? The organizers of the European Cancer Summit, the annual policy event where the incident involving Várhelyi happened, told POLITICO’s Mari Eccles they’ve launched a comprehensive review of their security procedures. “The safety of our speakers and delegates remains our priority,” Álvaro Jimber from the European Cancer Organization said, though he declined to disclose any changes to avoid undermining their effectiveness.

The EU executive “takes the safety and security of all its personnel as well as Members of the College very seriously,” Commission spokesperson Balazs Ujvari told Playbook. He added that the Commission will “continuously assess potential security threats to Commissioners in the exercise of their duties, including their participation in external events, and adapt security arrangements accordingly.”

MOVING AROUND WESTERN BALKANS MONEY

MORE CASH FOR THE GOOD PUPILS: We know Ursula von der Leyen loves a good announcement, especially when there’s a summit on the agenda. Today’s EU-Western Balkans gathering offers just the occasion: The Commission is preparing to reward the region’s star pupils with extra funding, redistributing money forfeited by countries that missed reform deadlines.

The Commission’s gifts: Montenegro and Albania are set to receive a 15% boost to their allocations, while North Macedonia will get 10% more, two EU officials familiar with the plan told Playbook. The losers? Serbia, Kosovo and Bosnia and Herzegovina. The Commission has finalized its assessment and an announcement could come “very shortly,” a third official said.

It’s not new money: The reallocation comes under the EU’s €6 billion Growth Plan, launched in 2023, which ties financial support to reform progress. Leaders will discuss its implementation today. The message to slower reformers? Miss your deadlines and someone else gets the cash.

Enlargement on steroids: This is the third EU-Western Balkans summit in less than 12 months — a sign of renewed political momentum behind enlargement and an unprecedented acceleration in accession negotiations, another EU official said.

Mind the wording: Another thing to watch is a statement called the Brussels Declaration due to land after the summit. EU countries have agreed on the text, and now it’s up to the Western Balkan governments to get on board. Expect a renewed emphasis on European values. “We expect partners to reject any glorification of war criminals, genocide denial and revisionism,” an EU official said, referring to the recent controversy over the funeral of Ratko Mladić, the former commander of the Bosnian Serb army and convicted war criminal.

Speaking of Serbia … its leadership won’t be there. It’s still trying to form a new government following parliamentary elections last month. European Council President António Costa has said he’d welcome new Serbian leaders to Brussels at the earliest opportunity.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The European Parliament’s plenary week in Strasbourg continues, with much of the work taking place in the Louise Weiss building … but who was she? Weiss was a French author, journalist, feminist campaigner and member of the European Parliament. She was 86 when she was voted in as part of Parliament’s first elections in 1979, and was still an MEP at the time of her death in 1983. The decision to name a building in her honor was made in 1998 and it was inaugurated a year after that.

CAIRO WANTS CREDIT

EGYPT WANTS MIGRATION RECOGNITION: The number of people seeking asylum has dropped in many European countries. But in Egypt the numbers have quadrupled, and the government in Cairo wants the EU to appreciate the effort it’s making.

“It’s important that our effort is recognized and seen, and that we’re also supported to continue to provide this protection,” Egypt’s Assistant Minister of Foreign Affairs Neveen Elhusseiny told Hanne Cokelaere.

Some context: One of the ways Brussels aims to reduce irregular migration is to partner with countries all along migratory routes: countries of origin, transit countries and countries of destination. Egypt is all three, Elhusseiny said.

Blind spots: The EU must engage in “burden sharing, not burden shifting,” the Egyptian minister said, and take note on the challenges of partner countries. The EU and Egypt signed a partnership in 2024 that includes funding for migration governance.

Mixed results: Egypt is “luckier in some areas than in others,” Elhusseiny said, adding that there is very good collaboration with Europe on issues such as border patrols and search and rescue missions. But when it comes to asylum and protection, “we hope we would have more support,” she added.

HEADLESS MENA: Michael Karnitschnig’s appointment as deputy director-general of DG ENEST has left staff at DG MENA wondering who’ll succeed him there as acting director-general. “Let the games begin,” said one official.

A PLEA FROM THE FORMERS: More than 80 European political figures, including four former European Parliament presidents and former PMs Enrico Letta and Guy Verhofstadt, are urging the EU in a letter today to do “whatever it takes” for Ukraine — and to put €210 billion in frozen Russian assets to work.

Also hitting commissioners’ mailboxes: MEPs from across the political spectrum have written to Ursula von der Leyen asking her to shield Europeans from the fallout from U.S. sanctions … lawmakers want the Commission to test whether plans for a deportation hub in Rwanda meet the EU’s human-rights standards, according to a letter to Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner and foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas … and nine countries are pushing to revise the bloc’s family reunification rules for recognized refugees and other non-EU nationals, arguing they’re putting increasing pressure on housing, education and public services, per a letter to Brunner seen by Nektaria Stamouli.

BRUSSELS CORNER

Metsola meant the stage, of course, but the irony wasn’t lost on the audience. “No, no, no,” Barley replied awkwardly, drawing laughter.

It’s Brussels’ Week of the Tree, with the city giving away fruit trees and shrubs to residents — apples, pears, figs, cherries, kiwis and more. Today’s giveaway is at Place Sainte-Catherine from noon to 2 p.m.

ORANGE IS THE NEW BRUSSELS: To mark the International Day for the Elimination of Violence Against Women, Manneken Pis is donning his “Orange the World” outfit, designed by Gioia Seghers and donated by UN Women. City Hall, La Monnaie and Brussels Central Station are also among the landmarks being lit up orange.

IT’S OK, BRUSSELS NEWBIES: If you’re spending your first Christmas season in the EU bubble and wondering why every reception invitation is for early December, don’t panic. The festive circuit is usually jammed between the November and December Strasbourg plenaries. This year, that’s just two weeks.

A Christmas party by any other name: Some are explicitly billed as Christmas parties, others are just events. Among the most eagerly awaited, kicking off next week, are Amazon and Spotify’s gatherings, as well as the traditional Lucia Day reception at the Swedish Residence, complete with its spectacular procession of white-robed singers led by Lucia (Saint Lucy) wearing a crown of candles.

The hottest tickets in town: One is the gala of the year: our POLITICO P28. Got a ticket? Consider yourself lucky. You can still register to watch it live here. The other comes courtesy of the Motion Picture Association, the film and television industry’s lobby, which is treating a select crowd of officials and bubblers to an exclusive private screening of “Dune: Part Three.” It’s happening on Dec. 7, 10 days before the movie hits cinemas. It’s all rather hush-hush … or it was, until you opened Playbook this morning.

Whatever you call your party … remember to invite at least one of us. Because like a tree falling in the forest, does an event really happen if it didn’t make Playbook’s “spotted” list? Speaking of which …
