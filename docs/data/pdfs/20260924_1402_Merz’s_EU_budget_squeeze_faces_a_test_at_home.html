DRIVING THE DAY

PUMPING THE BUDGET BRAKES: If European Council President António Costa can’t persuade Friedrich Merz to soften his push for budget cuts, maybe Germany’s state premiers can. That’s the hope in Brussels as the chancellor meets the powerful leaders of the Länder in Berlin today, I report with Gregorio Sorgi, Zoya Sheftalovich.

Pressure from Germany’s regions may be one of the few remaining checks on Merz’s drive to shrink the EU’s next seven-year budget, three officials familiar with the talks told Playbook, granted anonymity to speak frankly about the expectations. Merz is pushing to cut hundreds of billions of euros, complicating the European Council president’s effort to secure a deal by year-end — before a string of European elections makes a compromise even harder to achieve.

Germany’s state premiers want to preserve funding that flows directly to their regions, particularly EU cash for poorer areas and agricultural support — a position that may temper Merz’s hardline approach at the negotiating table. North Rhine-Westphalia, led by Hendrik Wüst — often touted as a future CDU leader — is calling to preserve “the strong role of the regions in cohesion policy and the use of European funds” in a statement seen by Playbook.

Merz’s meeting today with the premiers and Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil is “absolutely essential,” a senior German government official told POLITICO’s Nette Nöstlinger. He needs their backing, the official said, and the meeting should show him how much room he has to negotiate in Brussels. “Germany needs to step forward with a unified position.”

Merz is under pressure: The chancellor heads into the meeting after bruising regional elections in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. In the latter, Merz’s center-right CDU fell below the 5% threshold for entering parliament — a historic first for the party. Both are in eastern Germany, where public investment is seen as key to modernizing industry, cutting emissions and spurring growth.

“Many German regions understand this challenge very well,” Committee of the Regions President Kata Tüttő told Playbook. Asking them to manage that transformation with less EU funding risks “turning transition into divergence,” she said — in other words, widening the economic gap.

Don’t expect the Länder to declare war on Merz, one EU official warned. They want more cohesion money but are wary of weakening the chancellor’s hand in the wider budget talks. The fight is also about more than the cohesion cash, according to a second EU official. “It’s not about dividing the cake, but about the size of the cake.”

The calculation: Brussels will be watching whether Merz’s election setbacks harden his stance as he seeks a budget win to take home — or whether pressure from the Länder opens room for compromise at the Council table.

RUSSIA SANCTIONS

TWO OFF, TWO ON? The EU created a risky precedent by lifting sanctions against prominent Russian businessmen Alisher Usmanov and Mikhail Fridman, but it could win back credibility by sanctioning two other billionaires who have so far not been listed, Kyiv’s commissioner in charge of sanctions policy told Playbook.

Tale of two Vlads: Vladimir Lisin and Vladimir Potanin control big companies in the steel and nickel industries that are beneficial to Russia’s war economy. Their inclusion in the next batch of individual sanctions would effectively cut off their enterprises — NLMK and Nornickel — from trading in the EU, said Vladyslav Vlasiuk, who advises Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy on sanctions.

“Everyone is aware about these guys but avoiding any action against them,” Vlasiuk said. “But now arguably there is leverage to push this forward.”

Too big to sanction? The size of the businesses owned by Lisin and Potanin — and their substantial footprints in Europe — have so far kept them off the sanctions list. Lisin’s NLMK operates steel processing facilities in the Belgian region of Wallonia, leading the Belgian government to oppose his listing. The company also has production sites in Strasbourg, France; Verona, Italy; and Frederiksvaerk, Denmark.

As for Potanin, Europe’s ongoing dependency on Russian nickel and palladium, particularly in the car industry, has kept him off the list — enabling the billionaire to grow his empire during Russia’s war against Ukraine.

Too many exceptions: Vlasiuk linked the cases of Lisin and Potanin to the ongoing debate about whether to sanction Aughinish Alumina, an Irish exporter that supplies Russia. “It’s always the same story. You can have 20 countries for whom [sanctioning] is an obvious decision and one who opposes,” he said. “These alumina exports should really be banned. It’s obvious for us, and we have plenty of ideas for what to do next.”

A spokesperson for Lisin wrote to Playbook: “This information campaign is unfair competition, where the sanctions-related agenda is leveraged as a way of pressure to eliminate a competitor from the market and to inflict further substantial harm on the European economy. It is well established and has long been recognized that neither Mr. Lisin nor NLMK cooperate with the enterprises of the Russian military-industrial complex. NLMK manufactures exclusively civilian products.”

Representatives for Potanin did not immediately reply to a request for comment.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The big decision this week on Russia sanctions was taken at a Coreper meeting, but what is that? It’s the Committee of the Permanent Representatives, the main preparatory body for Council of the EU meetings, which means it’s where envoys try and reach compromises before ministers get together. There are two types of meeting: Coreper I is deputy permanent representatives and Coreper II is permanent representatives (that’s “permanent” because they are based in Brussels and “representative” as they represent national capitals. They have the status of ambassadors to the EU).

PROTECTING POLITICIANS

NOT SAFE TO SPEAK: Increasing threats, intimidation and violence against politicians, candidates and others who participate in public life are affecting democracies around the world, the EU’s Democracy Commissioner Michael McGrath will tell ministers and U.N. officials at the General Assembly in New York. The Commission is due to propose a recommendation on the safety of political representatives later this year.

Targeted: One of Ireland’s best-known politicians, Michael Healy-Rae, was assaulted in Dublin last week. Former British MEP and MP Ann Widdecombe was killed in her home in July. In Germany, MEP Matthias Ecke was badly beaten while putting up campaign posters in 2024. Slovakia’s Prime Minister Robert Fico survived an assassination attempt that year. The list also includes the murders of British MPs Jo Cox in 2016 and David Amess in 2021, and the 2025 assassination of former Ukrainian parliament speaker Andriy Parubiy.

“If people are afraid to stand for election or speak openly because of threats and abuse, our democracy is weakened,” McGrath said in a statement to Playbook. “People who choose to participate in political life must be able to do so safely and without intimidation.”

Court in the crosshairs: McGrath will also attend an International Criminal Court ministerial meeting and reaffirm the EU’s support for the court as Washington steps up pressure on it, including by imposing sanctions on court officials.

BRUSSELS’ SAUNA CIRCUIT: Estonia’s permanent representation is holding a briefing in its sauna tonight (swimwear required), Sebastian Starcevic writes, which got us thinking: where do EU officials and diplomats go to sweat it out?

Some like it hot: The Berlaymont headquarters of the European Commission has a sauna, which is for commissioners and officials by reservation only. Finland’s permanent representation also famously has a nice sauna, but an official told Playbook it’s undergoing renovations.

The Finnish ambassador’s residence is sauna-equipped, though, as is the Finnish Seamen’s Mission in Brussels, which can be accessed by the public. So can Animo’s sauna, and Aspria’s, with both gyms frequented by Eurocrats.

NIGHT TRAIN DIARIES: We’ve written plenty in this corner about Europe’s sleeper-train revival. Last night, POLITICO mobility reporter Martina Sapio put it to the test from Berlin to Brussels — and kept me entertained with running commentaries as I worked the Playbook night shift. It seems unfair not to share.

“We stopped at this station in the middle of nowhere,” read one late-night text. “Speakers say there’s a medical emergency on board and doctors are coming.” Martina and her cabinmates — a 24-year-old Interrailer embracing the adventure and a bona fide train aficionado — began wondering whether someone had overdone it at the bar.

It wasn’t the booze. A bed had apparently fallen on a train manager, colleagues told Martina during the unscheduled stop.

There were a few drawbacks: Rock-hard mattresses, bunks requiring a minor climbing expedition and a roughly 14-hour journey — versus 8 hours by car or 90 minutes in the air. Plenty of time to become intimately acquainted with both your carriage and the German rail network.

Still, there were compensations: Great service, no liquid limits and plenty of vintage charm. The trade-off: no morning shower and a journey that asks you to really commit to the romance of rail. By the time you read this, I’ll know whether Martina actually made it to Brussels. Fingers crossed.
