DRIVING THE DAY

UK-EU TALKS ON ‘MADE IN EUROPE’ KICK OFF: Hamish Falconer, the U.K.’s new EU minister under Prime Minister Andy Burnham, is meeting with European Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič today to secure concessions on the bloc’s “Made in Europe” procurement policy — pushing Brussels to include the U.K. in the scheme.

All about cars: The Industrial Accelerator Act sets European preference requirements for public procurement in critical sectors and clean technology, including electric vehicles. Most important for the U.K. and its auto sector are the rules surrounding corporate fleets. Under the currently written rules, vehicles assembled in the U.K. would not qualify for public support and incentives tied to corporate-fleet electrification.

Scale of the problem: Nearly 60 percent of the U.K.’s automotive exports are destined for the EU, and corporate fleets make up the majority of car sales. Under the European Commission’s proposed auto package put forward in December 2025, member states have binding electrification targets for corporate fleets. While having mandatory targets is a point of contention in negotiations, it’s a market that automakers with U.K. manufacturing cannot afford to lose.

The worst-case scenario, the U.K. car lobby SMMT says, is that zero-emission cars produced in the U.K. would not be eligible for the public funding toward corporate fleets or be ineligible for the emission supercredits the Commission wants to give automakers that produce small, affordable electric vehicles in the EU.

Some help: Burnham and French President Emmanuel Macron agreed on Sept. 3 to “work together” to protect U.K. and French interests amid growing concerns over the IAA.

Next up: The meeting takes place ahead of a planned EU-U.K. summit in November — where negotiators are expected to tackle other unresolved issues and continue negotiations over the IAA.

MARITIME

IMO NARROWS THE FIELD: The final draft report from this week’s shipping decarbonization talks at the International Maritime Organization, obtained by Martina, shows where negotiators managed to narrow the options — and where they left the fight unresolved.

Background: The document says there is general support for the basic system measuring ships’ fuel-emissions intensity and for keeping flexibility mechanisms in the framework. But countries remain split over mandatory payments and the future of a central IMO fund.

Softer start: One possible compromise is to ease emissions cuts in the early years, then tighten them later while keeping the overall trajectory aligned with the IMO’s 2023 climate strategy. Periodic reviews and checks on whether cleaner fuels are actually available are also on the tables, while during discussions countries showed interest in giving ships extra credit for using zero- and near-zero-emission fuels.

Where there is agreement: The report says there was broad support for ships cutting emissions onboard or using surplus units generated elsewhere in a fleet, including through pooling. There was also considerable support for using surplus units to cover compliance deficits, while China’s idea of a multiplier for cleaner fuels, which would give ships extra compliance credit for using the cleanest fuels — effectively rewarding them without relying entirely on cash payments from a central IMO fund — stays on the table for further work.

Where there isn’t: The financial side remains the real fault line. Some countries want to retain the Net-Zero Fund and mandatory revenues; others oppose financial contributions altogether or want lighter alternatives. Japan’s idea of direct contributions to approved projects also attracted interest, including from the U.S., though governments raised questions over governance, transparency and whether that model could provide enough predictable support.

Industry says progress: World Shipping Council Vice President for Environment and Climate Simon Bergulf said the meeting “achieved what it needed to do,” arguing it narrowed the options and put the next round of talks at the end of November on clearer footing. But he warned that delay adds cost and uncertainty after years of investment in dual-fuel ships and alternative-fuel infrastructure: “The industry urgently needs a meaningful global agreement at the IMO.”

Greens less impressed: Green NGO Transport & Environment said the talks had again failed to land a deal, while Clean Shipping Coalition President Lukas Leppert argued the existing framework “remains intact” and should be adopted without further delay. Opportunity Green’s Em Fenton said countries showing compromise “does not mean abandoning the principle of justice,” while Pacific Environment’s Jamie Yates said there had been “no clear majority shift” toward alternative proposals.

Clock ticking: The report acknowledges that “a number of issues remained unresolved” and urges governments to use the intersessional period to develop concrete compromise proposals. Talks resume Nov. 23-27, followed by the IMO’s main environment committee from Nov. 30 to Dec. 3 and a planned extraordinary meeting on Dec. 4 — leaving only months to turn a shorter list of options into a deal.

GREEN SHIPPING CAUTION: MPC Oceanic Group, which charters to major carriers, says it is pulling back from riskier green-fuel investments as uncertainty over IMO rules persists, while continuing to spend on retrofits and fleet modernization. “We are quite cautious now,” Sustainability Director Sebastian Ebbing was reported as saying. He expects the IMO’s net-zero framework to emerge without a strong carbon-pricing mechanism.

PORTS

INDIA TRADE HITS THE DOCKS: The Port of Antwerp-Bruges is already preparing for a jump in EU-India trade, banking on Prime Minister Bart De Wever’s visit to Mumbai to court Indian businesses, deepen port ties and position itself as the main European gateway for cargo showing results once the new trade deal takes effect.

What’s changing: The EU-India free-trade agreement is set to cut or remove tariffs on a wide range of goods and simplify customs procedures, potentially making it cheaper and easier to move products between the two markets. The European Commission estimates EU goods exports to India, worth nearly €49 billion in 2024, could double by 2032, with chemicals, pharmaceuticals, machinery, textiles and food among the sectors expected to benefit most.

Antwerp’s pitch: Antwerp-Bruges has had a permanent presence in Mumbai since 2006 and is now stepping up business outreach, training and investment cooperation with India’s port and maritime sector. It is also planning another mission in February 2027 with companies from the port community, aimed at turning the trade deal into logistics, investment and commercial partnerships.

CARS

FRENCH-GERMAN DÉTENTE: French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin told our colleague Giorgio Leali that the ministry plans on finalizing the Franco-German industrial package by the end of the month. “We are making progress,” he said when asked about the status of the negotiations on Friday at an opening of an impressionist museum in Giverny, northwest of Paris.

Joining forces: The two countries plan to present the joint position at the next Council meeting in mid-October on issues like the emissions standards for automakers, trade relations with China and local content requirements under the IAA.

HIGHER MANUFACTURING TARGET: EU member countries want to increase the IAA’s proposed industrial target, according to a revised version of the IAA obtained by POLITICO. The Commission proposed raising manufacturing to 20 percent of EU gross domestic product by 2035. The latest compromise text would raise that figure to 30 percent.

What’s next: The proposal will be discussed at Wednesday’s competition working group meeting.

Not an easy task: Germany’s Bundestag is back in session today and the country’s industrial groups are warning lawmakers that the EU is no longer attractive for business. “The fact is that European industry can no longer grow given the poor conditions for business,” said Hildegard Müller, the president of German auto lobby VDA.

Looking elsewhere: Companies are relocating “in order to remain competitive internationally,” she added. The comments come on the heels of the Saxony-Anhalt election, where the far-right AfD is on the cusp of holding a position of genuine power for the first time, and Volkswagen’s board agreeing to cutting 50,000 jobs and shutter up to four factories in Germany.

Bold action needed: Policymakers are squabbling over “mini reforms” like the gradual reduction of corporate taxes or reforming the pension system rather than taking broader measures to support industry, the groups say. Instead, they should take on a “growth agenda,” Müller said.

PIT STOPS

Major automakers, including Volkswagen and Stellantis, are calling on American lawmakers to bar Chinese vehicles from the U.S. market by the end of the year. Reuters has more.
