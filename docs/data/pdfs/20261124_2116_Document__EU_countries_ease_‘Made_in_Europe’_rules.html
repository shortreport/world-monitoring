Document: EU countries ease ‘Made in Europe’ rules for carmakers
By Jordyn Dahl, Francesca Micheletti · Nov 24, 2026, 1:15 PM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — European Union countries have opened the door for vehicles and components made outside the bloc to qualify as Made in Europe under the latest compromise text from the Irish Council presidency on the Industrial Accelerator Act.

The European Commission originally proposed that only vehicles assembled in the 27 EU member countries should qualify for public procurement and other public support schemes.

The latest text, obtained by POLITICO, would extend eligibility to vehicles assembled in Switzerland, the United Kingdom and the three non-EU members of the European Economic Area — Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway.

The EU institutions are separately negotiating a broader Automotive Package that would ease the bloc’s de facto 2035 ban on combustion engines by revising emissions targets for automakers. The two pieces of legislation are closely linked through their Made in Europe requirements.

A Franco-German compromise deal reached in October proposed moving the automotive provisions out of the IAA and into the Automotive Package. EU countries rejected that approach, with the latest presidency draft instead giving the industry its own chapter in the IAA.

“Given the industry’s significant contribution to employment, innovation and exports … it warrants specific measures reflecting its strategic importance to the Union’s industrial base, economic security and technological sovereignty,” the text reads.

Emission supercredits

The Commission wants to create a market for small, affordable electric vehicles and is using the Automotive Package to achieve that aim by giving automakers ‘supercredits’ to use toward their broader CO2 emission reduction targets.

Under the Commission’s proposal, every 10 small EVs sold would count as 13 for the purposes of calculating fleet emissions. The latest Irish presidency compromise — scooped by POLITICO earlier this week — would introduce an additional multiplier of 1.1 for electric vehicles of any size, provided they are Made in Europe.

Unlike the rules governing public procurement and support, however, the IAA compromise would restrict eligibility for supercredits to cars produced in the EU’s 27 member countries.

National officials will meet Wednesday to continue negotiations on the Automotive Package at working party level.

Component requirements

In a win for South Korea and Japan, vehicle components sourced from those two countries could count as Made in Europe for the purposes of public funding.

The latest draft also gives automakers more leeway in meeting local content requirements.

If at least 75% of a carmaker’s vehicles met those requirements in the previous year, all of its vehicles otherwise eligible for public funding would be deemed compliant for the next 12 months. The previous threshold was 85%.

That threshold would drop further, to 70%, for automakers that spend at least half of their research and development budget in the EU — a concession to an industry that has lobbied for its investments in innovation to count toward Buy European requirements.

In another win for the industry, the latest text eases sourcing requirements for battery components and gives carmakers more time to comply.

The Commission proposal required three battery components, including battery cells, be sourced from the EU six months after the IAA comes into force. Those requirements would increase to five components after three years.

The Irish presidency text would require two components be sourced from the EU 18 months after the regulation comes into effect, rising to four after four years.

Chemicals and FDI

On chemicals, the possibility for the Commission to introduce demand-side measures through a delegated act has been removed. The Council presidency instead proposes that the Commission should give particular consideration to this during the review of the regulation.

The text also clarifies that new conditions on foreign direct investment would not apply to investments made before the first 12 months following the regulation’s entry into force have elapsed. The criteria for granting exemptions are further tightened.

The Irish presidency has scheduled a political debate on the file for Dec. 3, when EU industry ministers are due to meet in Brussels, with a view to finalizing a negotiating position before the end of the year.
