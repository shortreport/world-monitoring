DRIVING THE DAY

BATTLE LINES: EU governments are gearing up for a fight over the Commission’s plans to strengthen security across the continent, with capitals bristling at the prospect of any kind of power grab by Brussels.

Action stations: Commission President Ursula von der Leyen is weighing new tools to protect against sabotage and cyberattacks — a key priority for her remaining years in office. But capitals that already have world-leading security and defense institutions, like Paris and Berlin, are resistant to any overreach from the EU, five diplomats involved in the talks told Playbook.

Incoming: The first test comes on Wednesday when the EU executive is expected to put ideas to tackle hybrid threats to ambassadors, with a separate Security Strategy to follow by the end of October. “It’s hard to see France and Germany wanting the Commission to have more power over security,” said an envoy from a different country, granted anonymity to speak frankly.

Wednesday’s discussion comes as Europe searches for ways to tackle the hacking, migration weaponization and explosive drones that have heightened security concerns in recent months. It’s designed to help the EU fill in gaps currently left between NATO and national governments, according to two diplomats and an official briefed on the contents. The upcoming Security Strategy, meanwhile, is expected to label Russia as the main threat to EU security, another three diplomats told Jacopo Barigazzi.

So, what do we do about it? Despite the skepticism from some of their neighbors, smaller countries — particularly those that share borders with Russia — support a far-reaching security overhaul, provided it comes with new money. Latvia, Lithuania and Estonia just this week requested around half a billion euros to strengthen their resilience.

Stay in your lane: “Ultimately, each EU member state decides for itself what contribution it makes in the field of defense,” said Marie-Agnes Strack-Zimmermann, the German MEP who chairs the Parliament’s defense committee. “Nevertheless, a coordinated approach on the part of the EU would be helpful.”

A second diplomat put it more bluntly: “If the EU sticks to its competencies, we will have no problems.” The diplomat said the Commission should have a role supporting innovation, AI and space development, while military needs and operational planning fall under the remit of national governments.

Finding the gaps: “Where do we already have the power in the EU?” is what Brussels should be asking, according to Tobias Cremer, lead EU lawmaker on the common defense market and a member of the Parliament’s NATO delegation. “Where do we have real added value … where we can quickly make a real difference?”

“You work with people on defense, you don’t tell them how to defend themselves,” said a senior EU official, who argued the Commission would earn the support of capitals. “If threats fall under NATO, we have no role. But if they fall under member state competencies, there’s more of [a role] for the EU. It’s [also] about threat detection. If Estonia sees something coming over the border, there should be a system so everyone finds out rapidly, rather than everyone finding out from POLITICO.”

BERLAYMONT’S AI BRAIN

FLIP THE SCRIPT: The EU should focus less on building the world’s best AI models and more on putting the technology to work across Europe’s economy, the EU’s special envoy for industrial AI urged commissioners at their seminar earlier this month in Tervuren, two Commission officials told Zoya Sheftalovich.

The U.S. and China may be ahead in developing models such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek, but Europe can still use AI to create value, Jim Hagemann Snabe said, according to the officials who were granted anonymity to discuss the confidential meeting.

The game plan: Snabe’s team will spend July through December doing “deep work” on AI in different sectors, or “verticals,” and on the technology stack — the models, chips and computing capacity underpinning AI. The work covers both how AI is used and the infrastructure needed to support it.

The team will then draft commitments and shape a policy package toward the end of the year, before examining AI’s societal effects from January to March 2027 as it prepares a final report and strategy. Its focus will be industrial AI, adoption and strengthening the tech stack.

Snabe’s presentation followed a separate seminar a week earlier, on Aug. 28, for the commissioners’ heads of cabinet, delivered by a Commission AI expert. Artificial intelligence is “our best bet to re-accelerate productivity growth,” the top official told the gathering, according to a copy of the presentation seen by Zoya. The EU’s approach should have a “strong bias for action and rapid impact,” per the document.

Some of the use cases: Better disease diagnosis and faster treatment, as well as more efficient production, transport and public administration, the expert said. The aim is to shift attention from whether Europe can build the world’s leading models to where AI can deliver practical gains across industries and government.

The big questions: Commissioners and their cabinet chiefs should consider what AI can make possible in their policy areas that was previously too costly or out of reach — and which assumptions no longer hold if cognitive work becomes dramatically cheaper, the experts said.

Risks vs. benefits: The push to harness AI comes amid rising alarm that it could escape human control and threaten humanity. At the U.N. General Assembly in New York overnight, Australian PM Anthony Albanese warned of the “furious pace” of development and urged world leaders to erect more guardrails. Donald Trump will meet top executives from the leading AI companies at the White House Tuesday (after they attended last night’s State Dinner in honor of Xi Jinping), my stateside colleagues report.

The sudden rush of political activity reflects wider unease: In a POLITICO Poll, carried out by Public First, majorities in France, Germany, Spain, the U.K., Canada and the U.S. said there was at least a “moderate” risk of annihilation, Hanne Cokelaere writes.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The U.S. is said to be preparing a 90-day ban on exports of diesel. How big a problem would that create for Europe? In August, the U.S. supplied 420,000 barrels of diesel to EU member countries and the U.K. — that’s more than half of total imports that month, according to data platform Kpler. Germany alone last month imported 30,000 barrels of diesel from the U.S. Since the war in Iran began, diesel imports from Qatar have dried up, according to Kpler, with imports from Kuwait, Oman and the United Arab Emirates also falling. That’s increased reliance on the U.S.

PROTECTING TREES IS A DIRTY JOB

WOOD YOU BELIEVE IT: The U.S. has a secret weapon in its long-standing bid to shoot down the EU’s controversial Deforestation Regulation — diapers.

Baby bills: Washington’s ambassador to the bloc, Andrew Puzder, is warning counterparts on the continent that their infants’ absorbent undergarments will be getting more expensive as a result of the EUDR (as the regulation is known), which obliges manufacturers to trace the origin of wood pulp back to the source. That’s as cost of living concerns drive discontent at the ballot box.

“EUDR will increase consumer prices without reducing deforestation when inputs — such as wood pulp used in diapers and other personal hygiene products — become more expensive from countries like the U.S. which pose no deforestation risk,” Puzder told Playbook.

Party poopers: It’s not just the U.S. that hates the green rules. Canada, Malaysia and at least five other non-EU countries are fiercely campaigning for the policy to be dropped or pared back. Canada’s Ambassador Jonathan Wilkinson told POLITICO that, despite his country’s rigorous forestry practices, it will still face “all of these burdensome requirements in terms of geolocation … that actually cost enormous amounts of money.”

Pushing back: “The EUDR is about protecting forests … It is not about making diapers more expensive for European families,” said Commission spokesperson Anna-Kaisa Itkonen. “Since the regulation entered into force in June 2023, the Commission has worked closely with industry and stakeholders to keep implementation practical, proportionate and cost-efficient, while minimizing compliance costs.”

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Sunny and warm. High of 24C.

THE MOST MYSTERIOUS JOB IN TOWN: A new role is on offer for those wanting to advise a foreign government. Which government? That’s none of your business.

Apply first, ask questions later: A job ad for a trade policy adviser at an unnamed Brussels diplomatic mission has caught the bubble’s attention. One curious Playbook reader asked who was hiring — only to be told the mission had specifically requested anonymity. “I’m unfortunately not able to share which government or diplomatic mission is behind this vacancy,” came the reply, seen by Playbook.

Nothing to see here: Belgian recruitment rules require that information is given about the job, but don’t expressly say the employer has to identify itself. That leaves Brussels’ aspiring trade wonks with an unusual proposition: Hand over your CV, career history and expertise to an unidentified foreign government — and perhaps find out who it is later. What could possibly go wrong? Deadline for applications, if you’re brave enough, is Oct. 15.

SPEAKING OF JOBS: It’s time for Playbook’s monthly roundup of weird vacancies — your regular reminder that EU careers are considerably stranger than their reputation suggests.

To infinity and beyond: The EU Agency for the Space Programme is looking for a space market officer in Prague. Apparently the EU space market is significant enough that somebody gets to have this as their actual job title. Applications close Oct. 20.

Are we human or are we officials? In Cologne, the EU Aviation Safety Agency wants a human factors expert — essentially someone paid to worry about how humans interact with cockpits, controls and increasingly clever aircraft. Deadline: Oct. 19.

Chair Force One: Playbook’s favorite is the Commission’s hunt in Luxembourg for a team leader for “Moves, Inventory and Furniture.” Translation: When Eurocrats change floors, somebody has to make sure the desks, chairs and cupboards arrive with them — and apparently lead a team doing it. Hurry as applications close Sept. 28.

Meanwhile, at the EEAS: The diplomatic service is recruiting a space threat response duty officer — difficult to read without hearing dramatic movie-trailer music — and a head of intelligence hub in Ukraine.

BRUSSELS ON A BUDGET: For many, payday hasn’t arrived yet. So here’s Playbook’s guide to a (nearly) zero-budget Brussels weekend:

Market therapy: The Marolles street market runs Saturday and Sunday. Free to browse, plenty of atmosphere and cheap-ish food if temptation strikes.

Design for free: “Curated” at Mix Brussels runs Friday through Sunday inside the former Royale Belge building, showcasing collectible design. Entry is free; brunch and cocktails are available for those willing to break the spending freeze.

Plant, don’t spend: The “Jardins de l’Hippodrome” festival brings plants, workshops and forest walks to Boitsfort this weekend. Entry is free. Buying a plant isn’t.
