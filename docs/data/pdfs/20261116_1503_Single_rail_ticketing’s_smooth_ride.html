DRIVING THE DAY

SINGLE TICKETING’S SMOOTH RIDE: The European Parliament has only just started work on the EU’s single rail ticketing proposal, but lead lawmaker Kai Tegethoff says a file once expected to trigger a major fight between incumbent railways and independent platforms is proving less controversial than expected.

Something has shifted: The German Greens MEP said attitudes have changed significantly over the past two years, with growing recognition that easier ticketing is the missing piece after years of EU efforts to open up rail markets. “There is surprisingly no fundamental opposition at the horizon,” he said.

Future-proof it: Tegethoff said the rules should not only focus on platforms owned or vertically integrated with big rail operators. His view is that, from a passenger’s perspective, it should not matter who owns a dominant platform: If an independent ticketing service later gains major market power, the legislation should be able to deal with that too.

The market fight: Tegethoff said that running trains and selling tickets should be treated as two distinct markets. Big incumbent operators, he argued, have historically enjoyed powerful mutually reinforcing positions in both areas. The goal now is to give third-party ticket sellers a “fair chance” to compete while preserving a system in which operators, platforms and passengers can all make the model work.

Three files, one package: That is also why Tegethoff wants the rail ticketing proposal to move together with the other parts of the Passenger Rights Package.

Parliament first: Tegethoff said the three Parliament rapporteurs are already broadly aligned despite coming from different political groups, and he expects amendments from the groups in December. His target is to begin compromise talks in January and reach a committee position by March. He expects the more difficult negotiations to come later with member countries, whose national rail systems and interests differ much more sharply.

What to watch: The Parliament amendments are expected to broaden the rules so that independent ticketing platforms with significant market power could also fall under tougher obligations, according to one person close to negotiations. They will also make a clearer distinction between merely displaying a connection and actually allowing passengers to book it.

And more: The amendments are also expected to strengthen EU-level enforcement, include more urban and suburban trains under the rules, and require a review after three years that could pave the way for extending the rules to aviation, buses, coaches and waterborne transport. They would also shorten the timetable for commercial agreements between operators and ticketing platforms.

CARS

LATEST IAA COMPROMISE TEXT: The Irish Council presidency is expected to unveil its fourth compromise text on the Industrial Accelerator Act today. This should pave the way for another technical level discussion on Wednesday, with the goal of coming to a final negotiating position by the end of the month.

Status update: Country positions on “Made in Europe” remained fairly stable during the last Coreper I discussion, a person briefed on the talks told Francesca Micheletti. Previous readouts seen by POLITICO revealed three camps, ranging from those backing a narrower scope to those in favor of softer reciprocity requirements. Catch up on that here.

Disappointment: The Franco-German compromise deal was supposed to unlock discussions, but a diplomat aware of the talks told Jordyn that the proposal does not have a qualified majority in Council. Beyond setting stricter Made in Europe requirements for the automotive industry, the deal proposed taking out the automotive elements from the IAA and instead negotiating them as part of a broader Automotive Package that includes reformed emissions targets.

What it means for auto: Neither Council nor Parliament back the idea. While MEPs reached a draft position on the Automotive Package, a plenary vote will not be held until the IAA is negotiated. As we’ve reported, the IAA is likely to be delayed until next year, which would similarly drag the emissions target reforms into 2027.

Recycling opportunity: The industrial policy is meant to jumpstart Europe’s battery sector, and industry czar Stéphane Séjourné wants that to include recycling. The IAA includes critical raw material requirements for European batteries, which could “very well come from recycling,” he told our colleagues Fiona McNally and Jakob Weizman during an interview at the opening of a new battery recycling facility by French utility giant Veolia.

Meanwhile in Parliament: There is a lot of IAA action this week. Today, technical conversations are slotted in, while a new meeting of shadow MEPs is scheduled for Wednesday. MEPs from the European People’s Party have yet to express a unified position on Made in Europe, with the three shadows each coming up with a different idea, according to a readout of last week’s meeting.

GERMAN INTELLIGENCE OFFICIAL WARNS OF TESLA SPYING RISKS: State intelligence chief Stephan Kramer warned that connected vehicles from American manufacturers, such as Tesla, should face the same security scrutiny as Chinese cars. The Bundeswehr has already restricted such vehicles at sensitive military sites over fears their sensors could collect information.

AVIATION

LUFTHANSA’S TAP DEAL FACES SCRUTINY AFTER ITA BATTLE: Lufthansa Group’s attempt to buy a stake in Portuguese flag carrier TAP is expected to face opposition from rival airlines.

Time flies: A month has passed since Lufthansa won the race against Air France-KLM to buy a 44.9% stake in the Portuguese flag carrier. The German group and Lisbon’s government are now expected to seek EU approval.

A second deal: Lufthansa also faced opposition over taking a stake in ITA Airways, which was approved by the Commission in 2024. The German carrier is now seeking to raise its share in ITA from 41% to 90%, which will require further approval from Brussels. Rival airlines want the Commission to scrutinize both deals more closely than it did the first time, Jacob Parry reports. Rivals see ITA as “the blueprint” for TAP. Lufthansa “is trying to buy an additional home market,” said a lawyer for a rival airline.

Questioning the Commission: The Commission’s approval is being challenged before the EU’s General Court, including by Luxair, which argues that smaller carriers were excluded from slot remedies. The lawyer told Jacob that it is, “A political decision at the end of the day.”

No worries: Lufthansa’s CEO recently told reporters he saw only “ fairly ⁠limited” overlaps between his group and TAP. If such assessment is confirmed by the Commission, this could make the approval process easier and faster than the in-depth ITA review.

Not that fast: According to experts, the approval is unlikely to be a formality. “Compared with ITA, the assessment could be more complex given TAP’s distinct hub position in Lisbon and its strong transatlantic network,” said Linus Benjamin Bauer, an aviation consultant at BAA & Partners.

Germany to the world: Lufthansa Group airlines and TAP are the only nonstop operators on routes linking Lisbon with Frankfurt, Munich, Brussels, Düsseldorf and Hamburg. Together they account for over half of departures at Lisbon Airport.

Latin American gateway: “TAP’s strategic importance as a gateway to Brazil and other transatlantic markets will also be relevant,” Bauer said. “I would not rule out a lengthy pre-notification process,” he added, referring to discussions preceding the formal notification of the deal to the Commission.

Lufthansa told us on Friday that the group and the Portuguese government “will jointly assess merger control filing requirements,” but did not comment on the timing of those steps.

TRAN COMPROMISE ON SUSTAINABLE FUELS: The Commission’s proposal to revise the Emissions Trading System would allocate allowances to support the use of sustainable aviation and maritime fuels produced either in the European Economic Area or in third countries with carbon pricing systems linked to the EU ETS. That restriction could be overturned by TRAN MEPs, according to draft compromise amendments seen by Tommaso.

Extra help: Political groups in TRAN — which is not the lead committee on this file, a role held by ENVI — have agreed on a compromise on the issue: Sustainable fuels produced in Europe would be eligible for an additional 5% in ETS support, with a further 5% for fuels produced using feedstocks sourced in the EEA.

Producers unhappy: The proposal, which has yet to be adopted by the committee, is worrying European sustainable fuel producers. A letter, signed by 14 companies in the sector earlier this month and seen by Tommaso, warned MEPs on the TRAN, ITRE and ENVI committees against the risk of providing support to sustainable fuels produced in third countries — particularly China. The letter stresses that fuel production is increasingly seen as a strategic asset amid supply uncertainty.
