US remote-access rules pose structural threat to China’s AI progress
23 Nov 2026 08:12 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 

summary
  • The US Commerce Department’s new rule restricting remote access to advanced semiconductors at overseas data centers will leave Chinese AI labs facing an inevitable performance lag.
  • EG’s base case remains US-China stability after the APEC summit, but the risk of a breakdown is higher than before as export control bills advance.

the top line
China, US, Geo-technology—Remote-access controls pose structural threat to China’s AI progress
  • The US Commerce Department’s new rule restricting remote access to advanced semiconductors at overseas data centers directly targets Chinese AI labs’ primary workaround for chip controls, cutting off more than half of their training compute.
  • US-China negotiations may produce a temporary suspension of the rule, but Chinese firms’ structural shift away from Nvidia-dependent supply chains has started in earnest, as evidenced by Chinese AI lab Moonshot’s cancellation of a $150 million Indonesian data center deal.
  • Chinese semiconductor companies stand to benefit from the forced localization of AI supply chains. But Chinese AI labs face an inevitable performance lag as domestic alternatives like Huawei’s Ascend chips are unlikely to close the compute gap within a few years.

EG Update—Ian Bremmer on the APEC summit
  • The structural misalignment between presidents Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin, and Donald Trump ensured nothing much could be agreed to, any deliverables would face challenging execution, and the trilateral itself would not prove a durable format.
  • Shenzhen allowed Putin to deliver a message to other countries who refuse to engage with him (most of America's allies) that persisting in that effort would prove costly and, in the near term at least, unsuccessful.
  • In Trump's worldview, he, Xi and Putin (in that order) are the leaders that should be getting together to make decisions involving their core interests and spheres of influence. And if that can't happen in terms of policy, at least the optics should show everyone else that these are the leaders that matter.
  • Xi had little to gain… There was no effort by Xi to insert himself in war negotiations (in Ukraine or Iran) that aren't going anywhere, and he's certainly not interested in deploying leverage over Putin on Trump's behalf.

For more on the summit and Ian’s latest views on Russia-Ukraine and India-US ties, see this week’s EG Update.
US, China, Japan, Geo-technology—Export control push raises risk of a US-China breakdown after APEC
  • Following the APEC summit, EG's base case remains US-China stability, with the Busan trade truce expected to be extended eventually. The probability of a breakdown, though, is higher than before. The anticipated AI dialogue did not occur given Chinese objections to the new US remote-access rule.
  • Three export control bills are very likely to be included in the National Defense Authorization Act. The Remote Access Security Act, which would legislatively extend export controls to foreign remote and cloud access, is also now a genuine possibility. The White House will retain enforcement discretion, but corporate compliance is already running ahead of enforcement, and the next president will likely take a more aggressive posture.
  • China is expected to respond in graduated steps, potentially including rare earth export slowdowns and merger interference.
  • Japan was sidelined at the summit. Curtailing rare earth flows to Japan would hit US companies that depend on Japanese-made magnets.

South Africa—ActionSA’s pivot to ANC raises odds of GNU failure
  • EG's basecase immediately after the local elections was constructive. The results indicated a sustained shift of voter sentiment away from poor governance and populist parties generally, represented by the robust showing in Gauteng by the Democratic Alliance (DA) and ActionSA.
  • However, ActionSA is now leveraging its Gauteng election results to negotiate municipal government roles with the African National Congress and coalition, which would require support from populist parties to secure majorities.
  • This development raises the odds of a breakup of the current Government of National Unity (GNU) from 20% to 55% as the internal succession race for the ANC presidency accelerates and the DA is pushed to reconsider its position in the GNU.
  • Fiscal, monetary, and economic growth targets are at risk. The Johannesburg economy, broader business confidence, and the currency will weaken further, driving up bond yields.

France—Le Pen's odds of winning the presidency rise to 45% from 40% as Philippe falters
  • EG is increasing the odds of far-right Marine Le Pen winning the presidency to 45% from 40% on a slight strengthening in the polls despite the dismissive reception for her unrealistic economic program.
  • Philippe's odds of securing the presidency have fallen to 30% from 35% as his message of gradual reforms struggles to resonate amid strong backlash against President Emmanuel Macron.
  • Former Olympic judo champion Teddy Riner's bid and Cannes Mayor David Lisnard's rise add to an already crowded race in the center, but a single, mainstream champion remains likely to emerge in the new year.
  • Far-left candidate Jean-Luc Melenchon's rise is more fragile than it seems (10% odds), and Philippe, though wounded, is still the most likely candidate to prevent an "all extremes" Le Pen-Melenchon runoff in May (20% odds).

eg in brief

US—The Trump-Vance split is widening: Vice President JD Vance’s X post on 19 November decrying some Republicans’ calls for “bipartisan immigration reform” in the next Congress is the latest instance of him publicly breaking with Trump on a key issue. Vance has begun to lay out his distinct political brand for a potential 2028 presidential run: more skeptical of foreign involvement and more favorable to negotiation with Iran to end the conflict, but more hardline on certain elements of domestic policy, especially immigration. If Vance were the nominee, he would probably be to Trump’s right. Trump has been tolerant of Vance’s actions so far, but his frustrations appear to be mounting. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: unitedstates@eurasiagroup.net.

US, Turkey—Reset running out of time to deliver: The recent improvement in relations between Turkey and the US rests on personal ties between the countries’ leaders, leaving its durability beyond Trump’s final term in doubt. Washington has reaped substantial commercial gains from the thaw, while Ankara has secured relatively little, with the window for further concessions narrowing as Trump’s presidency draws to a close. Turkish disappointment could imperil the expanded commercial relationship through heightened regulatory and political risks. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: mena@eurasiagroup.net.

Mexico—Fed-Banxico divergence will expose peso to external shocks: Banxico’s meeting minutes show that its dovish majority, led by Governor Victoria Rodriguez, remains willing to follow a monetary path that increasingly prioritizes countering low growth. The Mexican peso is now 5% weaker year to date, with its performance approaching that of the South African rand and Brazilian real. Concerns about government stability in South Africa and the fiscal outlook in Brazil after President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s reelection have risen sharply over the past month. The peso’s weak performance shows how a narrowing interest-rate differential with the US is increasing the currency's vulnerability to external shocks despite Mexico’s more stable political outlook. Likely further Fed tightening in December and early next year, alongside additional Banxico cuts, would narrow the interest-rate differential and increase pressure on the peso. Deputy Governor Jonathan Heath’s departure at year-end will further strengthen the dovish bloc, but it will not remove market constraints on future easing. Read more here and here. Reach out to discuss: mexico@eurasiagroup.net.

Colombia—Protests are unlikely to derail the fiscal bill: Opposition groups, including former President Gustavo Petro, took to the streets in Bogota and other cities on 19 November to protest President Abelardo De la Espriella’s fiscal adjustment bill. De la Espriella said 55,000 people participated, well below the scale of the 2021 demonstrations, which derailed the tax bill that then-president Ivan Duque proposed. The bill should clear its first committee vote in the first week of December to allow a final vote in plenaries before the session ends on 16 December. De la Espriella has enough votes in congress to pass most of the package. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: latinamerica@eurasiagroup.net.
