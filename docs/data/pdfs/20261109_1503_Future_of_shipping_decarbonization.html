DRIVING THE DAY

WHAT’S COOKING ON SHIPPING CARBON PRICES: A Council working party today discusses how Europe’s shipping carbon price should handle a global emissions deal, while at the International Maritime Organization a so-called “Risotto Group” explores a path to one.

EU effort on emissions: With no agreed position yet, the working party discussion is set to talk about dialing back the shipping ETS if the IMO adopts a global framework, according to an EU official.

The IMO clause: Under the latest ETS compromise text from the Irish Council presidency obtained by POLITICO, the Commission would report within 18 months of the IMO adopting a global measure, and before it becomes operational. Shipping companies would owe no EU allowances for emissions already effectively priced under the IMO scheme at a level equal to or above the average ETS price, and would pay proportionally less if it is lower.

Also in the text: The draft sets aside 0.9 million allowances a year until 2035 for least developed countries and small island states. It also keeps a reserve of 110 million allowance to help cover the extra cost of sustainable fuels and zero-emission propulsion.

Italian inspiration: Europe’s moves are being closely watched at the IMO, where a meeting later this month will address the hotly debated, U.S.-opposed net-zero framework for decarbonizing shipping. A so-called “Risotto Group,” an intersessional forum of about 25 member states representing every side of the debate, is now driving the conversation, according to two people familiar with the negotiations who spoke to Martina. For the past year, appetite for a deal at the IMO has partly depended on how the EU ETS, a regional measure, will work with the global approach, a point the group is also touching on.

The calendar: Most expect only progress toward a new draft at the next IMO meeting rather than a final deal. If everything comes together, a framework could be adopted by October 2027, enter into force in March 2029 and take effect in January 2030.

Still no meeting of minds: Countries are split over how to make shipping pay for its emissions. The original proposal, which the EU backs, would limit the emissions intensity of ships’ fuel; ships that exceed it would pay into a fund that rewards cleaner fuels and helps developing countries. Several alternatives are on the table. Japan wants countries and companies to contribute money voluntarily, with no central fund. Norway wants a version that does not need a dedicated pot of money. The U.S., backed by other petrostates such as Saudi Arabia and Russia, has repeatedly said it will not accept a global carbon tax on shipping.

AVIATION

BOMB THREATS AGAINST AIRLINES SPARK FEARS AHEAD OF SUMMIT IN DUBLIN: Bomb threats targeting airlines around Europe and Irish media organizations are sparking concerns over a potential link to Thursday’s European Political Community summit in Dublin, which is expected to bring together leaders from 47 countries.

Coordinated campaign: The Irish government said Friday that the threats to media organizations, along with the bomb threats against European aviation, were likely part of “a coordinated campaign of disruption.” An Irish official told POLITICO, “it’s no coincidence” that the threats are coming ahead of the EPC meeting. Tommaso and our Irish correspondent Shawn Pogatchnik have more.

Update: There were no bomb threats reported over the weekend, but authorities remain on high alert. Security experts from EU countries will meet in Brussels this week “to review the situation and discuss possible measures that may be necessary,” Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said on Saturday.

Aviation threats: Aviation has faced multiple threats linked to geopolitical tensions in recent years. Besides navigating airspace restrictions due to conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East and avoiding Russian airspace, the sector was also disrupted by drone incursions at European airports last year. GPS jamming and spoofing regularly affect flights near the borders with Belarus and Kaliningrad and around the Black Sea.

An easy target: Aviation professionals fear that air transport is becoming an easy target. “What worries us as a profession is certainly that civil aviation becomes a play-ball for disrupting systems,” said Paul Reuter, an airline captain and vice president of the European Cockpit Association.

Should passengers be worried? “I don’t think people should be afraid of flying at the moment,” Reuter told Tommaso. “The aviation system has quite stringent security procedures … from baggage screening to passenger screening,” he added, noting that “nothing has changed in the security measures that would make it now easier or more plausible for someone to smuggle a bomb onto an airplane.”

Some math: Eurocontrol’s figures put the scale of the disruption into perspective. The organization “is aware of bomb threats against 14 flights on Nov. 6, 14 flights on Nov. 5, 7 flights on Nov. 3,” Eurocontrol said in an emailed statement. On each of those three days, more than 26,200 flights operated across the European network, meaning the reported threats affected only about 0.05% of daily flights.

CARS

EXCLUSIVE — SUPPORT FOR CHINESE EVS GROWS: Europeans are increasingly interested in governments buying Chinese electric vehicles and solar panels. In Germany, 55% of consumers support government purchases of Chinese-made products, according to a new Chatham House survey published today and shared in advance with POLITICO.

Global appeal: The findings reflect a broader trend, with 68% of respondents across 33 countries supporting government purchases of Chinese clean tech, against 21% opposed. Across Europe, 52% are in favor and 31% against.

With a little help: Rising energy prices caused by the war in Iran may be making cheaper Chinese EVs and other green tech more attractive, the report concludes.

Shifting tides: Germany isn’t an outlier. Consumers in the Netherlands similarly support using government funds for Chinese goods. Even in France, which is the only European country where opposition exceeds support, as many as 39% of respondents said they would support the government buying Chinese-made tech, versus 43% who oppose that.

Yes, but: It’s “striking to see European consumers warming up to China’s clean tech offer, just as the continent’s political and industrial leaders reach consensus on the need to take robust trade measures,” Chris Aylett, senior research fellow at Chatham House’s Environment and Society Centre, told our colleague Caroline Hug.

Recap: The findings come as the EU negotiates the Industrial Accelerator Act that will set Made in Europe requirements for public procurement in clean technologies. The U.K. is also working with the EU to tackle China’s dominance in the car sector. Trade Minister Anas Sarwar indicated Britain was prepared to align with EU tariffs on Chinese EVs.

Competition problem: The findings highlight another problem for Europe’s embattled automotive sector: Consumers in non-European markets are increasingly likely to buy a Chinese electric car, with 60% in Latin America saying they are likely to buy one, rising to 80% in sub-Saharan Africa.

Affordability: The biggest barrier to potential buyers is price. In Latin America, where Chinese automakers are increasingly focusing their exports, 39% of respondents said they were interested in Chinese EVs but couldn’t afford them. That’s not great news for European automakers, which have struggled to compete with Chinese EVs in part because the Chinese models are significantly cheaper.

REPORT CALLS FOR DEEPER UK-EU AUTO TIES: Automakers on both sides of the Channel are facing headwinds, including new trade barriers, high energy prices and competition from Chinese rivals. The solution is for the two sides to work more closely together, according to a LSE Consulting report commissioned by EU car lobby ACEA and its U.K. counterpart SMMT.

In it together: “Our collective response must strengthen both sectors, with the UK included in ‘Made in Europe’ measures, a pragmatic solution to 2027 rules of origin, and stronger integrated supply chains,” Mike Hawes, SMMT’s chief executive, said in a statement.

Reminder: The industry is urging policymakers to delay new rules of origin that are set to go into effect next year as part of the post-Brexit trade agreement. Vehicles that don’t meet the rules would face a 10% duty. The sector also wants the U.K. to be included in the IAA as a trusted partner with vehicles produced across the Channel eligible for public subsidies.
