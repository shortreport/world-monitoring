START HERE — SCOOP: A group of countries led by Germany is reviving the row over using frozen Russian assets to fund Ukraine, pitching a new loan for Kyiv supported by the assets. That would replace at least some of the €100 billion planned for Ukraine in the EU’s upcoming budget, in line with Berlin’s aim of radically cutting the budget overall, four diplomats and officials told Hans von der Burchard, Nick Vinocur and Gregorio Sorgi.

Tough choices: Belgium, which hosts the bulk of the frozen funds, fears it would bear the burden of any legal challenge by Russia and shot down a similar plan last year. But one of the diplomats said the move is seen as preferable to slashing planned spending elsewhere. “It confronts countries like Belgium with the question whether they want to finance Ukraine support via the [budget], meaning there may be less money for farmers and regions, or whether they free up many billions by using the Russian assets instead,” they said.

DRIVING THE DAY

THE UNION’S IN A STATE: EU countries will today tell Commission President Ursula von der Leyen to use her State of the Union speech on Wednesday to commit to turning around the bloc’s flagging economy and tackle immigration — as public discontent drives up support for far-right parties across the continent.

Political appetite: Ambassadors from the 27 countries have been invited for a lunch with von der Leyen in the exclusive dining room on the 13th floor of the Berlaymont, off limits to ordinary staffers and catered by a team of top chefs.

On the agenda: While von der Leyen’s best-trailed policy announcements were on social affairs and geopolitical issues, including a social media “delay” for children under 13 and finding financial support for Ukraine, national governments seem to have other things on their minds. The speech comes two years after ex-ECB boss Mario Draghi predicted “slow agony” for the EU if it didn’t take action to support key industries — and, with the president entering the twilight of her time in office, little has been delivered to solve the crisis, according to POLITICO research.

Ambassadors will likely raise economic growth, job creation, the single market, competitiveness, cutting red tape and protecting Europe’s borders, five diplomats from across the bloc and two officials (granted anonymity to speak frankly) told Playbook. The push comes as von der Leyen faces flak from some quarters for her handling of growing concerns about borrowing costs, public spending and debt.

“We see the economy isn’t working for people … this is what is driving the surge of the far right,” said Petros Fassoulas, secretary-general of the European Movement, which brings together pro-EU politicians to campaign on key issues. “We are running out of opportunities to demonstrate to our fellow citizens that politics, democracy, Europe, can deliver answers.”

What do they want? “We can only show that we can deliver, by actually delivering,” said Hildegard Bentele, a German MEP from Friedrich Merz’s CDU. “That means concretely improving the conditions for businesses and citizens, like cutting red tape, lowering costs and improving infrastructure and security.”

Seeing red over green: A handful of countries, including the Czech Republic and Poland, want von der Leyen to further relax green legislation like the Emissions Trading System to ease pressure on heavy industry. Meanwhile, right-wing EPP and ECR lawmakers have revived a bid to scrap carbon rules if prices rise. A rival faction, led by Sweden and the Baltics, says that doing so would undercut companies that have already invested in the clean transition.

New arrivals: A coalition of countries, led by Denmark and Italy, is pushing for stronger powers to crack down on migration to avoid the issue fueling the extreme right, three of the diplomats said. But, with a package of measures already signed off and illegal crossing figures falling, von der Leyen will have to dig deep to find something new to say on the subject.

FAR-RIGHT FIREWALL

CROSSING THE RUBICON: The European People’s Party’s decision to work with the right and far right on a key spending file is inflaming divisions in the European Parliament — and threatening to derail negotiations on the bloc’s next long-term budget, Max Griera and Gregorio Sorgi report.

The flashpoint: Despite vowing to keep the far right out of budget negotiations, EPP MEPs last week used their support to push through an opinion on a file setting the rules for how EU money is spent, tracked and evaluated. The liberals, Socialists and Democrats and Greens wanted to retain green conditions and gender-mainstreaming requirements, while the EPP wanted to water them down.

The opinion is due to go before Parliament’s plenary next week. It won’t become law, but it will feed into the final legislation, which is still under negotiation. Now “we know what’s coming,” Danish Renew MEP Stine Bosse said, warning that the EPP will push to remove the green requirements.

Budget threat: Jean-Marc Germain, the S&D’s lead negotiator on the final text, said he wouldn’t take the opinion into account and would instead work with centrist groups. “This piece of legislation is key for the S&D. If it is weakened, it will lead to the S&D rejecting the entire [EU budget],” he warned.

EPP negotiator Niclas Herbst said he’d seek “broad support,” while urging the S&D to be “more open to cooperation and compromise.” Siegfried Mureșan, the group’s lead MEP on the overall budget, said the EPP remained “committed to a centrist majority” and that the far right would not become a “broader tendency” in budget talks. EPP group Chair Manfred Weber declined to comment.

The vote shows the difficulty of enforcing the firewall across a broad political group — and underlines that whether the EPP keeps the far right at arm’s length can come down to individual MEPs. With the long-term budget spread across dozens of files and committees, center-left lawmakers fear that similar cooperation could emerge elsewhere.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The EU ombudsman yesterday launched a probe into Ursula von der Leyen’s pick as special envoy on AI, but what does the ombudsman do? The office investigates complaints made against EU institutions, agencies and bodies, and looks into claims of the likes of unfair conduct, abuse of power and discrimination. Anyone can make a complaint to the ombudsman, or the watchdog can start its own investigations. The European Parliament picks the ombudsman for a five-year term, which can be renewed. The current office-holder is Teresa Anjinho.

CONSULTANCY CONUNDRUM

ETHICAL DILEMMA: What do you do when you land a Brussels public affairs job — and hate the idea of representing a particular client? It’s a problem that’s especially acute for junior staffers, according to five consultants who spoke to Mari Eccles for our sister newsletter, EU Influence. “You kind of have to take what you’re given,” one of them said, because “you just need to get out of the intern trap.”

“I remember I went into the office one day with just the very senior partners,” another young consultant told Mari. “They asked me whether I was interested in working on something new … And then they just said, ‘Well, it’s tobacco. Are you going to work for this or not?’ … You don’t really feel able to say no.”

Others say there is leeway at certain firms, which let staffers set boundaries. “You also don’t want anyone who doesn’t believe in the client,” the first consultant said, while a third public affairs worker advised younger colleagues who might have concerns about a particular client: “You have a lot more leverage than you think.”

No-go zones: The most frequently cited red lines among the consultants POLITICO spoke to were the tobacco, oil, alcohol, defense and gambling industries, plus the far right, Russian interests and Chinese companies. The first consultant said she turned down a fossil fuels client after a decade in the business. How did that go down? “I was fired.”

Are there sectors that don’t have the same reputational risk? “I’ve never met anyone doing financial lobbying that has any qualms about it,” the first consultant said. “I think it’s because there’s so much f*cking money.”

SPRITZ MISTO, PART 2: Remember the spritz misto saga? The cocktail MEP Brando Benifei ordered at the Italian Republic Day party — only to leave the bartender stumped over how to make it. But at Spirits Europe’s reception, Campari’s Enrico Bocedi confirmed to Playbook that the drink exists, particularly in Milan and northern Italy, where it’s better known as mezzo e mezzo — literally “half and half,” for its equal parts Campari and Aperol.

It’s not official, though: It hasn’t made the International Bartenders Association’s official cocktail list, Bocedi explained. Yes, cocktails are codified: There are now more than 100 on the IBA list, divided into three categories.
