DRIVING THE DAY

LE PEN WANTS TO THROTTLE EU DIPLOMACY: France’s far-right presidential front-runner is aiming to dismantle the EU’s foreign policy apparatus and hand diplomacy back to capitals if she’s elected, Clea Caulcutt and I report based on conversations with National Rally officials.

Paris has been the most steadfast backer of the European External Action Service since it was launched in 2011. It stood up for the institution and its leader, Kaja Kallas, when both faced criticism earlier this year. Yet that support will vanish if Marine Le Pen wins power, plunging the EEAS and its delegations around the world into an existential crisis.

Le Pen isn’t just eyeing the EEAS. The National Rally leader is also keen to rein in the Berlaymont, which has become a diplomatic powerhouse in its own right under European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen: participating in high-level Ukraine talks, striking deals with third countries and beefing up external-facing policy units.

“France will no longer accept that the Commission exercises diplomatic competences, even less military or intelligence competences,” Le Pen told reporters on a recent trip to Brussels.

But it’s the EEAS that is at greatest risk of becoming collateral damage as nationalist parties seek to wrest power back from the EU. A National Rally lawmaker, granted anonymity to speak frankly, blasted the service as a “waste of money” and a source of jobs for “friends of friends.” And National Rally MEP Jean-Paul Garraud criticized Kallas for using “very warrior-like language on Ukraine.” (Read more in our full story here.)

Pushing back: Kallas’ backers say her positions reflect those of the EU foreign ministers she represents. An EEAS official said: “Across Europe, the far right is against the very idea of European cooperation. EU diplomacy defends European interests, including those of France. The EU’s global delegation network is a major strategic asset.”

The far-right push would meet stiff resistance from pro-European parties, which see the EU’s diplomatic arm as essential at a time when global politics is becoming more competitive and volatile. “Without the collective power of Europe, France has no chance going up against the U.S. or China alone,” Irish Renew MEP Barry Andrews said.

Reality check: France can’t unilaterally pull the plug on the EEAS, which has an annual budget of about €1 billion. By the time French voters head to the polls in April, EU leaders will most likely have struck a deal on the bloc’s long-term budget and locked in funding for the service for another seven years.

But the fight could get nasty. Le Pen’s party is threatening to slash France’s contribution to the EU budget if she doesn’t get concessions from Brussels — which could include clipping the wings of EU diplomacy. Her allies think they’re likely to get support on that from nationalist parties in Spain, Italy and Czechia.

The bottom line: There’s been much discussion about whether Le Pen will follow Giorgia Meloni’s pragmatic Euroskeptic approach to Europe if she becomes French president. But her stance on EU diplomacy suggests she’ll take a far harsher line with Brussels than the Italian prime minister.

UKRAINE TALKS SPLIT EUROPE

SIDELINE SEATS: National security advisers from France, Germany and the United Kingdom — aka the E3 powers — will be present for Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi this week, according to three officials who spoke to me and Hans von der Burchard.

Their presence gives Europe a nominal say in negotiations between Ukraine and Russia mediated by the U.S. But advocates of a more inclusive approach — including Giorgia Meloni and Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski — are unlikely to be satisfied, setting the stage for more wrangling over how the EU should be represented.

The fact that talks will focus on a new “European security architecture,” as Donald Trump’s envoy and son-in-law Jared Kushner put it on Friday, is setting nerves on edge in eastern and northern Europe. “The last thing we want is western Europe dealing with Russia, over the heads of the eastern Europeans,” Sikorski told journalists in Warsaw.

French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot told local media on Sunday that France and Europe would be “well represented” at the talks in Abu Dhabi tomorrow and Wednesday. As of last night, it was unclear whether any representatives of the EU institutions would be present.

A German official told Hans that the E3 will be represented at the national security adviser level. Abu Dhabi is a “kickoff meeting” with “follow-up meetings” in the works, the official said. E3 officials will brief a wider group on the sidelines of the European Political Community meeting in Dublin on Thursday to “broaden the scope of the discussions.”

Asked whether those E3 national security advisers would actually be in the room in Abu Dhabi, the German official said the “format will be determined as discussions progress.” An EU official aware of the talks, granted anonymity to discuss confidential preparations, said NSAs would be present or nearby but wouldn’t directly take part.

With major questions about European security now in the frame, EU countries that have an outsized stake in the outcome are demanding more involvement. Meloni, Sikorski and European Council President António Costa are among those calling for a bigger tent. “There can be no discussion about Europe without Europe. And no discussion on European security architecture or indeed security discussion without us,” an EU diplomat told Playbook.

SUMMIT SECURITY

IRELAND ON HIGH ALERT: Dublin is gearing up for a massive security operation around this week’s twin summits, as a wave of bomb threats disrupts air travel across Europe, Sebastian Starcevic reports.

Irish broadcaster RTÉ’s studios in Dublin were evacuated after a bomb threat on Friday, and at least 27 aircraft were targeted by hoaxes last week, triggering diversions, fighter jet escorts and airport searches. EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it’s too early to determine whether the incidents were linked.

But the Irish authorities aren’t taking any chances. With more than 50 leaders set to arrive in Dublin in a few days for the European Political Community and informal European Council, Irish police are launching a “major security operation,” a spokesperson told Playbook. Public transport interruptions and road closures mean much of the city will grind to a halt.

Don’t tell Trump: “We have not seen a policing and security operation of this scale ever before in Ireland; this is bigger than a U.S. presidential visit,” Superintendent Liam Geraghty said. Irish Foreign Minister Helen McEntee said she’s “extremely concerned” by the threats and foreshadowed the tightest possible security measures for leaders and their delegations. “This is the largest event of its type that we’ve ever had,” she added.

Irish premier Micheál Martin said there’s no evidence the threats against Irish media were linked to the Kremlin, but that hybrid attacks in Europe have increased and have generally had a “Russian connection.” Martin added that he’s confident the summits will go off without a hitch.

Brussels is hands off: A European Commission spokesperson said it typically offers the rotating Council presidencies up to 10 staff to help organize summits, but security is the host country’s responsibility.

FED UP WITH NO-SHOWS: Restaurateurs in Brussels have started asking for deposits, requiring credit cards and even maintaining a blacklist because so many customers are failing to show up after making a reservation, BRUZZ reports. Philippe Limbourg of Gueuleton says no-shows cost him up to €8,000 a month. He’s sharing names with around 20 fellow restaurateurs, building a blacklist of roughly 100 customers.

There’s an inevitable EU twist to this: Sharing those lists might breach GDPR rules, according to David Debin of Horeca Brussels, which represents the hospitality sector.
