Morocco threatens China pivot over fears EU will shut out its carmakers
By Jordyn Dahl · Nov 19, 2026, 7:00 AM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — The EU’s push to shield its auto sector from Chinese competition risks driving Morocco into Beijing’s arms, as Rabat fears being excluded from the bloc’s industrial protection scheme.

“The Plan B is to look for new allies, to build new type of cars for new markets,” Ryad Mezzour, Morocco’s industry minister, told POLITICO in an exclusive interview. “We need to find new trusted partners for our development. Nobody is excluded, including the Chinese.”

The threat to cozy up to the very country the EU’s Industrial Accelerator Act is designed to limit is unprecedented from one of the bloc’s closest trade partners. Morocco is Africa’s leading car producer, with European automakers manufacturing some models in the country. The EU is its biggest market: 85% of the 493,000 passenger cars produced in 2025 were exported to Europe.

The EU’s institutions are negotiating the IAA — an attempt to shield strategic industries, such as electric vehicles, batteries and solar panels, from increasingly ferocious Chinese competition. It would limit Chinese investments and set “Made in Europe” requirements for public procurement.

But that provision depends on defining what counts as Made in Europe. Countries from Morocco to Turkey, Japan and South Korea are frantically lobbying to be included. The growing consensus is that the boundary will be tightly drawn — including the U.K. but excluding Morocco, for example.

Mezzour met with diplomats from France, Italy, Lithuania, Sweden and Denmark during a 48-hour tour through Brussels this week. He argued that Morocco should be treated like the U.K., particularly given Rabat’s association agreement with the EU.

But the pleas are not gaining traction, with diplomats responding: “We need to protect ourselves and you’re collateral damage,” according to Mezzour.

“We are less and less confident that the European doors will be open to Moroccan cars in the future,” he added.

Morocco’s potential inclusion in the IAA poses a particularly difficult political problem for Spain, which has advocated for a far narrower definition for what would qualify as Made in Europe. Its economic ties with Rabat are under renewed scrutiny after this summer’s migrant crisis in its Ceuta exclave.

Yet the political friction sits alongside a deeply integrated automotive relationship between Morocco and the EU.

The trade agreement between the EU and Morocco opened the door for automakers like Renault and Stellantis to set up assembly plants, tied to a web of local parts suppliers. The location allowed the companies to better compete with Chinese rivals on pricing, thanks to lower labor and production costs.

“We are not a partner to deindustrialize Europe. We consider ourselves as a partner to accelerate European industrialization,” Mezzour said.

The car sector is now the top industrial employer in the country. But the IAA puts Morocco’s economy at risk. Since the regulation was put forward by the European Commission in March of this year, 1,200 jobs have been cut from one automaker and 40% of the planned investments in the country have been axed, Mezzour said.

EU policymakers are also worried that if Morocco were included under the IAA, China could use it to circumvent the act’s restrictions and other trade measures. Chinese carmakers have announced over €10 billion in investments in Morocco — largely for electric vehicles and batteries.

Mezzour called the accusation hypocritical, saying Morocco is used “far less than Europe is used as a manufacturing [location] for the Chinese.”

Chinese automakers and battery manufacturers have set up production across the EU — particularly in Hungary and Spain — and European automakers like Stellantis are also partnering with Chinese brands, opening up dealership networks to the competition.

If Morocco courts more Chinese investments, it would also bring more ccompetition to the European automakers producing in Morocco, Mezzour warned, adding they will have “competition in the markets they’re considering as theirs.”

The threat — which Mezzour emphasized is not a bluff — goes beyond courting Chinese investments. It would be a rethinking of the overarching EU-Moroccan relationship, with Rabat enacting “some counter-protective measures.”

While Mezzour lobbies EU countries — it’s his third trip to Brussels to press on the issue — talks between Morocco and Chinese carmakers are ongoing, he said. One deals are struck, vehicle production could start by next year.

That leaves a narrow window to ensure that Morocco isn’t excluded from the IAA.

“Our hopes are, to be honest, diminishing every day,” Mezzour said.
