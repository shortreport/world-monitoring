DRIVING THE DAY

AUTOMOTIVE INDUSTRY IMPROVES CYBERSECURITY RISK: Automakers are better prepared for a cybersecurity threat, thanks to increased efforts to regularly check for weaknesses and oversee security, according to an assessment in the biennial cybersecurity report from the EU’s Agency for Cybersecurity released earlier this week. But policymakers are still raising concerns about Chinese cars roaming the streets and the data they’re collecting.

The problem: Cars are no longer just mechanical, instead containing cameras, sensors and software that collect data on road conditions and speed, among other things. Experts say the vehicles are vulnerable to the same hacking concerns as phones or computers — and can be used as surveillance devices themselves.

Not welcome here: Poland banned Chinese connected cars from its military bases earlier this year. Norwegian soldiers in September discovered that Chinese-branded vehicles they rented were recording videos of the routes taken and even the codes used to access facilities. The U.S. is the latest to take action, prohibiting cars from Chinese manufacturer BYD from bases in Japan, citing national security concerns.

Time to act: A group of European lawmakers sent a letter (obtained by POLITICO here) to French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, urging his government to prohibit BYD cars from sensitive sites, including military facilities and ministerial buildings. The same group — led by French lawmaker François Kalfon — put forward a written question to the European Commission that was seen by POLITICO, asking the executive to have the cybersecurity agency assess the danger posed by Chinese cars and put BYD on a list of high-risk suppliers under the proposed Cybersecurity Act.

Acknowledged risks: The Commission identified the automotive sector as a priority in its Cybersecurity Act, stating the industry is reliant on high-risk vendors.

Yes, but: The way in which the revised Cybersecurity Act applies to vehicles is somewhat murky. The legislation refers to critical-sector and critical-infrastructure cybersecurity — not products. That means vehicles themselves would not be affected. Instead, the legislation would apply to manufacturing facilities in the EU, our cyber colleague Sam Clark reports.

Unintended consequences: A carmaker could be barred from using a Chinese component in its German factory, but that same company could use the component in its U.S. or other overseas factory.

MARITIME

EU BAN THREATENS YAMAL’S SHIPPING LIFELINE: The EU’s full ban on Russian LNG starts on Jan. 1, and it could have a massive impact on Russia’s Yamal project.

Why ports matter: Yamal depends on specialized Arc7 ice-class tankers to move LNG through Arctic waters, especially in winter. Nearby EU ports let those vessels unload quickly and return north, keeping the shipping loop relatively short. The EU paid an estimated €8.7 billion for Russian LNG in the first 10 months of this year.

That gets harder: Once access to EU terminals is cut off, cargoes may have to travel farther to find buyers, raising voyage times and shipping costs and tying up the limited Arc7 fleet for longer. Urgewald argues that could significantly weaken the economics of Russia’s Arctic LNG trade.

Europe remains central: Between January and October, EU ports received 177 Yamal cargoes totaling 12.89 million metric tons — 81% of the project’s recorded deliveries by volume. Zeebrugge alone took 42 cargoes over the period.

Winter squeeze: The timing matters because the Arctic shipping season has already tightened. Since 2018, only 11 Arc7 LNG cargoes have traveled east via the Northern Sea Route between November and May, meaning winter exports remain heavily dependent on the specialized fleet and access to Western markets.

MARITIME SECTOR PRESSES ON TRANSSHIPMENT INCENTIVES: Four shipping lobbies urged MEPs to preserve and strengthen a proposed incentive-based mechanism for transshipment at European ports in the ongoing revision of the Emissions Trading System.

What is this about? Transshipment, the transfer of goods from one ship to another at an intermediate port, is “the most trade-exposed part of the sector,” according to a letter to members of TRAN and ENVI committees seen by Tommaso.

EU disadvantaging EU ports: The document — signed by the European Sea Ports Organisation, the Federation of European Private Port Operators, the World Shipping Council and Assarmatori, an Italian shipowners’ association — says that the ETS creates a competitive disadvantage for “European transshipment hubs relative to non-EU hubs.” The concern relates to cargo that neither originates in nor is destined for the EU but simply changes ships at an EU port while traveling between other countries. That’s because shipping companies pay ETS charges on 50% of emissions from voyages between EU and non-EU ports.

Praising the Commission: The sector, however, supports an incentive-based measure for transshipment included in the ETS revision proposal that would allow companies to surrender fewer ETS allowances — carbon permits that shipping companies must hand over to cover their emissions — for voyages by large container ships traveling from non-EU ports to EU ports over distances exceeding 300 nautical miles. Under the proposed mechanism, shipping companies would face ETS charges only on the outbound leg, from an EU port to a non-EU port.

But, but, but: The letter argues that this mechanism should be expanded to cover voyages “both in and out of the EU” as well as “the entirety of the services that call at European ports for transshipment,” with the same rules applying at every EU port.

CARS

EV-MAKERS CHALLENGE EU CHINA TARIFFS: Tesla Shanghai and Geely have received dates for their hearings in Luxembourg on their complaint against the EU’s anti-subsidy duties on their electric vehicles, our trade colleague Koen Verhelst reports.

Save the dates: Tesla’s Shanghai unit will be up first on Dec. 8. While the Chinese branch of the American automaker received the lowest duties of 7.8% in the EU’s EV probe, it still sued the bloc at the General Court in Luxembourg in the hope of shaving off a few percentage points. Geely, which owns Volvo Cars, is up the day after on Dec. 9. Cars made by that company face a much steeper 18.8% duty.

Exercise patience: The duties were put in place in the fall of 2024. If they win, they could seek refunds for duties already paid.

More in the works: BMW, BYD, SAIC and a broader Chinese lobby group called CCCME have also filed cases, but they are not yet on the General Court’s agenda.

PASSENGER PROTECTIONS

EU PASSENGER RIGHTS: The Commission’s Directorate for Justice and Consumers will have centralized enforcement powers over passenger rights regulations in air, bus and sea travel, according to a proposal presented Wednesday. The presentation confirmed earlier reporting by Eliza Gkritsi and Sebastian Starcevic.

Slim staffing: The Commission envisages 19 full-time equivalent staff working on consumer protection enforcement at DG JUST in 2028, scaling to 30 FTEs in 2034. The Commission will have the option to take up cases that can reach two-thirds of the EU population and a specified number of member countries. Consumer protection cases are currently handled by national authorities.

DIGITAL TRANSPORT

OMIO WARNS ON DIGITAL FAIRNESS RULES: As Brussels unveils its Digital Fairness Act, travel-booking platform Omio is warning that new consumer rules risk overlapping with the EU’s separate push to make cross-border and multimodal ticketing easier.

Ticketing first: Omio argues the bigger obstacle to consumer choice is still access to operators’ fares, booking systems and real-time data. The Commission’s Passenger Package, proposed earlier this year, is already meant to make it easier for travelers to compare and book journeys across operators and modes, while improving access to rail tickets for independent platforms.

Don’t double up: The company wants the Digital Fairness Act to complement those transport rules rather than pile on new obligations around price comparisons, fees and search results. Omio says independent platforms face more complexity than operators selling only their own tickets and could therefore be hit disproportionately by additional requirements.

Follow the money: Omio also wants the Passenger Package to tackle what it sees as the underlying commercial problem: intermediaries incur costs integrating operators, processing bookings and supporting customers, and say transport companies should compensate them fairly rather than having to recover more of those costs through booking fees.

Bottom line: Omio’s message to Brussels is that consumer protection and competition should reinforce each other — with the DFA focused on clear, proportionate pricing rules and the Passenger Package dealing with access to tickets and fair commercial conditions.
