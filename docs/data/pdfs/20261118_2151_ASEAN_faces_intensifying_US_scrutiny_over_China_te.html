ASEAN
By EG Southeast Asia

US chip restrictions cloud ASEAN’s data center outlook. The US Commerce Department released an interim final rule on 16 November that imposes end-use controls on overseas data centers that provide “significant” computing power to China and other restricted countries. It also introduced new licensing requirements for advanced chip exports to data centers in countries including Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand, though US-headquartered firms that file monthly reports are exempt. This follows the fallout from the Nvidia scandal, which in the region has centered on Singapore, where Megaspeed is based, as well as Malaysia and Indonesia, where reports suggest Nvidia chips may have been routed or accessed remotely by Chinese users. All three countries were already probing Megaspeed and its subsidiaries prior to the recent scandal.

  • The new limitations could moderately affect the region’s appeal for non-US data center investors, adding to existing headwinds from tightening regulations across much of the region amid growing public backlash (see ASEAN countries will tighten rules for new data centers, 2 November 2026). This could result in planned data center investments being scrapped or scaled back, though Washington’s new rule is unlikely to derail the wider data center build out, especially given the strong role of US hyperscalers and growth of non-Chinese demand. ASEAN countries will collaborate with US investigations and make policy changes to avoid more chip restrictions. There is a risk, though, that countries do not address US concerns sufficiently and further reports of chip diversion via the region result in even harsher measures by Washington, with more significant impacts. Meanwhile, the new Commerce rules could help US firms gain strategic advantages in the region.
  • Singapore said it is reviewing the details of new export controls and is engaging US authorities on scope and implementation. It is also exploring ways to ensure trusted access for Singaporean companies. The city-state has stressed its commitment to the rule of law, noting that its investigation into Megaspeed has been ongoing since February 2025, and has highlighted its cooperation with US authorities, even though it does not enforce unilateral US export controls. Minister for Digital Development and Information Josephine Teo also discussed the case with senior US officials during her recent visit to Washington. There is limited planned future data center investment in land-scarce Singapore, but firms based in the city-state are important investors elsewhere in the region, especially neighboring Malaysia and Indonesia.
  • Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has said there was “no indication” so far that advanced Nvidia chips were re-exported from his country but committed to cooperate with the US investigations. Putrajaya last week announced plans for a new law to strengthen data center oversight and prevent chip diversion. The proposed law follows last year’s introduction of a permit requirement for advanced chip exports in response to earlier US concerns on diversion. The latest Commerce rules seemingly leave most data center builds in the country unaffected, because US hyperscalers drive much of that investment.
  • Indonesia and Thailand are also likely to tighten oversight of advanced chip imports. Both have less data center capacity than Malaysia and Singapore, but with major expansions planned they have incentives to address US concerns and avoid further restrictions. Thailand’s vice commerce minister said authorities would scrutinize chip importers more closely, while a spokesperson for Indonesia’s economic ministry stated the government would assess the implications of the US rules. According to UNCTAD, Thailand was the sixth-largest recipient of data center FDI globally in the first nine months of 2025, ahead of Malaysia.
Philippines/Thailand
By Peter Mumford

Trump’s visit to Manila leaves more questions than answers on trade deals. President Donald Trump briefly visited the Philippines this week for the US-ASEAN Special Summit on 17 November and other meetings.

  • In a meeting with Thailand’s Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul, Trump announced the substantial conclusion of negotiations for an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART) with Thailand. However, it is unclear that this marks significant progress over the framework ART agreed by the two countries last year. The US side highlighted that Thailand would eliminate tariffs on 99% (Trump said “all”) of imports, recognize US automotive standards, and purchase $2.6 billion worth of goods from the US. But these commitments were already made public with the framework ART. Nevertheless, the two countries claim a full ART will be reached soon, when further details will be released.
  • During his bilateral with President Ferdinand Marcos Jr, Trump stated that the US and Philippines had concluded a trade deal, but Marcos did not refer to this in his remarks—Manila later indicated an “understanding” was reached—and at the time of writing no further details have been released.
  • The US has so far signed (full) ARTs with Indonesia, Malaysia, and Cambodia, but these deals have not been ratified and implemented—US tariffs apply, regardless.

By Fadli Yusoff

Long-term trajectory:

Upcoming state poll likely to further dent Anwar’s popularity. The Sarawak state legislative assembly elections will take place on 12 December.

  • The election will likely heighten tensions within Anwar’s government. The contest is expected to pit Anwar’s Pakatan Harapan (PH) coalition against the incumbent state government, led by the Gabungan Parti Sarawak (GPS) regional bloc. GPS also belongs to the federal ruling coalition and holds one of the deputy premierships in Anwar’s cabinet, underscoring its influence. As campaigning gathers pace, the regional bloc has become more openly critical of PH’s perceived policy failures in Sarawak, including canceled infrastructure projects.
  • GPS is widely expected to win and maintain its dominance in Sarawak, dealing Anwar his fourth state election defeat this year. Losing either of the two remaining PH seats in Sarawak would further damage the prime minister’s standing. Anwar has also suffered recently from former premier Najib Razak’s transfer to house arrest following a royal pardon, though Anwar has managed to distance himself somewhat thanks to his government's efforts to keep Najib in prison.
  • Nevertheless, Anwar is expected to remain in power until the end of his term. GPS is unlikely to back an alternative federal government, which could diminish its kingmaker role, and especially given that Sarawak has received more state funding and greater autonomy under Anwar. Following the state election, GPS is expected to continue pressing for greater control, especially over oil and gas rights.
Indonesia
By Olga Letticia
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:

Higher government take could increase costs and deter mining investment. President Prabowo Subianto on 14 November instructed the energy minister to ensure that at least 70% of the benefits from mining permits accrue to the state, with operators retaining the remaining 30%. The presidential palace subsequently said that the target refers to proceeds from the management of mining permits, although the calculation and implementation remain unclear. The remarks revive an earlier proposal.

  • The move reinforces Prabowo's push to increase state revenue from natural resources. It builds on broader efforts to capture more value from commodity exports through tighter oversight and the establishment of Danantara Sumber Daya Indonesia, which aims to centralize commodity trading and reduce revenue leakages from under-reporting and offshore intermediaries. The government could pursue the target through higher royalties, revised concession terms, or greater state participation in mining operations.
  • The prospect of greater state ownership and a higher government take could increase operating costs and uncertainty for mining investors, particularly if changes affect existing concessions or contractual arrangements. However, the immediate implications will remain unclear until the government explains how it will calculate and implement the 70% target.
  • Watchpoints include whether the government revives the previously abandoned gross-split proposal or pursues alternative mechanisms, whether similar revenue-sharing requirements extend to other resource sectors such as coal and palm oil, and how far Prabowo is willing to expand state control despite the risk of deterring investment and foreign capital.

Dasco likely to become vice president. Following Gibran Rakabuming Raka’s resignation as vice president on 12 November, Sufmi Dasco Ahmad, deputy speaker of the House of Representatives and Gerindra Party’s chairman, has received endorsements from several regional Gerindra branches and political figures from other parties, including the National Awakening Party and Indonesian Democratic Party of Struggle (PDI-P). Coalition leaders have now also signaled support for a Gerindra candidate. The People’s Consultative Assembly (MPR) is scheduled to elect the new vice president on 9 December.

  • Prabowo will submit two nominees for lawmakers to consider. The choices will indicate how he intends to manage the coalition and consolidate power following the split with former president Joko Widodo. A close loyalist such as Dasco would reinforce Prabowo’s control over the administration, while giving it to another party would help accommodate coalition partners but could create competing demands for influence.
  • Other potential contenders from Gerindra include Sugiono, the party’s secretary general, and Ahmad Muzani, MPR speaker. Names from other coalition parties previously discussed include PDI-P’s Puan Maharani, Golkar Party Chairman Bahlil Lahadalia, and Democratic Party chairman Agus Harimurti Yudhoyono.
  • Growing cross-party support for Dasco suggests limited immediate friction over the succession. His close relationship with Prabowo and experience managing parliamentary and coalition relations have strengthened his position. Coalition partners appear increasingly willing to defer to the president rather than advance their own candidates, although the final choice could still create tensions if parties feel overlooked or seek greater influence in return.
Charting ASEAN

Indonesia and the Philippines saw FDI inflows decline in 2025, bucking the broader regional trend. Indonesia recorded a 13.7% decline, while the Philippines experienced a 17% drop. In Indonesia, domestic political unrest, concerns over fiscal policy, and uncertainty about the government's economic agenda have likely weighed on investment. In the Philippines, the decline underscores ongoing challenges in attracting foreign capital despite efforts to position the country as a regional technology and manufacturing hub—high electricity costs and weak infrastructure are among the deterrents. That said, FDI numbers can be volatile, so year-on-year changes need to be interpreted with some caution.
