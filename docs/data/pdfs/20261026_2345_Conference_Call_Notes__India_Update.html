BJP’s caste coalition is fraying

Youth and opposition-led protests that roiled Delhi over the past month have largely dissipated. The protests centered on the deletion of millions of voters from India’s electoral rolls and the conduct of the chief election commissioner. The opposition is expected to take up the issue again, but the government isn’t concerned. It believes the issue is too narrow to mobilize mass opinion. Modi also remains far more popular than any opposition leader, and trust in the government is high.

The more serious problem for Modi is friction between two key components of his electoral coalition: the Hindu upper castes and the more numerous lower-middle castes (known as other backward castes or OBCs). Upper-caste voters, feeling sidelined by the government’s perceived favoritism towards OBCs, have begun voting against the BJP in local elections in northern India. This vote is not yet going to opposition parties but rather to independent candidates. Youth disaffection fueled by high unemployment will compound the BJP’s caste problem. About 30% of college graduates are out of work, with the unemployment rate rising to 40% among graduates under 25.

The near-term challenge for the BJP is local elections in February and March 2027. Four of the five states going to the polls are ruled by the BJP, including Uttar Pradesh, the country’s most populous state. Uttar Pradesh is particularly important for the ruling party as it forms the lynchpin of its domination of northern India, but the BJP’s single-party majority in the state assembly is now under threat.

With the government unable to address the unemployment problem before February, it is seeking to bolster its position through welfare measures, particularly in healthcare. It is also looking to create a forward-looking narrative around jobs through trade agreements. India’s FTAs with the UK, the EU, Canada, and the US will give India preferential access for low-skilled, labor-intensive goods such as apparel, footwear, and jewelry. The government hopes this will generate jobs ahead of the 2029 general elections.

December will see key trade agreements concluded

Modi’s travel schedule in December will see India conclude a series of trade agreements:

  • Ottawa, 11-12 December: FTA with Canada likely to be signed. Useful for both sides—India gains market access, and Canada demonstrates economic diversification from the US. The two countries are also racing to complete negotiations on a bilateral investment treaty before Modi’s visit to Canada.
  • Miami, 13-14 December: An FTA announcement with the US is anticipated on the sidelines of the G20. This will essentially be the framework agreement announced in February 2025, with some added detail. The announcement will give President Donald Trump a deliverable while deferring final tariff schedules till the conclusion of the US’s Section 301 investigations.
  • Brussels and Berlin, 16-17 December: The EU-India FTA is expected to be signed, with the European Parliament’s ratification expected in early 2027. India is also likely to sign a pending $8 billion deal to buy German Type 214 diesel-electric submarines.

Combined with existing agreements with the UK, the European Free Trade Association, Australia, New Zealand, the UAE, Oman, and Saudi Arabia, India would enter 2026 with a broad FTA network covering its major export markets.

Economic growth is resilient but under pressure

The government is confident India’s economy will grow around 7.5% this fiscal year (ending March 2027). However, the two primary drivers of growth are expected to slow: domestic consumption is increasingly debt-driven, while government infrastructure spending is under mild pressure due to subsidies.

India recorded record FDI inflows of $94.5 billion in the 2025-2026 fiscal year, but net FDI stood at only $7.6 billion due to repatriations by foreign companies and by Indian companies investing abroad. Meanwhile, despite high factory utilization rates, private investment remains muted because of stagnant domestic demand. The government hopes that the FTAs will boost private investment by opening up export markets for Indian goods.

India’s targeted fiscal deficit of 4.3% of GDP for 2026-2027 could come under pressure if the conflict in the Middle East drags on. The conflict has cost India approximately $25 billion per month due to elevated crude prices, plus comparable costs for LNG and LPG. The government has already begun cutting discretionary spending and postponing defense purchases to meet its fiscal targets.

Relations with China have stabilized

Border tensions with China have de-escalated, and President Xi Jinping struck a warm tone during his meeting with Modi at the BRICS summit. However, China continues to disrupt the shift of supply chains to India in areas like electronics, electric vehicles, batteries, and solar cells. This includes expanding export controls, prohibiting Chinese technicians from traveling to India, and denying visas to Indian technicians seeking to visit China. Beijing has proposed a bilateral investment framework to stabilize economic ties, but this faces resistance from India's security establishment.
