Hong Kong leadership selection will likely favor policy continuity
18 Nov 2026 15:13 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 
 
  Listen now · 6 min
  • Hong Kong’s March 2027 chief executive election will formalize a decision Beijing makes behind closed doors, with a 75% probability that an administration candidate will win out, offering continuity in support for the international business environment alongside closer alignment with mainland priorities.
  • The incumbent John Lee remains the likeliest individual choice, although Beijing could also select another official, with either outcome likely preserving the balance between national security and economic priorities.
  • A business candidate (15%) would signal a stronger push to reassure investors, while appointing someone with a Mainland background (10%) would represent a precedent-breaking move, potentially accelerating institutional alignment with mainland China while disrupting Hong Kong’s established governance practices.

Beijing will make the final decision on Hong Kong’s next leader. The formal election of the next chief executive in March will provide insight into Beijing’s priorities over the next five years. The central question is whether Beijing favors continuity under incumbent Lee, a leader with stronger economic credentials and international appeal, or one better positioned to accelerate alignment with mainland priorities.

The Election Committee (EC) will play a largely symbolic role. The 1,500-member body formally nominates and selects the chief executive. Subsector elections on 22 November will fill EC seats with representatives from business, professional, and other sectors. The de facto selection will take place behind closed doors within the Chinese leadership before being formalized through the EC’s vote.

Prospective candidates will likely announce bids in February, mostly with assent from Beijing. Few would do so without informal backing from at least some quarters of the Chinese leadership. EC members may not receive a clear indication of Beijing’s preferred candidate until March. Regardless of who emerges, the next chief executive will be loyal to Beijing. All candidates must pass national security vetting introduced under the 2021 electoral reforms before securing nomination. EC members are expected to ascertain Beijing’s preferred outcome through informal channels before voting.

An administration candidate is the most likely outcome (75%). Three of Hong Kong’s five chief executives had substantial government experience before taking office, having risen through the career civil service and subsequently held senior political appointments. Choosing another administration figure would signal policy continuity, maintaining Hong Kong’s international business environment while further aligning with national strategic objectives. Within this scenario, there are two main possibilities:

  • Second term for Lee (40%): Reappointing Lee would be the path of least resistance, affirming Beijing’s satisfaction with his current balance between national security and supporting Hong Kong’s financial and business ecosystem. Lee has received favorable assessments from Beijing and sought to demonstrate engagement with the business community despite his predominantly law-enforcement background. He points to continued international investor confidence following the national security law’s imposition as evidence of his administration’s success. Although no chief executive has completed two full terms, this is not a political obstacle. Tung Chee-hwa, the only previous chief executive to secure a second term, resigned halfway through amid public pressure and protests. A similar outcome is much less likely given the political environment established by the national security law. The clearest watchpoint is whether Lee announces a bid for a second term.
  • Non-incumbent from the administration (35%): Beijing could instead select another senior official. Some in the private sector believe Lee neglects business interests. His age—69 at the start of a potential second term—also counts against him, given Beijing’s hints favoring generational change. The main uncertainty is which alternative would command Beijing’s confidence. Financial Secretary Paul Chan faces similar age constraints. Chief Secretary Eric Chan and Justice Secretary Paul Lam are more plausible contenders. Both have operational experience and less security-dominated profiles. More junior officials, including Rosanna Law and Alice Mak, have been noted for their administrative competence, although neither appears to be a frontrunner.

A candidate from Hong Kong’s political leadership outside the administration is possible, though much less likely. Starry Lee, the Legislative Council president and Hong Kong’s sole delegate to the National People’s Congress Standing Committee, has been mentioned. However, Beijing has historically favored administrative and executive experience over party-political or legislative leadership.

A business candidate (15%) is less likely. Selecting a business figure—such as one of Hong Kong’s mainland-friendly tycoons—would signal openness to outside experience and the international business community’s needs. Yet Beijing’s preference for governance experience, and candidates’ overseas assets and associated sanctions exposure, make this less likely. Tung remains Hong Kong’s only leader drawn directly from business. His selection three decades ago helped reassure businesses—which wielded greater political influence than today—that Beijing would protect their interests and preserve Hong Kong’s post-handover economic system. A comparable appointment could bolster business confidence.

A mainland-background appointment (10%) is a tail risk. A candidate whose formative years and substantial early career were spent in mainland China, but who subsequently built a career in Hong Kong and met legal eligibility requirements, would represent the greatest departure from precedent. Beijing could favor familiarity with mainland institutions and the ability to bridge the two systems. This partly motivated its selection of Macau’s chief executive, Sam Hou Fai, a mainland migrant. But Beijing has not signaled significant dissatisfaction with Hong Kong’s current administration, and such an appointment could disrupt the deeply localized civil service.
