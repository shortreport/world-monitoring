SNEAK PEEK

— Unified permitting and regulations are needed to move robotaxis from pilot projects to scaled commercial operations across Europe, experts say.

— Greek shipowners warn the next round of global shipping decarbonization talks at the IMO could hit another dead end thanks to an overly rigid EU position.

DRIVING THE DAY

UNIFORMITY NEEDED ON AUTONOMOUS DRIVING: While self-driving car pilot projects are taking off across Europe, there needs to be more uniformity across permitting to get the projects to commercial development. That was the main takeaway from an autonomous driving conference hosted by Uber on Monday.

Regulatory headache: While overall autonomous driving regulations and technical rules are set by the United Nations, robotaxis need operational permits when they are commercially deployed, which must be allotted by national governments. Some member states have a process and laws in place to issue such permits, but many others do not, said Jakob Lohmann, a national expert with DG MOVE.

Bureaucratic backlog: Even in cities where policymakers are keen to scale the technology, bureaucratic roadblocks cost time. “It still takes six months just to receive a testing permit,” said Lavro Nobilo, head of government affairs at Verne, a robotaxi company that operates a pilot project in Zagreb, Croatia.

Think of the benefits: With human error “overwhelmingly” causing the majority of crashes, autonomous cars are a far safer alternative to regular cars driven by people, said Richard Willder, Uber’s head of global autonomous policy, adding that “if deployed responsibly, this technology has the potential to prevent tens of thousands of traffic deaths over time.” Proponents of the technology argue it will make transportation more affordable and accessible, particularly for seniors or people with disabilities who can’t drive.

Labor issues: But deploying autonomous taxis at scale means putting the livelihoods of drivers at risk — a contentious issue in Europe with strong labor protections. “Innovation must go hand in hand with social responsibility,” the European Transport Workers’ Federation said in a June press release, adding that workers have been largely left out of the EU’s push to roll out pilot projects.

Hybrid model: But Willder said it’s “not a binary choice between humans and robots.” Uber envisions a hybrid network where autonomous vehicles are operating at scale, but humans are still behind the wheel in many scenarios or when autonomous fleets are grounded, like in severe weather, he said, although he did not elaborate on what these scenarios were. And the platforms operating the vehicles will need safety drivers to test software updates, as well as others behind the scenes to intervene when necessary.

Skepticism persists: The throughline at many of these automotive conferences is the focus by policymakers on integrating the technology into public transportation. Amélie Pans, a member of the Brussels parliament, used her address at the start of the conference to highlight pilot projects across Belgium that focus on autonomous shuttles. But when it comes to robotaxis, she was far more reticent, warning they should not become a Trojan horse for more cars on the road. “Technology on its own is neither a policy, nor a guarantee of progress,” she said.

Next up … Japan: U.K. autonomous operator Wayve is launching operations next in Japan, whose public authorities “overwhelmingly” welcomed the company, Nourie Boraie, Wayve’s head of European policy, said on a panel discussion. “We heard from the digital minister from Japan that their view is that autonomous vehicles are absolutely necessary to the future of the Japanese economy and Japanese transportation.“

MARITIME

GREEK SHIPOWNERS FUME OVER IMO: The Union of Greek Shipowners is warning that the next round of global shipping decarbonization talks risks collapsing again, accusing the EU of taking an overly rigid position ahead of negotiations at the International Maritime Organization from Nov. 23 to Dec. 4.

The EU angle: The warning comes as member countries are separately negotiating the next overhaul of the EU Emissions Trading System, including the future of the bloc’s carbon price on shipping. The latest Council compromise, obtained by POLITICO on Monday, would preserve the maritime ETS but create a mechanism to reduce companies’ EU carbon bill for emissions also priced under a future IMO global measure, in an effort to avoid double payment.

Not flexible enough, says Athens: UGS President Melina Travlos said in a statement shared with POLITICO that Greece and three other EU countries representing 70 percent of the EU-controlled fleet had pressed Brussels to go into the IMO talks with greater room to negotiate, but those calls were ignored. She accused the EU of “steering the IMO process to a new dead end,” and questioned whether it places greater value on preserving its regional measures — and their revenues — than securing a global agreement.

Their ask: Greek shipowners want the IMO framework to account for the availability and cost of alternative fuels, technologies and infrastructure, and to recognize efficiency improvements alongside cleaner fuels. Travlos warned that an overly rigid global regime could deepen divisions and push the negotiations back into impasse.

Why this round matters: Few people involved in the talks expect a final deal this month, with the next major IMO meeting in April 2027 increasingly seen as the more realistic deadline. But negotiations are entering a delicate phase: Some countries are pushing to delay or soften the framework, while others are trying to build a compromise around meaningful emissions cuts, enforcement and an initial experience-building phase.

BLACK SEA IN THE SPOTLIGHT: Turkey, Romania and Greece used meetings in Brussels on Monday to push a mix of transport connectivity and maritime security measures as pressure grows around the Black Sea, following several attacks on commercial ships during the weekend.

Turkey’s pitch: Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan told a cross-regional ministerial that the EU, Black Sea, South Caucasus and Central Asian countries should coordinate more closely on projects including the Middle Corridor, Development Road, Three Seas Initiative and Global Gateway. On the sidelines, Fidan also met Romanian Foreign Minister Dan Neculǎescu, adding a bilateral layer to the wider push on Black Sea security and connectivity.

Athens raises the alarm: Greek Foreign Minister Giorgos Gerapetritis, meanwhile, called for a “global maritime truce” after Greek ships were among those attacked by drones in the Black Sea. “Maritime security is not merely a condition for global trade,” he said before the start of Monday’s Foreign Affairs Council. “It is a necessary condition for energy and food security and, ultimately, for the prosperity of peoples.”

TRUCKS

TRUCK INDUSTRY TELLS EUROPE TO ELECTRIFY OR FALL BEHIND: A coalition spanning truckmakers, battery producers, charging companies, utilities, logistics operators and fleet owners is urging Brussels and EU governments not to weaken the bloc’s heavy-duty vehicle CO2 rules, warning that China is already pulling ahead in electric trucks, according to a letter obtained by POLITICO and sent to Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

The China gap: Electric trucks reached a 28% share of China’s domestic market in 2025, compared with around 2.4% of new heavy-duty registrations in Europe, according to the letter. The signatories argue the real contest is not just over vehicles, but over where batteries, charging networks, freight technology, jobs and investment end up.

Don’t touch the target: Ahead of the upcoming review of EU CO2 standards for heavy-duty vehicles, the coalition of more than 50 companies and organizations says this is “not the time to lower ambition or change direction,” warning that watering down the technology focus would undermine investments already underway.

Their ask: The coalition wants governments to close the cost gap between electric and diesel trucks, speed up high-power charging along freight corridors and at depots, expand grids and permitting, and support Europe’s battery and component industry. It also wants the Clean Vehicles Directive revised alongside the CO2 standards to boost demand for zero-emission trucks.

CARS

TESLA UNVEILS GERMAN INVESTMENTS: U.S. automaker Tesla is investing €3 billion to expand its production plant in Grünheide, near Berlin. Starting in 2029, Tesla plans to build its self-driving “Cybercabs” at the plant, which already produces the brand’s electric vehicles.

Hiring bonanza: Tesla is already looking to hire several hundred employees for construction and production planning. “We will begin construction of a new factory building as early as the first half of next year,” said plant manager André Thierig.

EU CAPITALS SEND SEP LETTER: Nine EU governments are pressuring the Commission to propose a new plan to overhaul how standard essential patents — think the 4G and 5G patents used in mobile phones and connected cars — are being licensed, according to a letter dated Nov. 10 and obtained by Mathieu Pollet. (We previously told you about the concerted efforts behind the push.)

The signatories include: Austria, Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Latvia, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia and Spain. The EU executive had withdrawn an existing proposal in 2025 amid stagnating negotiations. The delegations now urge it to “promptly revisit this issue and to invite, without undue delay, the stakeholders and experts for exchanges and present a new proposal.”

PIT STOPS

The Commission approved Slovenia using €38 million in state aid to help road transportation companies struggling with high fuel prices due to the war in Iran.
