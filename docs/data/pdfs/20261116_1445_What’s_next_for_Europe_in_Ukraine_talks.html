DRIVING THE DAY

WHAT DOES EUROPE WANT? EU leaders on Friday agreed who should be in the bloc’s negotiating team for Ukraine peace talks. Today, foreign ministers meeting in Brussels will turn to what those negotiators should say if they face their Russian counterparts, three EU diplomats told Playbook.

Fighting for relevance: Neither the EU’s chief diplomat Kaja Kallas nor the foreign ministers are part of the negotiating team agreed in Dublin, which is made up of national security advisers from Germany, France, the U.K., Italy and Poland, plus representatives from the European Council and European Commission. Shaping the mandate is a chance to make an impact amid criticism that the Foreign Affairs Council is losing its grip on the world’s major security issues.

The question of what the EU wants has come into sharp focus as the Ukraine talks have widened into a debate about Europe’s future “security architecture” and Washington has floated Ukraine joining NATO in exchange for territorial concessions.

“There are some strong foreign ministers in that room,” said one EU diplomat granted anonymity to frankly discuss the mood. “Kallas is a strong political character. If they want, they can provide very valuable inputs into the discussions, but they need to be proactive.”

The negotiating team “urgently needs a mandate,” added Austria’s Foreign Minister Beate Meinl-Reisinger. “Europe finally has a negotiating team that is expected to join the next round of talks. Now the focus must shift from the ‘who’ to the ‘what,’” she told Playbook.

In a 15-point paper circulated to EU capitals and seen exclusively by Playbook, Austria urges the EU to work on an international framework to oversee any ceasefire, drawing on the EU, NATO, the U.N., the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) and the Coalition of the Willing, the group of countries supporting Ukraine’s defense. It also calls for “strategic use” of sanctions leverage to “incentivize Russia to fulfil commitments”; a ceasefire-monitoring mechanism using EU satellites; and partial or sector-specific ceasefires that could lead to a wider truce.

That follows previous proposals, like this one from Kallas, on how the EU should approach talks. But the debate about the mandate could prove as challenging as picking the negotiating team, and some diplomats say the job will fall to ambassadors rather than foreign ministers.

The view from Kyiv: “Europe can and should take more responsibility. The times of security outsourcing are over,” Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha told Playbook. “We don’t need a new Yalta,” he added, referring to the deal near the end of World War II that divided Europe into spheres of influence.

Need for speed: While no date is set for the next round of Ukraine talks, U.S. envoy Jared Kushner is pushing for a quick resumption after the three-way meeting between President Donald Trump, Russia’s Vladimir Putin and China’s Xi Jinping in Shenzhen later this week. Washington is working to secure European negotiators a direct role in future talks with the Russians, Kushner posted on X over the weekend.

RWANDA RETURNS

DEPORTATION DEAL: EU countries plan to announce a deal today to send rejected migrants to a deportation hub in Rwanda as part of a pilot program that could pave the way for further deals, according to four people familiar with the matter.

Austria, Denmark, Greece, the Netherlands and Germany are spearheading what they call a “new and innovative solution” to irregular migration. But the deal to be announced in Vienna will face intense scrutiny from a human rights standpoint after similar attempts by Australia and the U.K. ran into fierce criticism.

The “small-scale project” in Rwanda is meant to be a template for similar deals with the same country or others, according to an EU diplomat: “The vision is that in a few years’ time you will see not one hub but a variety of projects with a large group of third countries that could provide new and innovative ways to deal with the migration challenge.”

The deal would only apply to asylum-seekers who have already exhausted all of their options for securing legal residence in the EU and been rejected, Nektaria Stamouli, Hans von der Burchard and Hanne Cokelaere report.

The deal is already facing criticism from advocacy groups that argue it’s a recipe for abuse with devastating consequences for the people who get sent there. “Looking at what we know from previous agreements with third countries, it has proven to be very difficult to guarantee any kind of human rights standard,” Meron Ameha Knikman, senior EU advocacy adviser at the International Rescue Committee, told Hanne.

“It doesn’t matter what you put on paper — as long as it’s not under your jurisdiction, it’s extremely difficult if not impossible to enforce … any kind of guarantees,” Ameha Knikman said.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

There’s a meeting tomorrow of the European Economic Area Council … but what is that? It’s a twice-yearly meeting that involves the EU — represented by the country holding the rotating Council presidency (in this case, Ireland) plus the European Commission and European External Action Service — and three other countries: Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway. They will discuss the EEA Agreement, which extends the single market beyond the EU’s borders.

PLAYING SHERLOCK IN SCHUMAN

SPY SCHOOL: Brussels is teaching EU officials to think like spies — and to spot them, too. Staff in the European Commission and Parliament are getting lessons in counterespionage, including a game that asks participants to break into a colleague’s digital life, Antoaneta Roussi and Gerardo Fortuna report.

License to snoop: The staff are getting crash courses on just how easily sensitive information can fall into the wrong hands. In one exercise, participants are tasked with hunting around a room for suspicious objects that could be used as spy gadgets, one Commission official, granted anonymity to discuss internal training, told Gerardo.

Child’s play: Materials seen by POLITICO reveal a 45-minute “Cyber Escape Game” inviting teams of up to five Commission officials to break into a private office and “step into the shoes of a spy.” Developed by the Commission IT department’s cybersecurity directorate, the game is available on EU Learn, the Commission’s internal training platform, to staff including trainees.

“Don’t miss the chance of becoming a spy in the office!” reads the invitation — a particularly tempting proposition in a place like the Berlaymont. The mission? Piece together enough clues from the office to gain access to the target’s online accounts and private data. Who knew the Berlaymont had its own James Bonds? Full story here.

BUDGET CORNER

MERZ WARMS TO A DIGITAL TAX: German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has signaled openness to a tax on digital giants as part of a potential Franco-German deal over the EU’s next seven-year budget, two diplomats with knowledge of proceedings told Gregorio Sorgi.

French President Emmanuel Macron lobbied Merz to support an EU-wide levy on tech multinationals to finance the EU cash pot at a bilateral meeting on the sidelines of the EU leaders’ meeting in Dublin last week, according to one official with knowledge of the meeting.

Germany has traditionally opposed the tax — which would overwhelmingly affect U.S. firms such as Google and Meta — over fears that retaliation by Washington could outweigh the potential revenue. The European Commission estimated that a digital tax could generate €5 billion per year.

But Merz and Denmark’s PM Mette Frederiksen signaled they could be open to the idea during a meeting of so-called frugal northern European leaders in Dublin — though Germany and its allies insisted that new EU-wide taxes need to be complemented by major budget cuts across the board.

Paper tiger: Supporters of the levy argue that Donald Trump’s domestic political weakness following the Republicans’ defeat in the midterm elections makes retaliation less likely. “The probability of trade threats from the Trump administration has been reduced after the results from the midterms. We should not see a reaction from the U.S. as [a] self-fulfilling prophecy,” said Siegfried Mureșan, the budget negotiator for the European People’s Party.
