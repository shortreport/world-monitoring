notes from my trip to the middle east. a spanner in this week’s xi-trump meeting. responding to technopolarity. and a return to russia-ukraine talks.

middle east

an important time to be in the gulf, the region by far the most impacted by the geopolitical disruption and uncertainty of ongoing security commitment from the united states. both in terms of the direct impact of the iran war and the broader need for self-reliance if the americans retrench (because, unlike other middle powers looking for trade partners to hedge economically, global security hedging is rather more challenging).

the most immediate concerns subsided over the past week as president trump decided to hold off on further military strikes in iran, pivoting back to his “we already control the strait and have destroyed their economy” refrain, so there’s no need for a new deal. trump then pulled vice president jd vance from negotiations (after vance’s latest effort to engage with iranian supreme national security council secretary mohsen rezaei failed), instructing him to instead focus on fighting “corruption and crime” in the united states. facing potential impeachment proceedings from the house of representatives, secretary of defense pete hegseth shifted from being the most supportive of further strikes to saying the iranian military has already been destroyed...and the americans are surveilling iran’s nuclear program, so iran risks further destruction if they try to recover/rebuild. add to the mix newly appointed chief of staff stephen miller, who is supportive of maintaining the status quo, and another round of american strikes (near term, at least) is off the table.

but how do you get to the end of hostilities? iran's confidence in this environment is higher than pre-us elections. accordingly, iranian leaders are demanding both an end to the us blockade and for the americans to release billions of frozen funds before iran is prepared to allow for unhindered passage through the strait. the iranians have signaled willingness to talk about their nuclear program (nothing new here, though there haven't been such discussions in months)...but with no concessions until the americans are prepared to meet their demands. absent that, none of which is likely to happen, iran intends to continue to escalate strikes on tankers and container ships transiting (easier given that iran has also now rebuilt much of their drone and missile stockpiles), as well as occasional strikes on us military targets (as seen in bahrain over the weekend).

for their part, the americans are expanding on their existing strategy. in addition to the naval blockade, they’ve now announced a second blockade for overland trading routes ("operation iron gate"); though harder to enforce and with more limited impact. the presumption is that eventually iran can't handle the economic pressure and will accept better terms. i think that's a misread of the iranian leadership and their capacity to resist; over the coming year, it’s more likely the iranians redouble their efforts to disrupt the strait.

the regional response is concerned and fragmented. for now, i heard a wait and see approach—major new investments across the gulf are mostly paused but not with a desire to go somewhere else instead. the expectation is eventually the war ends (even if it's not clear precisely how) because iran loses its ability to inflict damage and seek rents as the gulf states improve defenses, harden infrastructure, and diversify supply chains. there's greater worry in managing the security front: the gulf states recognize the americans won't play the same role they used to, but nobody else could bring that type of security and intelligence support. the saudis are disappointed in the limited tangible military commitments afforded by their new islamic coalition (pakistan, turkey, egypt, and now syria); the emiratis feel similarly about how little military support and equipment has been made available by america’s european and asian allies, most of whom rely on the strait for their economies more than the americans; and the qataris feel isolated, with a need to buckle down and wait for the end of the trump administration. china’s talk about diplomatic engagement to bring an end to the conflict has been just that. and perhaps the biggest disappointment has been regional coordination—with little trust or leadership beyond self-reliance, the gulf cooperation council (gcc) has been absent since the united states and israel launched the war in february.

but while there’s no good security hedging strategy, there’s a great deal of focus on economic resilience—how to harden and secure critical infrastructure to reduce exposure to iranian and proxy attack. the emiratis are the furthest ahead on this; with a new secondary pipeline intended to bring an additional two million barrels of crude a day to market now looking ready to enter operation in january (nearly half a year earlier than initial expectations); alongside a quadrupling of annual solar investment to eventually make energy supply less vulnerable. two bigger question marks: 1) container ports: jebel ali (in dubai) and khalifa port (abu dhabi), the country's largest container ports, remain practically shuttered, requiring massive rerouting of trade. 2) data centers: there’s strong intention to invest (especially on the back of expected political delays in us domestic data center construction in the post-midterm environment), with an expected spend approaching 5% of gdp a year (!), but uncertainty around how to ensure their security in an environment where drone and missile strikes are a continued reality (underground being too expensive; greater diffusion of smaller centers impractical; etc). that’s an absolute top priority for the government leaders i spoke with.

it's also worth mentioning the normality of day-to-day life in most of the gulf at present (especially given how much i've heard concerns about security from those outside the region). in the weeks after the war started, there were significant attacks, including on civilian infrastructure; casualties remained low but still it was a source of fear (a la post-9/11 in the united states) in gulf populations. but with the exception of ongoing houthi attacks on saudi arabia—which are going badly (and the saudi-backed yemeni forces needing to pull back to help defend the provisional government)—that's no longer true across the gulf. if you weren't reading headlines on the war, you'd have no sense from walking/driving around dubai and abu dhabi that there's a war on—and i heard much the same from other regional leaders i spoke with.

also a moment on israel, where it now looks much more likely that netanyahu is out, and a new opposition-led government (likely led by gadi eisenkot, possibly in a rotational/shared environment), with participation/support from a larger israeli arab bloc will come together in the next few weeks. israel has lost a lot of support from the united states and europe, but a post-netanyahu government is likely to improve its relations in the middle east: in an environment where gulf leaders have been extremely disappointed with the unilateralism of the united states and the lack of military support from europeans who rely on the region for transit and trade, israeli intelligence and resourcing has become more important and appreciated. a new government taking security seriously for palestinians in the west bank and renouncing expansionism in lebanon and syria will also facilitate that.

us-china summit

president trump’s most important foreign policy success (albeit on the back of his own disruption given the short-lived trade war) has been building stability in the us-china relationship. not easy to do given the complete absence of trust between the two governments, the lack of alignment in political and economic systems, and the feeling among many in both administrative apparatuses that they aren’t invested in the success of the other country.

still, trump has wanted to personally engage and reduce the potential for future hostilities. which has had success because he doesn’t have an ideological issue with china being authoritarian or state capitalist (in fact, in general, it’s easier from his perspective since it means you can in principle negotiate everything with the man on top). trump is much more transactional than strategic in his orientation toward china, despite the misalignment of that with most in his administration. so over much of the past year, both trump and xi have seen their summit meetings as an opportunity to make progress.

this week’s meeting is going to be more challenging. because despite those intentions, the reality of a massive spy scandal involving nvidia, an essential us technology company underpinning the two countries’ relations while also critical for understanding who has the advantage in the ai race, has suddenly become the most important issue in the relationship.

nvidia ceo jensen huang had been arguing for years now that his company should be allowed to export advanced chips to china because that way china will become dependent on them. it's a compelling argument for nvidia shareholders (more revenue) but not credible for us national security, because 1) in the short term, it undermines us advantages by helping chinese ai labs keep pace; and 2) more broadly, china is already convinced that they need to develop their own semiconductor industry and derisk from the americans as fast as humanly possible. and now there’s evidence that he oriented his largesse specifically to direct some $4 billion in advanced chips to (and order nvidia to intervene with the department of commerce to suspend investigations of) megaspeed, a singapore-based firm with an executive he was having an affair with (and believed by us intelligence sources to be working for the chinese government—a matter the us president had been briefed on before the last us-china summit in beijing, which trump still invited huang to attend).

trump's perspective is there's nothing to see. he likes nvidia ceo jensen huang personally, seeing him as an important driver of us economic growth and a major donor to trump’s personal causes (like the inauguration and the white house ballroom). trump said it was no big deal, and indeed he reached out to jensen before his resignation to tell him to fight to keep his position. with jensen having stepped down, trump’s view on china’s responsibility for espionage is that everyone does it, nvidia’s chips are too advanced to be copied, and he doesn’t want the scandal to be a distraction.

but none of that is likely to hold water. instead the us ai industry is now more vulnerable given the abrupt necessity for an unplanned transition; china’s spying and the (lack of) us official response will be the issue of greatest media and congressional interest surrounding the trump-xi summit (alongside a xi-organized trilateral with putin), and there's now going to be a series of investigations into the issue…all of which will bring into public view details of nvidia’s manipulation of export controls around the most advanced semiconductors in china’s favor.

with jensen now out (and significant pressure as investigations proceed for trump to remove secretary of commerce howard lutnick as well, who facilitated nvidia’s efforts against the warnings of secretary of treasury scott bessent and other cabinet members…who are now leaking to undermine him), we’ll see a change in policy from congress, to prevent nvidia from exporting chips to china or third countries without suitable know your customer rules in place; as well as a tighter export control regime toward china more broadly. in the short term, stronger export control bills, previously expected to be dropped, will be added to the national defense authorization act (ndaa) as amendments—the chip security act, ai overwatch act, and the match act—as otherwise republicans would be in the position of defending the now disgraced huang. trump can make exemptions, but it will be harder to do—and many republicans will be skeptical. and if trump tries to sweep the whole thing under the rug (and/or supports huang's efforts to make a comeback), we'll see serious leaks from inside the intelligence community and congress.

all of which makes it harder for the present, stable us-china relationship to persist over the coming year. trump-xi relations notwithstanding, a more aggressive tech decoupling, with tit-for-tat punitive policies implemented by both sides, now looks likely.

our ai overlords

the broader issue here is that artificial intelligence, the core driver of present us economic growth and an unprecedented spike in direct infrastructure investment, is self-governed by a small number of individuals with unusual control of (and limited corporate governance or oversight from) their private sector corporations. and while that has not yet reached a peak, the present trajectory is not sustainable.

the nvidia example is instructive and it won't be singular. it’s a structurally essential firm—one of the most important for the american ai boom (arguably tsmc matters more)—as a key chokepoint for the world’s most advanced semiconductors. and jensen huang has been essential for the firm, a founder/ceo with a unique knowledge base and experience, over 50 (!) direct reports, his children occupying senior roles in the firm, no discussion of any potential succession, and a weak board/internal governance.

so on the one hand, it's hard to see how the sudden leadership vacuum at nvidia gets filled. on the other, it's a comparatively useful early crisis to have. you've got the most important company out there engaged in serious risk taking, with a ceo that has far too much power, isn't adequately aligned with national security concerns, and is accordingly a critical vulnerability for the stability and security of the united states. leaving aside the president, nearly everyone with direct or tangential involvement in the issue understands what needs to happen—more transparent and resilient leadership; a stronger board and corporate governance; and a strategic direction aligned with and accountable to the laws and priorities of the government and national security complex.

this isn't only a jensen/nvidia challenge. elon musk represents a different version of a similar issue, in the singular level of control he exercises over his companies. as well as how essential he (and starlink) are for ukraine's ability to defend itself and effectively fight against the russians. once again, not a particular concern for president trump, but it is for most everyone else. and if the first major investigation against the trump administration likely to be brought by the new democrat-controlled senate in january is jensen/nvidia/china, that’s quickly going to be followed by elon/spacex/russia.

there are other versions of this for other key ai leaders in the us private sector. like jensen and elon, anthropic's dario amodei and openai's sam altman are both extraordinary and visionary founders. but they're not established leaders of major multinational corporations. and while the two are extremely different in terms of their priorities, values, and management styles (dario a biophysicist by training who delegates and wants consensus; sam an entrepreneur and an investor who sells more effectively than almost anyone i've encountered), it's risky to have the most important and potentially dangerous products being developed and pushed to market by labs. they don't have the product development focus, the internal governance, the government relations and corporate affairs...all things that you have in long-established corporations that focus on selling products (say, a major airline, automotive company, industrial manufacturer; or the better-established tech giants like apple, amazon, google, and microsoft). mark zuckerberg's meta is in better shape on these fronts, bigger and more well developed than the labs, but retaining a founder-dominated governance structure that ensures mark remains in charge and can change direction and priority at will.

the trends weigh heavily against this continuing. with constraints coming from above: congress driving major investigations into nvidia/china and spacex/russia. from below: a wave of popular animosity toward artificial intelligence, perceived corruption of the techno-billionaire class and their lack of empathy for working, middle and white collar workers; all of which will lead elected officials to want to clip their wings. from outside: with the chinese government having near complete control (and full national security alignment) over their ai companies, while the us government doesn't and increasingly sees that as a vulnerability.

and from the technology itself: concerns that come from the growing (and perceived out of control) capabilities of frontier models and the agents that are programmed by them. the reality that by the end of 2027 most of the cutting edge research that develops these models will itself be directed by artificial intelligence (i’d say that’s recently become near consensus among experts in the industry) makes it more likely we have cyber and other crises that come from these self-regulated models…and demands core governance and national security responses by the government to fix alignment—not the models and agents with their programming (though that’s a major challenge), but more fundamentally, the founder/ceos sharing more of the core national security, economic, and political values of the country.

so the question is whether a democrat-led senate and house can take this up collectively, work with the republicans and the professional bureaucracy, with support from private sector actors as appropriate, to address these issues.

i'm not yet confident on this front. but the nature of the election winners from two weeks ago (a geographically diverse group of moderates) and the nature of the challenge (made starker by the nvidia scandal) make it more possible.

*

and the first trilateral negotiations in months between the united states, russia, and ukraine. jared kushner and steve witkoff drove the process, with president trump still committed to ending the war (and, barring that, normalizing relations with russia). they did manage to achieve another significant prisoner exchange, so both sides can say something was accomplished. and both countries are committed to continuing to engage—the ukrainians because they don’t want to be cut completely loose by the trump administration; the russians because they see potential upside in further dealmaking (like the recent suspension of the diesel sanctions) from the united states.

the bigger news was trump and then jared kushner talking about a broader deal, not about ukraine but european security architecture (which is a consistent kremlin talking point). the russians would be happy to move the negotiations in that direction, but kushner also made it clear the europeans would need to be onside (who were briefed by the americans on the meeting and clearly aren’t prepared to play ball in a negotiations process that cuts them out). it’s hard to see progress in the near term—i spoke with both russian and ukrainian foreign ministers on this count last week...and came away with the view that there's close to zero prospect of progress in the coming months, putin has no interest in a ceasefire and has maintained maximalist demands in any negotiation with the ukrainians—and for now both sides have the ability to continue military escalation.

*

traveling to the middle east did allow me to avoid the us airplane shutdown. that's a resilience story: the faa is losing redundancy, laying off people and leaving fewer who know the systems, aging infrastructure that isn't being replaced, and more shared dependencies. it's easy to forget that tech infrastructure is layers upon layers of redundancy, because one layer going bad can take the whole system down unless you've thought it out in advance. no reason to be confident on that front anytime soon...

and for now, i’m on the west coast this week: the peninsula for the semafor tech meetings and then a little speakers series with my friends in the southland.

very best,
