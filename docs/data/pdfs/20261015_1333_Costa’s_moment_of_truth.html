DRIVING THE DAY

COSTA’S BIG TEST: Nearly two years into the job, European Council chief António Costa will today face the biggest test of his presidency yet, Gregorio Sorgi reports.

Taking over negotiations between squabbling governments on the EU’s next seven-year budget at today’s leaders’ summit in Brussels, Costa is determined to secure a deal by the end of the year. That would not only avoid the messiness of all the high-profile national elections next year — it would also enable him to show off a clear victory before potentially securing a second term.

“It’s impossible to have a new budget [from] Jan. 1, 2028, if you don’t approve this budget by the end of this year,” Costa told reporters yesterday. Failure to reach a deal would lead to an investment shortfall and stymie Europe’s capacity to defend itself, he said.

If only it were that easy … At a private meeting recently, officials could barely conceal their laughter when told that an end-of-year budget deal was possible, according to a senior EU diplomat who was granted anonymity to speak frankly about the fraught conversations.

Messy domestic politics is getting in the way of Costa’s plan. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, who is running for re-election and under pressure from angry regions and farmers, told MPs on Wednesday that “we will sign an agreement only when it is right for Italy.”

Ominously for Costa’s ambitions — and for today’s summit — Italy, France and others have already watered down a commitment to secure a deal by the end of the year, according to draft conclusions for the meeting seen by Playbook.

Berlin and Paris are key to resolving the deadlock, but Friedrich Merz and Emmanuel Macron are hamstrung by weak political positions at home, as my colleagues Nick Vinocur, Giorgio Leali and Clea Caulcutt report. (The front-runner to succeed Macron, far-right leader Marine Le Pen, piled on the pressure in Brussels last night, telling reporters that the attempt to speed up the budget talks is a “democratic scandal.”)

Costa’s timeline is also imperiled by an election in Spain on Nov. 29, which polls suggest Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez is on track to lose. The Spanish vote adds uncertainty to a special EU summit on the budget that is provisionally scheduled for Nov. 26. “It will probably take place,” a senior EU official said. “We will have to then see what will be on the table.”

So what could delays mean? From today, leaders will start knocking on Costa’s door to save their pet projects. “The more time passes, the more it plays into the hands of those pushing for cuts,” said a senior EU diplomat.

Today’s discussion will signal how far leaders are from a landing zone — especially on the tricky issue of EU-wide taxes, known as own resources, to finance the budget.

The toughest fight will be over the figures presented by Ireland, which has steered the talks over the past four months under its rotating presidency of the Council of the EU. Dublin has floated major reductions to the European Commission’s budget proposal.

ESN FIGHTS FOR SURVIVAL

EXCLUSIVE — AfD’S PARTY PLEDGES REFORMS TO DODGE A BAN: The far-right Europe of Sovereign Nations, the EU party whose members include Alternative for Germany, rejected claims by the EU’s party watchdog that it breached the bloc’s values — but promised new internal rules to keep its national parties in line, according to a 160-page defense obtained by POLITICO.

ESN argued that many of the breaches alleged by the Authority for European Political Parties and Foundations predate the party and can’t be attributed to it, and that investigators rely too heavily on subjective interpretations of what constitutes a breach of EU values, Max Griera reports. The defense said deregistration would be disproportionate and proposed lesser sanctions, including a “reprimand” or “temporary suspension of funding.”

The party said it amended its statutes this summer after the allegations surfaced, creating an ethics committee to monitor compliance with EU values, investigate internal breaches, recommend corrective measures and produce an annual report. It also drafted a code of conduct to guide its member parties on respecting EU values.

Russia defense: The watchdog’s case cited Dutch Forum for Democracy leader Thierry Baudet’s praise of Vladimir Putin and Bulgarian party Revival’s pact with United Russia as examples of breaches of EU values. ESN said Baudet was criticizing NATO and Western foreign policy, not directly endorsing Russia’s war.

“One may reject that view, but it should not be regarded as a violation of EU values,” the defense said. It called Revival’s pact “merely a symbolic declaration intended to promote dialogue and normalization,” and argued that without financial or material support, it breached no EU sanctions.

Historically, the watchdog has favored fixes over punishment. Between 2022 and 2025, the APPF identified 30 breaches of spending, donation and other rules but imposed sanctions only twice, after parties failed to take corrective action.

YOUR EUROPEAN COUNCIL CHEAT SHEET

ON YOUR MARKS: António Costa wanted to focus on the next seven-year budget at this EU leaders’ summit. Instead, leaders are going to talk about … pretty much everything. “It’s the largest agenda ever since Costa took over,” one senior diplomat said. Here’s your handy guide to the first day of the marathon diplomatic session.

Blaming each other … Leaders will kick off with talks on energy, with capitals and the Commission expected to point fingers at each other over high prices. Climate rules could take a beating, too.

… then blaming Beijing: Lunch is devoted to “macroeconomic imbalance” — Brussels-speak for China. Ursula von der Leyen will brief leaders on trade talks with Beijing. Expect renewed demands for the Commission to “urgently” sharpen its trade defenses and protect more European industries from Chinese competition, according to POLITICO’s Camille Gijs, who’s been following the prep.

Show me the money: After China, they’ll turn to the elephant in the room — the bloc’s next long-term budget, on which the warring camps are still miles away from an agreement. Spoiler: Don’t expect a breakthrough. “I don’t think we are there yet, nor will we be in the next two days,” one senior EU diplomat said. It’s not looking great for that December deadline.

More mouths to feed: After arguing over how to divide the EU’s money, leaders will discuss letting more countries into the club. Internal reforms to make enlargement possible are on the menu, with Montenegro hoping for a pat on the back and Ukraine hoping its path is not decoupled from that of Moldova.

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Sunny spells, with a high of 21C.

OKTOBERFEST, ICH LIEBE DICH: After all the hype, your author finally made it to the opening of Brussels Oktoberfest. The verdict? Quite possibly the best party I’ve attended in 10 years in Brussels.

“It’s something in between an Irish wedding and Bilbo Baggins’ birthday,” a Commission source told me, looking utterly flabbergasted as we bumped into each other in the crowd. “I’m German, but this thing is wild,” added a journalist from another news outlet.

Where to start? No beer came in anything smaller than a liter. My memories are a little hazy, but I distinctly remember falling off a bench and getting drenched in beer in front of my partner in crime for the evening, Berlin Playbook’s Hans von der Burchard.

The Berlaymont goes Bavarian: Tables were assigned by Commission cabinet, with Ursula von der Leyen’s team inexplicably parked by the entrance. It was basically the Berlaymont with beer and lederhosen — and more networking than at your average EU summit.

Spotted? Forget it. For the first time in my Playbook career, I gave up on keeping tabs on the guests. Too many people. Let’s just say half the EU bubble was dancing on benches (how much weight can those things take?).

Among the familiar faces I did note: EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič, sporting a traditional Lebkuchenherz gingerbread heart around his neck … former Commissioner Günther Oettinger, who happens to celebrate his birthday today (“He’s always here, every year,” one EU official told me) … and European Parliament President Roberta Metsola, who made her entrance alongside a delegation of Bavarian VIPs, briefly looking as if she was the guest of honor. Bavarian premier Markus Söder was a no-show.

One piece of advice for next year: Get. An. Invitation.
