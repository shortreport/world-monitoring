DRIVING THE DAY

YEMEN WARNS SHIPPERS OFF HOUTHI DEALS: Yemen’s Foreign Minister Afrah al-Zouba is urging shipping companies to stop seeking Houthi permission to pass through the Bab al-Mandab Strait and wants the EU to expand its Aspides naval mission and work more closely with Yemen’s coast guard to protect Red Sea navigation.

Don’t normalize it: Al-Zouba told Martina that companies seeking clearance from the Houthis — Iran-backed anti-government rebels — are engaging in “risky behavior.” The international community, she said, should talk to them about alternatives rather than let Houthi-imposed rules on navigation become the norm.

The context: Her warning follows media reports last week that some shippers have been emailing the Houthis for transit clearance. In the messages reviewed, captains said their vessels had no links to countries the group targets and asked for safe passage, and in some cases the Houthis replied with approval and instructions for the voyage. International maritime guidance is not to engage with or register vessels with the Houthi-run office.

More from Aspides: Al-Zouba called on the EU mission to deepen cooperation with the Yemeni Coast Guard and countries around Bab al-Mandab. But she called naval protection a short-term fix, “like a painkiller,” and said lasting freedom of navigation depends on the Yemeni government regaining control of the strait and its territory.

Brussels is already moving: The minister spoke to Martina the day after meeting EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas and Commissioner for the Mediterranean Dubravka Šuica, who announced that the Commission is preparing new support for Yemen’s coast guard and maritime security. The initiative, expected to be adopted in 2027, aims to strengthen coast guard capacity, curb arms trafficking and protect navigation through Bab al-Mandab, while complementing Aspides.

Red Sea meets Hormuz: Al-Zouba argued that the threats to Bab al-Mandab and the Strait of Hormuz are increasingly linked, accusing Iran and the Houthis of coordinating pressure on major shipping routes. She is also seeking more political and security backing, intelligence-sharing and help cutting weapons-smuggling routes, and confirmed Yemen was talks with European partners on a possible terrorist designation of the Houthis.

CARS

CLIMATE ADVISORY WARNS U.K. AGAINST WEAKENING EV MANDATES: Any reduction in the zero-emissions vehicle, or ZEV, mandate would increase the U.K.’s exposure to fossil fuel markets, the government’s top climate adviser Nigel Topping warns in a letter to Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander, published on Wednesday and seen ahead of time by POLITICO. Such a move would “damage long-term competitiveness as our major export markets rapidly transition to electric vehicles,” he writes.

Reminder: As our U.K. colleagues reported earlier this month, ministers are considering slashing mandated electric vehicle sales targets and aligning the ZEV mandate with EU rules. The current ZEV mandate currently requires 80% of new car sales to be zero-emission by 2030, rising to 100% by 2035. The EU rules call for automakers to reduce their emissions by 55% from 2021 levels by 2030. But a fight is brewing between the environment, transport and business ministers on how much to change the rules. (Catch up on that here.)

Commitment at risk: Weakening the mandates would put the U.K.’s “commitment to tackling climate change at risk,” the letter says. It would undermine its push to protect voters from rising fuel prices, potentially increasing fuel consumption by 15 million barrels of oil a year in 2030.”

Keep your nerves: “Ministers should hold their nerve, keep the targets, and close the plug-in hybrid loophole, so the billions already invested in British batteries and charging get the certainty they were promised,” Ben Nelmes, CEO of New AutoMotive, U.K.-based transport research organization, told POLITICO in a statement.

INDUSTRIAL ACCELERATOR ACT

SCOOP — COUNCIL DELAY ON IAA: The Irish Council presidency’s plan to usher countries into a common negotiating position on the Industrial Accelerator Act by the end of November is facing a delay. EU countries were told that a fourth compromise text on the bill would be circulated by the presidency on Nov. 23, with a focused technical discussion planned for Nov. 27, according to an internal note seen by Francesca Micheletti.

Next up: The Irish presidency also told member countries that they would not be seeking a Coreper I mandate on Nov. 27, as initially planned. Diplomats will meet today for an all-day technical discussion on the regulation, which seeks to prioritize EU products in procurement and other public programs.

Recap: The biggest challenge will be ironing out a common position on which third countries qualify as “Made in Europe,” with a majority of countries so far favoring a relatively open model, spearheaded by Germany. France, meanwhile, is pushing for a narrow club, with Italy, Cyprus and Greece edging toward Paris at the last political meeting.

We want more: Austria is joining the fray, siding with Italy in calling for a stronger IAA, its Economy Minister Wolfgang Hattmannsdorfer told our competition colleagues in an interview. “Our approach is Made in Europe, open to trusted partners,” he said, adding “we support including reliable partners in public procurement under reciprocal conditions, while allowing a stronger European focus for subsidies.”

Be more confident: Europe shouldn’t be shy about pursuing its own industrial policy, the minister said: “Europe has enormous economic strength. It is time we used it more confidently.” He drew a contrast between supporting European industry and excluding foreign goods. “Economic patriotism is not protectionism,” Hattmannsdorfer said. “China uses industrial policy strategically. Europe must defend its economic interests just as confidently.”

Best of both worlds: The Austrian minister named Japan and South Korea as examples of important strategic partners. “We want to strengthen these relationships, not weaken them,” he said. “Europe must produce more at home while building stronger partnerships abroad. These are not competing goals.”

Yes, but: Those same countries are lobbying hard to be included as trusted partners in the IAA, particularly when it comes to their automotive industries. A Franco-German deal reached in October kept a narrow definition for the automotive industry, with only the U.K. as an additional partner outside of the 27 EU members. Tokyo and Seoul pushed Chancellor Friedrich Merz to advocate for a “Made with Europe” approach during the German leader’s Asia trip last month.

Meanwhile at the Parliament: Just a few streets away from the Council building, Made in Europe will also be a hot topic at a meeting of shadow MEPs. All eyes are on the European People’s Party: Shadow MEPs from the Parliament’s center-right grouping have expressed diverging views on subject so far, prompting a likely delay of a committee vote slated for Dec. 1.

AVIATION

AIRLINES AND SAF PRODUCERS WANT MORE ETS CASH BACK INTO AVIATION: A coalition of European airline groups, producers of sustainable aviation fuels and technology providers — including Airlines for Europe, ERA, IATA, Neste, Moeve, Enilive, OMV, Honeywell and Topsoe — is urging EU lawmakers to use more of the aviation sector’s Emissions Trading System payments to support SAF production and uptake, according to a letter seen by POLITICO ahead of its circulation.

Their argument: The coalition says the ETS should serve not only as a climate tool but also as industrial and competitiveness policy, with more of the money raised from airlines redirected toward scaling up SAF and supporting Europe’s fuel manufacturing base.

Three asks: They want more SAF allowances through 2040, continued support beyond 2029 for all SAF types eligible under ReFuelEU, and higher price-gap coverage because current support levels are not enough to make cleaner fuels competitive with conventional jet fuel.

Why it matters: The groups argue stronger ETS support would make it easier for airlines to sign long-term SAF purchase agreements and help producers reach final investment decisions on new projects.

MARITIME

19 RESCUED FROM BLACK SEA GAS CARRIER: All 19 crew members aboard the Liberian-flagged Gas Husky were rescued Tuesday after the vessel was struck in Bulgaria’s exclusive economic zone, Greek manager Stealth Maritime confirmed. The company said the ship was carrying LPG from Egypt to Romania.

Second incident: The crew were airlifted to Varna by Bulgarian authorities, a day after 12 sailors were rescued from another cargo ship that capsized off Bulgaria following a suspected drone strike.

RAIL

NO TRAINS FOR UKRAINE: Hungary is suspending its railway connection with Ukraine for two days after a Russian drone struck 300 meters from Mukachevo railway station in Zakarpattia region, Prime Minister Péter Magyar said in a statement on Tuesday.

Context: Ukrainian state railway operator Ukrzaliznytsia clarified that Hungarian trains will stop at the border station of Chop instead of continuing to Mukachevo, and that it will organize transfers for passengers while Ukraine negotiates with Hungary on restarting the connection. “This security-related decision is linked to Russia’s attack today on an enterprise in Mukachevo. Railway infrastructure was not targeted,” the company said.
