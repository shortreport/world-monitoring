Hong Kong’s Five-Year Plan aligns development with mainland policy goals
16 Sep 2026 13:39 EDT
Discover more on the Portal

What Happened

On 16 September, Hong Kong’s Chief Executive John Lee unveiled the city’s first Five-Year Plan (FYP), which runs from 2026 to 2030, aligning with the timeframe of Beijing’s 15th FYP.

Our Take

Hong Kong will retain its market-based economic model. The city lacks the regulatory tools and administrative culture needed to implement mainland-style industrial policy, and the FYP does not suggest a governance overhaul to change that. The FYP’s announcement is primarily about aligning Hong Kong’s existing policymaking cycle with that of the central government, which announced the national FYP in March this year.

The main purpose of the FYP is to convey that Hong Kong’s medium- to long-term projects will serve China’s national strategic and economic objectives. It does not represent a sea change in the city’s policy planning. The FYP introduces a multi-year framework without replacing annual budgets and policy addresses. Many listed projects were already announced in existing multi-year policy blueprints, such as the HK2030+ urban planning scheme and the 2022 ten-year Innovation and Technology Blueprint.

The FYP adds little that is new to the city’s financial sector strategy. Many policy measures listed in the FYP intend to enhance the city’s existing cross-border financial services, such as strengthening Hong Kong’s role as a hub for offshore yuan and digital asset trading, as well as expanding the Stock Connect and Bond Connect schemes. The Hong Kong government has pursued all of these for at least a decade through policy addresses, ordinances, and regulations.

Only five out of the FYP’s 22 indicators are binding targets to be reached by 2030, all of which concern the environment or land use. The remainder are nonbinding or “aspirational,” including two finance-oriented indicators. This suggests that the government is taking a cautious approach and avoiding overcommitment to an expansive wish list. Senior officials have also stressed that the government does not intend to remake Hong Kong’s administrative system to pursue more ambitious objectives.

Integration with the mainland will accelerate over the next five years. There is clear political momentum in favor of integration, reflected in language that increasingly mirrors mainland political messaging and Beijing’s emphasis on “one country” over “two systems.” The main focus is deeper integration with the rest of the Greater Bay Area (GBA) that encompasses Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Macau, primarily by means of building the Northern Metropolis, a flagship infrastructure project to develop land along the Shenzhen border into a financial and technology district.

First announced in 2021 and estimated to cost $30–$50 billion, the project faces significant budget and timetable constraints. These pressures will intensify ahead of a likely visit by President Xi Jinping in summer 2027 to mark the 30th anniversary of Hong Kong’s Handover, when the government will seek to demonstrate visible progress.
