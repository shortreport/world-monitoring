DRIVING THE DAY

AVIATION INDUSTRY SOUNDS ALARM OVER EASA FUNDING: Europe’s aerospace industry is warning that insufficient funding and staffing at the EU Aviation Safety Agency, or EASA, could delay certification of new aircraft technologies and leave European manufacturers dependent on foreign regulators.

Direct R&I funding, please: ASD, the lobby representing Europe’s aerospace and defense industries, is set to release a position paper today calling for at least €100 million a year in dedicated research and innovation funding for EASA in the EU’s next long-term budget, which is currently being negotiated. The agency has no dedicated budget line for these activities, relying instead on funding from programs such as Horizon Europe.

Smaller team, same league: ASD believes “EASA ‘punches above its weight’ when its resources are compared with those of the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the Civil Aviation Administration of China.” According to the document, seen by Tommaso ahead of publication, the FAA employs 1,400 full-time staff in certification, compared with just 280 at EASA.

Waiting for the cavalry: According to the document, EASA would need between 70 and 130 additional certification staff to meet the demands of “future programme certification, other industry project needs and continued airworthiness/safety.”

Getting worse: “EASA has two persons dealing with aircraft seat certification,” said Vincent De Vroey, ASD’s civil aviation director, who recalled previous delays in introducing new business-class seating options because their designs had not yet been certified — affecting both EU and U.S. airlines. He told Tommaso the structural problem is getting worse: EASA is “facing a wave of retirements of experienced people. They don’t have budget to hire the right people or to train them and that obviously creates problems.”

Don’t forget clean tech: Europe’s aircraft industry is also concerned about falling behind in launching cleaner technologies to decarbonize aviation because of certification delays. Low- and zero-carbon aviation systems involve “a totally new type of technology, new type engines, new type of wings,” De Vroey said, stressing that these products are expected to enter the market in the next decade.

Relying on the others: “If [EASA certifiers] don’t have the expertise we will be in the hands of foreign authorities,” said De Vroey, referring to the possibility of EU companies achieving certification elsewhere. However, this path would be even more difficult, the ASD representative said, because foreign regulators usually don’t support the EU industry.

MARITIME

BULGARIA WANTS EU CASH FOR BLACK SEA SECURITY HUB: Bulgaria is seeking dedicated EU funding in the next long-term budget to help quickly get a planned Black Sea Maritime Security Hub with Romania up and running, according to a Bulgarian official who spoke to Martina about the security situation in the region.

The ask: Sofia and Bucharest have developed a joint concept for the hub and are negotiating a bilateral memorandum of understanding. The Bulgarian official said the project is meant to turn the EU’s Black Sea security priorities into operational capabilities but warned that rapid deployment will depend on identifying “concrete and timely European funding solutions,” including through the upcoming long-term budget.

Why now: The appeal comes amid growing concern over attacks on civilian commercial vessels in the Black Sea. Bulgaria says it has stepped up military readiness, rescue operations and diplomatic engagement, while seeking closer cooperation with Romania and Turkey and more support from the EU and NATO.

Attribution caveat: Sofia says it does not yet have direct evidence allowing definitive attribution for the latest attacks, although the official said “the logic points out to Russia.” Bulgaria’s prime minister has summoned the Russian ambassador.

Connectivity angle: Bulgaria is also linking Black Sea security to the EU’s wider push on transport and trade links toward the South Caucasus and Central Asia, including the Trans-Caspian Transport Corridor.

SHIPOWNERS URGE MEPS TO BACK INDIAN RECYCLING YARDS: European Shipowners are calling on lawmakers to reject a motion seeking to block the addition of two Indian ship recycling yards to the EU’s approved list, ahead of an Environment Committee vote on Nov. 4.

Their argument: ECSA says the two yards went through an extensive inspection process and were found to comply with EU safety and environmental requirements, arguing that facilities outside Europe should be recognized when they meet the bloc’s standards.

Credibility test: “Objecting to the inclusion of facilities that comply with the stringent EU safety and environmental standards would undermine the credibility of the EU’s own legislation,” ECSA Secretary-General Sotiris Raptis said.

AVIATION

AIRBUS SAYS EIB LOAN NO REASON FOR TURBULENCE: The European Investment Bank’s €3 billion loan to Airbus is in line with its routine financing operations, said a spokesperson for the European aerospace business after U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer accused the loan of potentially breaching trade rules.

Stormy skies: The EIB announced in June that it would extend the loan to the company, aimed at supporting Airbus’ investments in advanced technologies, including in military R&D.

Nothing to see here: “Like most large global manufacturers, Airbus regularly diversifies its funding sources, which is necessary to manage the complexity of a high-value global production system and supply chain,” an Airbus spokesperson told Tommaso. “The recent EIB loan envelope is a standard corporate financing facility, similar to commercially-available loans we’ve signed in the past.”

No fair: In a statement,Greer said that, despite requests, USTR had received “no details about this loan” and raised concerns that it could amount to an unfair subsidy to the airplane-maker, our trade colleague Carlo Martuscelli reports. “I had sincerely hoped that trade-distorting European subsidies to Airbus were a thing of the past,” Greer said in his statement. “It is important this situation is resolved as soon as possible to ensure market-driven production in the aerospace sector and to avoid violation of the terms of our hard-won [large civil aircraft] deal.”

Cleared for landing: Under former President Joe Biden, the U.S. and EU reached a truce in 2021 on a 17-year trade dispute over aircraft subsidies to Airbus and Boeing. Both sides agreed to pause retaliatory tariffs imposed over the course of the conflict for five years — which ended in June. EU countries kept on ice the bloc’s retaliatory tariffs against the U.S.

RAIL

RAIL INDUSTRY WANTS EUROPE TO PLAY TOUGHER: European rail suppliers are being shut out of nearly half of the global market by trade barriers, costing them access to around €97 billion in contracts each year, according to the Czech rail association ACRI.

What’s the problem: ACRI says EU manufacturers face local-production requirements, mandatory partnerships and technology-transfer demands in markets including China, India, Turkey and Saudi Arabia, while foreign suppliers can compete more openly for European contracts. The group says that imbalance is particularly painful for export-heavy manufacturers and affects everything from trains and signaling to rails and turnouts.

China in focus: ACRI points to OECD research showing Chinese rolling-stock and signaling groups received more than 80% of roughly $5 billion in identified government support between 2016 and 2020, while European suppliers have faced restrictions on access to Chinese procurement.

What they want: The association is calling for tougher use of the EU’s Foreign Subsidies Regulation and International Procurement Instrument, greater reciprocity in market access and more weight for factors such as cybersecurity, security of supply, servicing and European jobs when public contracts are awarded.

Why Brussels should care: The push feeds directly into the fight over the Public Procurement Act, where rail manufacturers have been lobbying for stronger European preference rules, arguing that EU procurement policy should better reflect the barriers European companies face abroad.

PARLIAMENT GETS TO WORK ON PROCUREMENT: The European Parliament is starting work on the Public Procurement Act, with the Internal Market Committee leading and Dutch Greens MEP Catarina Vieira set to draft the International Trade Committee’s opinion.

Why transport should care: The file is particularly important for rail manufacturers, which have made public procurement a major lobbying priority in Brussels. They have long argued that European companies face far more limited access to China’s rail market than Chinese manufacturers enjoy in Europe, and is pushing for stronger European preference in procurement rules as a way to address that imbalance and protect the sector’s competitiveness.
