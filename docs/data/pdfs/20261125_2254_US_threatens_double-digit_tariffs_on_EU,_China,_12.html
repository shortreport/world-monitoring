US threatens double-digit tariffs on EU, China, 12 others
By Daniel Desrochers, Oliver Ward, Ari Hawkins · Nov 25, 2026, 2:53 PM · View in your browser

The Trump administration laid out plans Wednesday morning to raise tariffs on the European Union, China, Japan and 11 other trading partners, a sign the president remains determined to reinstate duties the Supreme Court struck down earlier this year despite midterm voters’ resounding rejection of his economic policies earlier this month.

The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative found that the 14 trading partners had artificially deflated prices by flooding markets with manufactured goods, following a trade investigation launched in March.

USTR is recommending imposing a 20% tariff on imports from China and 18% on Cambodia, Indonesia, Thailand, Bangladesh and Malaysia. He proposed a 15% rate on the European Union, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and Switzerland, to keep within agreed ceilings, and a 10% rate for Singapore. If officials reach a trade deal with Vietnam that remains under negotiation, it could get a 20% rate. But absent a deal, USTR proposes a rate of up to 35%. Similarly, if India finalizes its deal with the U.S., USTR recommends a 17% tariffs on its goods, but absent a deal that rate could go up to 25%.

USTR did not recommend new tariffs on two other countries that were part of the initial investigation — Mexico and Norway. In a press briefing before its release, a senior U.S. official said the administration would continue to review the two countries’ excess capacity, but provided no timeline for completing their probe.

The official said the proposed duties on the other 14 trading partners would likely take effect in January, at the earliest, after USTR holds hearings and receives public comments.

USTR launched the investigation under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows the U.S. trade representative to impose tariffs in response to unfair trade practices, but first requires a review and series of consultations with the targeted countries.

The person also emphasized that the proposed tariff rates will abide by the caps the White House agreed to as part of its recent trade deals with several of the trading partners, including a 15% ceiling on tariffs on imports from the European Union and Japan, and a 20% rate Trump agreed to with Chinese leader Xi Jinping as part of a temporary trade truce reached last year.

Critically, the official said the new duties will be imposed concurrently to another set of Section 301 tariffs the Trump administration imposed this summer on the 14 trading partners and dozens of others, with countries only paying the higher of the two rates, rather than the sum of both.

Nor will the duties stack on other U.S. tariffs on specific manufacturing sectors like steel, aluminum, semiconductors and wood products, including upholstered furniture, the administration officials said.

USTR is also recommended excluding Chinese goods that are subject to the terms of a newly created U.S.-China board of trade, as well as tariff carveouts on fertilizers, energy and food products not made in the U.S. that the administration exempted from its previous tariff actions, the official said.

Even so, the proposed duties are likely to inject fresh tension into U.S.-China trade relations, shortly before Trump is scheduled to meet with Xi on the sidelines of the G20 summit in December. And they risk complicating a fragile trade truce with the country that expires in January. The tariffs also land as lawmakers weigh stiffer curbs on chip exports to China and after Commerce took steps to close a loophole Chinese firms use to access U.S. computing.

“China will look at all of these things as an overall hardening of American policy towards China,” said Scott Kennedy, senior adviser in Chinese Business and Economics at the Center for Strategic and International Studies.

In a separate announcement Wednesday morning, the Trump administration proposed a 25% tariff on imports of Brazilian refined sugar that surpass designated quotas — an action that risks driving up grocery costs amid concerns about high prices.

The U.S. also proposed a 10% tariff on finished aircraft from Brazil, which are currently exempt. Finished aircraft are currently the fourth largest export from the country and the official said the action was intended to spur negotiations.

The tariff hikes on Brazil stem from an investigation USTR completed in July under the same Section 301 authority, and come after the two countries failed to resolve the issues in closed door negotiations, the administration official said.

A public hearing on the proposal will take place on Jan. 14.

Trump’s latest tariff moves are unwelcome news to many U.S. industries that rely on foreign imports, after a period of relative calm on global trade.

“We are troubled by the administration’s continued reliance on tariffs as the tool of choice to address complex international policy challenges,” said Tiffany Smith, the Vice President of Global Trade Policy at the National Foreign Trade Council, which counts giants like Amazon and Google among its board members.

“A perpetual, unilateral tariff on our top trading partners is unlikely to solve the root causes of excess manufacturing capacity and will simply result in added long-term tariff costs for U.S. businesses and consumers.”
