The Nvidia “spy” scandal will accelerate US-China decoupling

This is the latest in a series of periodic letters to clients in which Eurasia Group's practices present their views on forthcoming political and macro developments for their regions or sectors.
16 Nov 2026 17:55 EST
Discover more on the Portal

Dear clients,

As a former career intelligence official and someone who handled the most sensitive intelligence memos for the president at the White House, I’m suspicious of the Nvidia “spy” story. Nonetheless, I think the long-term implications of the Jensen Huang scandal are more significant than many appreciate. Given my background, I thought it would be useful to offer some context on the intelligence side of the story and weigh in on why this scandal will outlast the news cycle.

The story doesn’t hold up

Most of the press descriptions of Alice Huang and her relationship to the Chinese government misunderstand how intelligence works. The differences among a “spy,” a paid intelligence “asset,” and someone coerced by a security service are critically important. A spy, in the strict sense, is a trained intelligence officer working for a foreign service, often undercover. An asset is someone recruited by that service to provide information or access, usually in exchange for money. A coerced individual is someone the security services pressure into cooperating, typically by threatening their business, their family, or their freedom. In my experience, intelligence coercion is common in China, and it probably explains the Alice Huang case. Let me unpack my logic.

First, no career intelligence officer would post photos all over WeChat or pose happily for photographers. Alice Huang also had a meandering career, which doesn’t fit the profile of a career spy. Chinese media indicate that she trained as a nurse and worked in television. She also held roles at a video game company, a bank, and a hedge fund. During the 2010s, she made a lot of money, including $145 million from the sale of a video game company to the Chinese game firm 7Road in 2019.

Beijing likely pressured Alice Huang into cooperating

In my experience, it’s unlikely that someone with so much money would be susceptible to recruitment as an asset. Pressure is a more plausible explanation. At some point, the Chinese intelligence services likely realized that they could use her to get to Jensen Huang, and by extension, President Donald Trump. She was wealthy, well-connected internationally, and able to help them procure the US’s most advanced and restricted microchips.

Her business dealings depended on the goodwill of Chinese regulators. China’s 2017 National Intelligence Law requires citizens and organizations to cooperate with intelligence work when asked, and someone whose business depends on access to China would find it very difficult to refuse.

The activities of her husband and business partner, Kang Wei, who introduced her to Jensen Huang, further support my theory. He and his firm have been fined by Chinese securities regulators, and he was detained by Shanghai police earlier this year. Why would the Chinese government do this to someone whose wife was a spy, or even a paid asset? It’s more likely the Chinese intelligence service approached the two of them in the early 2020s, after they had accumulated wealth and an international network and had become vulnerable to pressure from Chinese regulators. Kang’s detention and fines may have been part of this pressure campaign.

By March 2023, only months after the US imposed broad restrictions on exporting advanced chips to China, Alice Huang took control of a dormant Singapore spinoff of 7Road and renamed it Megaspeed. By that fall, the company had become an Nvidia Cloud Partner, which gave it preferred access to scarce chips.

Over the following year, Megaspeed’s assets grew from roughly $33 million to about $3 billion. Growth of that speed and scale is very hard to explain without a large backer in China, and the Chinese state is an obvious candidate. To me, the likeliest explanation is that Beijing’s security services exploited Alice Huang to help China get access to Nvidia chips. That matters beyond this case. It has long been an open secret that US chips are smuggled into China, but Megaspeed suggests that Beijing’s security services are at least aware of, if not directing, these smuggling operations.

The degree to which she cooperated willingly or under pressure may never be fully clear. Even so, the episode will have long-term consequences for US export-control enforcement, Washington’s view of China as a threat, and the way both Democrats and Republicans approach China in the coming years.

What does this mean for US-China relations in the year ahead?

To unpack the broader implications of this scandal, I turned to Gerard DiPippo, who covered China at the CIA for many years. I’ve known him since my first day at the CIA, and I feel very fortunate that he recently joined Eurasia Group as our director for Global Macro. Few people combine his depth on Chinese decision-making with his understanding of economics, and our clients will benefit greatly from having him on the team.

Gerard and I have combed through pending legislation in Congress, a new rule from the Commerce Department, and China’s likely response. Our bottom line is that the scandal is already speeding up Washington’s push to close the gaps that let China use US AI chips, especially chips its firms rent abroad, and Beijing will hit back once Chinese firms start losing access. This will pose threats to Nvidia and its suppliers, hurt the countries that host China’s computing power, and could threaten the US-China truce in 2027.

In effect, the scandal brings forward some of the tightening we had expected from Trump’s successor after 2028. Our long-term forecast assumed that Trump’s transactional approach and wariness of Chinese retaliation would restrain US policy for the rest of his term, and that the next administration would be less accommodating of Beijing (see: Managed decoupling will define US-China relations through 2031, 27 August 2026). Though Trump still wants to preserve bilateral stability, the balance of power within his administration has shifted toward the China hawks. Commerce has already issued a new rule this week on restricting chips abroad that was “written by and at the direction of” the Treasury Department and the National Security Council, a sign that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s standing has weakened.

Even so, the rule leaves Trump in control of enforcement. Commerce will review licenses on a case-by-case basis, so the White House can tighten or loosen the rule depending on how talks with Xi Jinping go, and Trump still wants a stable truce. However, if Congress writes the new controls into law, Trump will find them harder to trade away in a deal with Xi, and the next president will inherit them. The risks now tilt toward faster decoupling.

Are Nvidia chips really that important?

Most Americans are discovering, through coverage of the Jensen Huang scandal, that China’s dependence on advanced Nvidia chips to train its AI models is substantial. China’s leading models are still trained on Nvidia chips; Chinese chips mostly run models rather than train them (see: A world run on Chinese AI models and US chips, 19 October 2026). By our estimate, Nvidia chips will supply roughly 60% of the AI computing power Chinese firms can use by the end of this year. Most of that, about 44% of China’s total, comes through smuggling or third-country access. That is why China hawks have been sounding the alarm that the US is failing to prevent chips from reaching China.

Nvidia has now lost the benefit of the doubt in Washington

Jensen Huang’s argument, which won over Trump, was that selling chips to China keeps Chinese firms dependent on American technology. That argument is much harder to make now. Meanwhile, the doubts about Nvidia’s compliance continue to mount. Megaspeed arranged to send nearly $12 billion of Nvidia chips to China. Prosecutors in the US and Asia have also brought about half a dozen other chip-smuggling cases in the past year. A month after the Megaspeed investigation became public, Nvidia suspiciously stopped breaking out sales booked in Singapore. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent now asks whether Nvidia had a “culture of compliance” and whether the failure extended to the C-suite. Nvidia says about 7% of its revenue comes from Chinese customers; outside estimates that count chips rented abroad or smuggled run as high as 10%–20%.

The debate now is about how far and how fast the US should tighten controls

Trump has defended Jensen Huang personally, but the White House is distancing itself from Nvidia, and congressional leaders in both parties now back tighter controls. Three chip bills will probably make it into the final defense bill next month. The question is how much lawmakers will water them down and how authorities will enforce them if they become law (see: Nvidia scandal increases odds of stronger export controls, 9 November 2026).

The measures that would hurt China most are curbs on its access to US chips abroad and to chipmaking tools—two distinct fronts in the US-China AI competition. The AI Overwatch Act would limit chip sales to China, banning the most advanced chips and attaching conditions to others (like the H200, mostly blocked by Beijing anyway). The Chip Security Act would require US chipmakers to verify, through audits and software checks, that their chips end up where they are supposed to, which would slow smuggling over time but would not affect chips already in China. The MATCH Act would cut off chipmaking tools to China’s leading fabs, including those run by SMIC, Huawei, and memory makers CXMT and YMTC. It would hurt China the most but take the longest, because Commerce would have months to write the rules.

The administration is trying to get ahead of the congressional debate. Commerce’s 16 November rule, which takes effect once it is published in the Federal Register, requires licenses for US chips going to any data center abroad that rents Chinese firms more computing power than about 760 Nvidia H100s. It will also apply to all advanced chips going to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil. Commerce will review licenses on a case-by-case basis; existing hosts will have 90 days to get them; and US data centers will not be covered. Those licenses—as long as they’re enforced—would be the most immediate brake on China’s AI training. The rule also makes it harder for TSMC and Samsung to make advanced chips for Chinese chip designers.

Lawmakers can push measures they know will provoke Beijing because they won’t bear the consequences. They get credit for being tough on China, while the costs of any Chinese retaliation fall on companies and on the administration, which has to manage the truce with Beijing. Those incentives will grow in January, when Democrats take control of both chambers. They will want to look tough on China and to raise the costs for Trump

Beijing will not respond immediately, but it could hit back hard

Beijing wants a smooth APEC summit on 18-19 November and a stable truce, and Chinese firms still want US chips. Commerce’s rule also gives Beijing time: Existing hosts have until mid-February to get licenses. If the bills in Congress pass as part of the defense bill, we would expect Beijing to retaliate narrowly, then escalate.

Once the rule takes effect, Beijing could cut specific US firms off from Chinese supplies and slow its rare-earth export licenses. If Commerce then denies licenses to hosts already training Chinese models, Beijing would mount a faster and harsher response, possibly including penalties on data center hosts that drop Chinese customers.

If Congress cuts off chipmaking tools through the MATCH Act—and the White House enforces it—Beijing would hit back hardest, putting the truce at serious risk, because the measure threatens China’s own chipmaking. Beijing has already warned it would respond, likely by curbing mineral exports and pressuring allied toolmakers, as in the Nexperia dispute (see: China’s growing economic coercion toolkit, 16 July 2026). Those curbs would also slow the US AI buildout, which relies on Chinese indium, yttrium, and specialized magnets (see: China expands rare earths export control leverage, 27 July 2026).

Southeast Asia is now caught in the crossfire

The region hosts about three-quarters of the computing power Chinese firms use abroad, and Commerce’s rule now requires licenses for advanced chips going to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia unless the buyer is a US firm. Malaysia has the most at stake: Aolani Cloud, a Singapore-based host whose anchor customer is ByteDance, had about $2.5 billion of AI hardware there by early this year. Hosts such as Aolani are squeezed from both sides. They now need US licenses for new chips, while also risking Chinese penalties if they cut off Chinese customers (see: ASEAN countries will tighten rules for new data centers, 2 November 2026).

The MATCH Act would hit US, Japanese, and Dutch toolmakers

China accounts for 28%–35% of the sales of the three largest US toolmakers, Applied Materials, Lam Research, and KLA. It accounts for more than one-third of Tokyo Electron’s sales and about one-fifth of ASML’s. Over time, the MATCH Act would force these companies to surrender market share to Chinese rivals such as Naura, a broad-line toolmaker, and AMEC, an etch specialist. If the Netherlands and Japan match US controls, Beijing could retaliate against them, and they have fewer ways to respond than Washington. The winners would be chipmakers such as TSMC, Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron, who would benefit from the increased difficulties of Chinese manufacturers.

Do these developments change Eurasia Group’s basecase on US-China relations next year?

Eurasia Group still believes that the US and China will continue along a path of managed stability through next year. But the extraordinary publicity of the Jensen Huang scandal, and the extensive involvement of the Chinese security services, will make it more difficult for US lawmakers, Trump administration officials, and public servants to maintain a more relaxed approach to export control enforcement. The new Commerce rule is the first sign of this shift. Moreover, companies in industries exposed to US export controls will likely proceed with more caution, adhering more closely to enacted rules, even if the Trump administration is not enforcing them. The direction of US policy is toward tightening, and that will have a chilling effect on firms that were betting on continued acquiescence from Trump and Lutnick.

Marc Gustafson and Gerard DiPippo
