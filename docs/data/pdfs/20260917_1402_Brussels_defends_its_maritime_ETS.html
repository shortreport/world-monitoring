DRIVING THE DAY

BRUSSELS DEFENDS ITS MARITIME ETS: The European Commission is defending a deliberately narrow approach to its new maritime ETS support mechanism, arguing that scarce public money should focus first on closing the price gap between conventional and sustainable shipping fuels rather than trying to subsidize every green technology.

Why fuels get priority: Speaking at an event on Tuesday, Polona Gregorin, head of DG CLIMA’s unit for air, rail, water and intermodal policy, said the proposed SMAP mechanism is meant to target areas where the carbon price alone does not provide enough incentive. “You absolutely do need to bridge the price gap between … a much more expensive sustainable fuel and conventional [fuel],” she said.

Reminder: SMAP — the proposed Sustainable Maritime Alternative Fuels and Propulsion support mechanism — is the Commission’s maritime decarbonization scheme funded through the revised ETS. The Commission is proposing 110 million allowances, potentially worth around €15 billion through 2040, with the mechanism designed primarily to narrow the price gap between conventional and sustainable shipping fuels.

Energy efficiency left out: Energy-efficiency projects — such as hull, engine or retrofit improvements that cut the amount of fuel a ship uses — are not covered by SMAP. “Energy efficiency is not supported. That’s true,” Gregorin said. But she stressed that this was a choice to keep the mechanism focused and manageable, not a judgment that efficiency measures do not matter. “That is not to say that energy efficiency is not important — on the contrary, it’s very, very important. But there might be better ways to support investment.”

The Commission’s logic: Efficiency measures can already produce direct savings by cutting fuel use, whereas cleaner fuels remain significantly more expensive and therefore need additional support. As Gregorin put it: “You use less fuel — it’s a win-win.”

Industry pushback: Shipbuilding and shipping representatives have argued that the scheme should cast a wider net and include more technologies, retrofits and energy-efficiency projects. Gregorin’s response was that every additional category would spread a limited pot of money more thinly and make the mechanism more complex to administer.

That’s fair: Sotiris Raptis, secretary-general of European Shipowners (ECSA), said provisional in-house analysis suggests the sector would need twice the money currently earmarked just to meet the FuelEU Maritime sub-target alone. “More money should be earmarked for sustainable fuels and clean tech,” he said, arguing that the Commission had taken a first step but that Parliament and Council now need to go further.

Timing: Gregorin also said the file could move unusually fast under the Irish presidency, with the aim of wrapping up the Council and European Parliament work before the end of 2026 and concluding negotiations by March next year. She acknowledged that would be “quite unprecedented,” but added that, while the timetable is ambitious, capitals and lawmakers are already working at speed because several provisions are due to kick in from 2028.

STATE OF THE UNION

TRANSPORT OMNIBUS CONFIRMED: The European Commission has formally put a transport simplification package on its 2027 agenda, confirming the direction of travel POLITICO first reported in May when DG MOVE began preparing a sector-by-sector review of EU transport red tape. The package appears in Ursula von der Leyen’s State of the Union letter of intent under the competitiveness and jobs heading.

As we scooped: DG MOVE’s June “Reality Check” workshops brought together aviation, maritime, rail and road stakeholders to identify EU rules creating unnecessary administrative burdens and to flag where requirements could be simplified or digitized. The Commission said the exercise was intended to collect first-hand evidence from practitioners on obstacles, unintended consequences and disproportionate compliance costs. The workshops took place on June 15 and 16, with aviation, maritime and inland navigation first, followed by road and rail.

What Brussels was looking for: Stakeholders were asked to point to pain points around reporting obligations, authorizations, permits, certifications, registrations, inspections and subsidy applications, as well as cases where companies are submitting information authorities already hold elsewhere. DG MOVE also wanted examples of where digital tools could eliminate duplicate paperwork.

Sector pain points: In aviation and maritime, the review was expected to pick up complaints around emissions reporting, fuel-compliance systems, safety certification and overlaps between EU and international rules. Rail and road groups, meanwhile, have long raised concerns around permitting, technical requirements, interoperability and freight documentation.

Now it becomes a package: What was initially a technical stocktake is now feeding into a formal simplification initiative. The State of the Union letter does not yet spell out which regulations or directives will be reopened, so the scope of the omnibus remains unclear. But its inclusion in the 2027 work program means transport is now explicitly part of the Commission’s broader push to cut burdens on business.

Background: Brussels has set itself a wider target of cutting administrative burdens by at least 25% for companies and 35% for SMEs, and transport operators have been among the sectors pushing hardest for relief. The political fight now will be over whether the omnibus mostly tidies reporting and paperwork — or becomes an opportunity to reopen substantive sectoral rules that industry has been lobbying to change.

STATE OF THE UNION LANDS WITH A WHISPER: You can’t say we didn’t warn you. While Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s speeches in years past have been used to launch the small car initiative or announce an anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese EVs, this year’s barely even touched upon competitiveness.

What it missed: The president made some remarks about tackling Chinese exports but continued her line that “this is why we are engaged in a dialogue with China.” Automakers hoping to get a hint as to which countries would make the trusted partner list under the Industrial Accelerator Act were sorely disappointed.

What it did include: The speech announced more actions to stockpile critical raw materials needed for electric vehicles and the batteries that power them. The Commission will establish a new corporation tasked with coordinating purchases and building a stockpile of the goods.

Unimpressed: The German car lobby VDA came out swinging against the speech, saying in a statement to media that von der Leyen “presented little to no new, concrete solutions or courses of action” to help make the bloc’s industries more competitive. “Concrete answers were particularly needed for the German and European automotive industry—one of the most important key sectors. Yet this did not happen,” VDA President Hildegard Müller said in the statement.

Some agreement: The one thing both the far right and the left can agree on is a new for local content requirements. French MEP Jordan Bardella, chair of the far-right Patriots, said that public money should only go to companies employing European workers. “We need to make European public procurement a genuine weapon like China or the U.S.,” French MEP Valérie Hayer, head of the Renew group, said in her remarks after von der Leyen.

Missing a backbone: “We adopted a Green Deal. Let’s stop unravelling it and implement it, for goodness sake,” Hayer said. Much of von der Leyen’s speech focused on the climate and massive wildfires from the summer.

Promises to keep: The EPP’s Manfred Weber, meanwhile, used climate to reiterate the party’s campaign promise to “stop the ban on the combustion engine,” referring to the legislation that previously mandated the sale of a 100 percent zero-emission vehicles starting in 2035. The Commission’s proposed reform that is currently under negotiations in the institutions would allow powertrains past 2035 but with various emission offsets.

CARS

EPP NEGOTIATIONS ON IAA: The center-right European People’s Party is meeting at 9 a.m. today to discuss the Industrial Accelerator Act. The party’s national delegations submitted their thoughts on the Commission’s grand industrial strategy. EPP leaders will have plenty to comb through today.

Reminder: We scooped the contributions from 12 member states earlier this month. Finding a common position in Brussels won’t be an easy task. The national delegations are split over everything from whether the IAA should have a binding manufacturing target to which products should be included. Catch up on that here.

Does it matter? None of the file’s rapporteurs in Parliament are from the EPP, but it is the largest group in the institution, meaning its votes will carry weight.

Tick tock: A committee vote on the IAA is scheduled for Dec. 1 with a possible plenary vote in mid-December, giving policymakers little time to reach an agreement. When asked when the EPP could come to a common stance, an MEP told Jordyn it would be before Christmas.
