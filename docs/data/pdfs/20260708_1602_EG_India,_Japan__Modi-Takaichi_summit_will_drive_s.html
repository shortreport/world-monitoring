India, Japan

08 Jul 2026 03:01 EDT

  
 
 

Modi-Takaichi summit will drive spurt of government-backed joint ventures

 
  
 
 
 
  • Japanese Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae’s first visit to India operationalized bilateral economic security initiatives designed to hedge against uncertainties in relations with both the US and China.
  • With the energy crisis top of mind, the bilateral cooperation will deliver Japanese support to kickstart India’s green hydrogen and biofuel ambitions.
  • After a modest start, increasing signs of success in industrial projects are encouraging more ambitious plans in the areas of AI, space, and financial regulation.
 
 
 
 

Bilateral industrial policy is designed to hedge against US and China uncertainties. India and Japan deepened and expanded last year’s Economic Security Initiative (ESI) during Takaichi’s visit to India on 1-3 July. The original strategic rationale for the ESI was a shared concern over Chinese hostility; a new preoccupation with increasing US unreliability has reinforced its mission. Both India and Japan have been seeking to hedge by developing bilateral supply chains and investment structures. While both are pursuing similar initiatives with other so-called middle power countries, the Japanese relationship is especially important for India. The original ESI agreement, signed last August, targeted cooperation in four supply chains. By December, that number had increased to six. It has now reached double digits.

While the two leaders made the expected comments about the importance of the Indo-Pacific region and the Quad grouping, Modi and Takaichi prioritized implementing the many agreements signed over the past year. In the months ahead of the meeting, Japanese officials carefully reviewed India’s industrial sectors and supply chains, identified gaps, and moved to address those gaps with a mixture of government assistance and coordinated business partnerships. At the summit, Indian and Japanese officials were relieved that Modi and Takaichi developed a good rapport, given the latter’s dislike for showmanship and her straight-talking habits.

Gulf energy crisis prompts focus on Indian energy transition. Following the Iran war shock, the two governments have prioritized energy security. India sought help in two areas: infrastructure for petroleum reserves and financing for long-gestation projects in green fuels. At the summit, they negotiated a roadmap to implement existing plans to promote business partnerships in green mobility—especially electric vehicles, batteries, and green shipping. Japan also agreed to provide financial and technological assistance to industrialize India's biogas and biofuel sector, moving it beyond small-scale, decentralized production, along with a green methanol project in eastern India. The two sides also struck a deal to provide India with two very large ethane-carrying ships—a specialized type of shipping dominated by Japanese firms—initially on lease, with a path toward joint ownership.

India has struggled to advance its green hydrogen ambitions because of high capital costs, uncertain demand, and technological barriers. These challenges concern New Delhi, which needs reliable green energy sources to support future heavy industry exports. As a first step, Japan will finance and build a green ammonia plant and then serve as a guaranteed buyer of the output. Japan will also help India develop its strategic petroleum reserve system and emergency stockpiling mechanisms.

Cooperation will move beyond manufacturing to AI governance and financial regulation. Japan is confident it has developed a functional approach for a bilateral industrial policy under which the two governments identify business partners that can work together to address gaps in India’s manufacturing capacity. For example, an early ESI project targets one of the most persistent gaps in India’s manufacturing capacity. India has set up almost the entire cycle for solar cell production, but it remains dependent on China for some key components. Over the past year, Tokyo scouted for Japanese firms that have similar technologies, including for wafer ingot production, and then matched them with Indian companies.

This summit identified life sciences, AI, frontier science technologies, and even lunar exploration as additional areas for commercial and technological cooperation. Such plans seem aspirational at a time when some original ESI sectoral agreements, such as pharmaceuticals and defense, remain nascent. However, the announcements are partly designed to force Japanese firms to take a closer look at opportunities in India. Small- and medium-sized Japanese firms continue to see India as a difficult business environment.

The summit showed that the ESI is slowly climbing the value ladder. In semiconductors, the initial ESI plan focused on simpler opportunities such as legacy chips and chip assembly plants. The latest summit produced joint ventures in semiconductor materials, one of the most sophisticated parts of the ecosystem. In addition, the two sides are working on strategies to collaborate in third countries—for example, on critical mineral mining and downstream energy infrastructure in Africa and Latin America—and to align on AI governance and financial regulation.

Explanation of Political Trajectories (ie, “Trajectories”)

Eurasia Group's (EG) Political Trajectories are indicators of the net impact of political factors on the macro business environment in a country over specific timeframes. For each country, two Trajectories are provided, a short-term (6 months) and a long term assessment (24 months). Trajectories are assigned by Eurasia Group’s country analysts through a structured qualitative process. Each Trajectory represents a net assessment, indicating how political changes in a country impact the macro business environment, defined as the economic and investment climate in the country. Once initiated, Trajectories are updated both periodically and as events warrant. Trajectories may be suspended from time to time if there is no active analyst opinion or analyst coverage, but the opinion or coverage is expected to resume. Trajectories can be:

Positive: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a positive impact on the macro business environment

Neutral: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a neutral or negligible impact on the macro business environment

Negative: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a negative impact on the macro business environment

Disclaimers: Trajectories are solely for informational purposes and should not be relied upon as investment advice. Trajectories do not indicate asset price movements, and have no value in forecasting market prices. Trajectories are based on data and analysis we deem to be reliable; they are not guaranteed as to accuracy or completeness. The Trajectories indicate the political outlook in a single country, over time. Trajectories do not address sector-specific or city/regional political risks that are often different than the country-level outlook. Trajectories represent an analyst’s net qualitative assessment of a country's political outlook. EG sales, analytic staff and products may provide analysis reflecting different opinions than those expressed by the Trajectories.
