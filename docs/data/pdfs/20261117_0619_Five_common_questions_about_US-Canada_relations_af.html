Five common questions about US-Canada relations after the midterms
16 Nov 2026 16:16 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 
 
  Listen now · 6 min
  • Democrats’ resounding midterm victory does not shift our view that negotiations on a limited US-Canada trade deal are likely to drag into 2027, as the decision on whether to spend political capital to secure a measure of economic relief from a trade deal ultimately still rests with Prime Minister Mark Carney.
  • Democrats in the next Congress will pass symbolic measures aimed at opposing tariffs on Canada but have no viable path to reclaiming trade authority from the executive branch, leaving the White House in the driver’s seat.
  • Although some Republicans in a more empowered non-MAGA wing of the party will likely chart a more moderate course on the US-Canada relationship ahead of the 2028 election, Canadian public opinion toward the US is unlikely to significantly change under the current administration.

Since the US midterm elections, Eurasia Group has received many client inquiries on the implications for US-Canada relations. Below we examine five key areas of interest.

What do the midterms mean for US-Canada trade talks?

We remain skeptical that the US and Canada will reach a limited trade deal before the end of 2026 (40% odds). Although negotiating teams have resumed talks, political hurdles should temper expectations of a deal. An agreement before the end of the year would not surprise us, but our basecase is that negotiations drag into 2027 (see: Zombie USMCA will endure through Trump’s term, 7 October 2026).

On the Canadian side, Carney must explain to a skeptical public how a deal now would improve on the terms he rejected in August. Some officials oppose any deal that accepts US tariffs on Canadian goods, especially autos, under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement. They believe Carney should wait for the new Congress to put additional pressure on the administration to change its approach. Others argue that President Donald Trump’s administration has already shown flexibility and that economic pain from Section 232 tariffs must be mitigated. The decision to accept a potential deal to achieve a measure of economic relief, even if it leaves some auto tariffs in place, would likely cost Carney several points in public opinion.

Do the midterms shift the long-term political calculus on either side?

On the US side, probably not. Affordability pressures contributed to the Republican defeat, but that is not how the White House sees it. Trump is therefore unlikely to pivot to a more conciliatory posture. The more relevant watchpoint is whether Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is pushed out or otherwise loses influence in the White House over the Nvidia scandal (see: “Nvidia scandal will threaten Howard Lutnick’s job security,” US Daily, 13 November 2026). Lutnick, who administers Section 232, has been a significant impediment to reducing auto tariffs. His departure would probably strengthen voices more inclined toward a deal such as US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, whom Ottawa views as a good-faith negotiating partner.

Canadian officials privately view the Democratic victory as vindication of their strategy of seeking to delay a deal until after the midterms, believing that a Republican defeat would strengthen Canada's negotiating position. Ottawa has cultivated a broad network on Capitol Hill, in state legislatures, in governors' mansions, and with local business associations, which officials hope will bolster the case for cross-border trade going forward. That long-standing strategy for managing relations with the US, which pre-dates the Trump administration, is unlikely to change, although the White House will continue to drive US trade policy.

What can the Democrats do to shift US trade policy?

Probably not much of substance. Reclaiming Congress’s trade authorities from the executive branch would require two-thirds majorities in both chambers to override a presidential veto.

Democrats will probably focus on symbolic measures. A resolution condemning the Canada tariffs would likely sail through the House and could gather enough Republicans in the Senate—Democrats would only need four—to overcome the filibuster. But it would be vetoed by the president, and it is hard to see eleven Republicans voting to override that veto.

Democrats could also make more substantive moves. They have two plausible options:

  • Attempt to end the Section 301 or 338 tariffs through the Congressional Review Act (CRA), which allows Congress to overturn recently imposed rules. (It is not clear tariffs count as a “rule” for this purpose.) A CRA resolution would pass both chambers—it is exempt from the Senate filibuster—but would be vetoed.
  • Pass a measure carving out Canada (and potentially other countries) from Section 301/338 authorities, short of repealing those laws entirely. This would also be subject to a veto and be unlikely to pass.

Will Republicans adjust course on Canada?

The party is likely to keep following Trump’s line, at least for now. But Republicans became pro-tariff largely because elected officials needed to follow Trump’s lead; as his influence diminishes, figures such as Senator Ted Cruz (R-TX) or Florida Governor Ron DeSantis will probably adopt more moderate stances ahead of the 2028 election. Cruz has already blamed the Canada tariffs in part for the midterm defeat.

The non-Trump GOP stance will probably coalesce around ending Section 301 and 338 tariffs and moving towards a long-term trading relationship with substantially reduced (though not eliminated) sectoral tariffs. Importantly, the tone will change from bellicosity toward more constructive engagement.

How will the midterms change the outlook for US companies operating in Canada?

Canadians broadly welcomed the midterm results and are cautiously optimistic they will make it easier to reach a trade deal. Deteriorating sentiment toward the US has likely bottomed, though it will not recover quickly; a new US administration is likely necessary for any significant positive shift. Grassroots consumer boycotts of US products where reasonable substitutes exist are likely to continue for the foreseeable future. The Carney government, moreover, will continue with efforts to hedge Canada's dependence on the US (see: Carney’s variable geometry will supplement, not replace, Canada’s US ties, 26 March 2026).
