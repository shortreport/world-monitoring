APEC Leaders' Summit outcomes

20 Nov 2026 14:02 EST
Listen to the recording
  • The APEC summit produced no material US-China deliverables; the anticipated AI dialogue did not occur, the Busan trade truce was not extended, and the Nvidia/Mega Speed fallout has injected significant new risk into the bilateral relationship.
  • Four pieces of export control legislation are now highly likely to pass in the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA); China is expected to respond with graduated countermeasures, potentially including rare earth export slowdowns and merger interference.
  • The Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral reinforced Trump's preference for personal diplomacy but yielded no concrete outcomes; a Ukraine ceasefire remains unlikely given unchanged Russian negotiating positions.

US-China: No Deliverables, Elevated Risk

The Trump-Xi meeting at APEC produced no substantive outcomes. The AI safety dialogue — pledged since May and reaffirmed in September — did not occur. The Busan trade truce, in effect since October 2025 and already extended by only two months to a 10 January renewal deadline, was not extended at APEC. The Board of Trade met on 6 November but produced no specific tariff reductions; its only concrete output was an agricultural working group. There were no new commitments on energy, aviation, or agricultural purchases, and no Bessent-He meeting took place.

The primary disruptor was the Nvidia/Mega Speed fallout, which energized Congressional hawks and prompted a Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) remote access rule — restricting Chinese access to offshore compute at data centers — that dropped the same day as the summit. David Meale noted that Chinese officials told Eurasia Group directly this week that they are "watching, very concerned and ready to pursue countermeasures." The base case remains US-China stability — Meale described it as "yellow light mode" — with the Busan truce expected to be extended eventually, but the probability of a breakdown is higher than before.

Export Controls: Legislation Now Highly Likely

Heather West assessed that three bills — the Overwatch Act, the Match Act, and the Chip Security Act — are very likely to be included in the NDAA. A fourth, the Remote Access Security Act (RASA), which would legislatively extend export controls to foreign remote and cloud access, was previously considered near-impossible to include but is now a genuine possibility given the shift in Congressional tone.

Clayton Allen framed the key variable as a binary: does the White House retain enforcement discretion? His view is that it will, meaning the laws will exist but enforcement will be selectively applied — at least under the current administration. However, he flagged that corporate compliance is already running ahead of enforcement, and that the next president is likely to take a more aggressive posture. Meale noted current real-world effects: a Moonshot-Indonesia data deal has fallen through, TSMC and Samsung are withholding certain chips, and Tencent has delayed its next model.

China's Likely Response

Meale outlined a graduated Chinese response: signaling displeasure now (already underway via withheld dialogues and official warnings), potentially allowing the Busan truce to dangle into January, then escalating via rare earth export slowdowns and interference with foreign mergers where China's state assets regulator has jurisdiction. He described the US position as structurally weak — during the Liberation Day fallout, the US ultimately backed down — and noted that Bessent's increasingly hard rhetoric may reflect a view that US-China trade flows are already performing poorly enough that there is "not much to lose."

Russia-Ukraine and the Trilateral

The Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral elevated Putin rhetorically but produced no deliverables. Clayton Allen noted Trump appeared more aligned with Russia than some of his own negotiators. Allen assessed a ceasefire as unlikely in the near term: Russia continues to demand caps on Ukraine's military size, additional territorial concessions, and limits on any peacekeeping mechanism — all of which are incompatible with a durable settlement. Jared Kushner's floating of Article 5-like guarantees for Ukraine (not NATO membership) was assessed as unlikely to advance, partly because Russia and the West define such guarantees differently. Allen advised clients to watch Russian negotiating positions — not US envoy statements — for genuine signals of movement.

Japan: Sidelined and Concerned

Jeremy Chan assessed that Prime Minister Takaichi failed to secure a bilateral meeting with Trump at ASEAN (rating her performance a "6 out of 10"), and that China-Japan tensions will remain elevated for at least the duration of her tenure, which Eurasia Group estimates extends through at least summer 2028. Japan was not consulted before the trilateral and was effectively named in Chinese talking points framing the summit as a gathering of the World War II victors. Chan flagged that a meaningful curtailment of rare earth flows to Japan would flow through to US companies dependent on Japanese-manufactured magnets — which Tokyo views as its best source of leverage against Beijing.

Tech Controls: Third-Country Spillover

West noted that Southeast Asian data center hosts — Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, Singapore, and Brazil — are caught between US licensing requirements and Chinese customer relationships. Singapore in particular is "fairly grumpy" about the remote access rule despite considering itself a rule-follower. West estimated China will lose approximately 75% of its offshore compute and roughly half of its advanced training compute as a result of these restrictions. Domestic Chinese chip suppliers were identified as the primary winners. Chan added that companies integrated into the AI supply chain globally should be preparing for a further decoupling scenario and building capacity to serve two distinct markets.

Key Watch Items

  1. Busan trade truce renewal (10 January deadline): Whether China allows it to lapse or demands concessions is the most proximate near-term tripwire for bilateral escalation.
  2. NDAA passage and RASA inclusion: Watch whether RASA makes it into the final bill and how much enforcement discretion the White House retains across all four pieces of legislation.
  3. Rare earth export flows: Early signals of slowdowns — particularly to Japan — would indicate China has moved from signaling to active countermeasures.
  4. G20 in Florida (mid-December): The next scheduled Trump-Xi meeting; watch for movement on AI dialogue, Busan extension, and diplomatic channel-building ahead of 2027.
  5. Russian negotiating position on Ukraine: Changes in Russian demands on Ukrainian military caps, territorial concessions, or peacekeeping mechanisms are the real indicators of ceasefire viability — not US envoy statements.
  6. Corporate compliance behavior: Even absent US enforcement, watch whether company actions (deal cancellations, chip withholding, model delays) accelerate decoupling faster than either government intends.

What Clients Are Asking

AI dialogue collapse and what comes next: Clients wanted to understand why the AI safety dialogue failed and whether it can be revived. Speakers attributed the failure directly to Chinese objections to the BIS remote access rule and Congressional export control momentum; near-term revival is unlikely without resolution of those underlying tensions.

Taiwan arms sales: Clients asked whether Taiwan came up in Trump-Xi discussions. Meale confirmed it did, based on the Chinese readout, which included a warning to Trump to "do the right thing." No new arms sale commitments were announced; the Chinese appear willing to accept smaller, phased sales in 2027 rather than a single $14 billion package.

China's retaliation toolkit beyond rare earths: Clients pressed on what specific tools China would use. Meale outlined investigations targeting foreign companies' supply chain presence in China, enforcement of decrees on compliance with foreign economic restrictions, and merger interference for deals involving companies that do business in China — even if neither party is Chinese.

Third-country investor exposure (Southeast Asia): Clients asked what foreign investors in Chinese-adjacent markets should watch. West and Chan both flagged that Southeast Asian data center operators face a structural dilemma between US licensing requirements and Chinese customer retention. Chan advised investors with AI supply chain exposure to begin modeling a two-market decoupling scenario.

Russia-Ukraine ceasefire prospects: Clients asked whether Kushner's Article 5-like proposal signals real progress. Allen was direct: it does not. The proposal reflects definitional confusion between Western and Russian interpretations of security guarantees, and Russia's core demands remain unchanged.
