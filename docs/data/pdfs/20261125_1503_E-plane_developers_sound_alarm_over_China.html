DRIVING THE DAY

COMAC’S E-FLIGHT HEATS UP GLOBAL RACE TO ELECTRIC AIRCRAFT: European electric aircraft developers are feeling the heat after the Chinese state-owned manufacturer COMAC announced the first piloted flight of a 19-seater electric aircraft earlier this month.

Certification matters: The electric aircraft will need a type certificate from the Civil Aviation Administration of China to operate domestic routes and, after that, approval from the EU Aviation Safety Agency to fly in Europe. But the EU sector is warning that the future of European aircraft manufacturing is increasingly threatened by competition from the strategically subsidized Chinese companies and startup-friendly U.S. Tommaso has more here.

Too slow to take off: Alessia Affinito, EU public affairs manager at Væridion, a Germany-based electric aircraft developer, warned that EU funds for green aviation take too long to reach beneficiaries. A Væridion project was granted almost €100 million by the EU Innovation Fund, but “from the moment you have positive feedback, to the moment that you sign the agreement, to the moment when you get the first batch of money, it can take years,” Affinito told Tommaso.

Too risky: “Not to mention that sometimes some funding can be risk-averse, so they don’t want to go for the most innovative project,” she added, noting that such an approach makes it harder to compete globally.

From EVs to e-planes? Marina Efthymiou, professor of aviation and lead of the Sustainable Aviation Hub at Dublin City University, recalled that China’s success in electric cars over its European counterparts rested on several factors: “A huge domestic market, short product cycles, aggressive local industrial policy, and above all a battery supply chain that drove costs down faster than anyone in Europe or the U.S. expected.”

In aviation, by contrast, “Europe’s leadership rests on three things: Airbus’ position in large aircraft, a deep engine and systems supply chain, and a regulator, EASA, whose signature is trusted worldwide,” Efthymiou told Tommaso. These factors work in favor of the European industry, but its future leadership can’t be taken for granted.

Game-changing tech: The shift from aircraft powered by conventional jet fuel to electric alternatives could be a turning point. “In a conventional aircraft, the strategic and critical part is the engine, and that remains a Western stronghold,” Efthymiou said, noting that even the Chinese C919, which is seeking EASA approval to operate in Europe, is powered by French-American CFM engines. “In an electric or hybrid-electric aircraft, a large part of that strategic weight shifts to the battery, the motors and the power electronics. Those are the parts of the supply chain China dominates,” she added.

MARITIME

THE FATE OF INDIAN BEACHING YARDS: Lawmakers will decide tomorrow in plenary whether they support an objection from centrist and leftist parties over the EU’s decision to add two Indian beaching yards to its Ship Recycling Facilities list.

Hold up: There’s been a last-minute concern from lawmakers within the European People’s Party over how it would impact the EU-India free-trade agreement that’s to be signed on Dec. 16.

Reminder: That comes on top of news about the bloc considering a compromise to allow India to continue importing EU scrap metal — albeit with a 15% duty on aluminum exports — to avoid jeopardizing the FTA. Now, the Parliament’s largest ruling party is discussing whether passing the objection will further damage what could potentially be one of the largest free trade deals ever.

Clapback: “If we want to remain credible to our trade partners, we should adhere to the rule of law. That is why we should not break our own environmental laws in order to incentivize and legitimize unfair trade with India,” Greens/EFA MEP Sara Matthieu told POLITICO, highlighting the threat of deindustrialization and loss of scrap metal and jobs for European recyclers and the steel industry. “Instead, we should respect our laws, our industry and the EU-India FTA, which explicitly says that our respective environmental laws cannot be weakened as a result of the agreement,” added Matthieu.

The fine print: In Chapter 16 of the FTA on “Trade and Sustainable Development,” it states that both parties should not waive or derogate from their environmental rules “in order to encourage trade or investment between the parties.”

The EPP side: Angelika Niebler, the chair for the EU-India Delegation, told POLITICO that “it would send a very negative signal to our Indian partners if Parliament now rejects that finding without good reason. India holds enormous potential for the EU, both geopolitically and economically, and so does the EU-India Free Trade Agreement.”

Don’t forget: The German MEP stressed that the Commission confirmed the facilities meet EU standards, and that if anything changes, they can carry out a reassessment.

CARS

GROUPS WARN AGAINST PUSH FOR ALTERNATIVE FUELS: A coalition of 11 groups — including green NGO Transport & Environment and EU consumer group BEUC — sent a letter to the EU’s climate and environment ministers today, warning that the latest negotiations on the Automotive Package are “falling out of touch with reality.”

What’s this about: The Automotive Package, which reforms emission targets for automakers, is being negotiated by the institutions. The compromise draft deal in Parliament established a new category for vehicles running exclusively on alternative fuels. These cars would qualify as low-emission or zero-emission and count toward an automaker’s emission targets.

A distraction: The letter calls the focus on combustion engines running on alternative fuels “distracting from the electric future” and “treating them on par with battery electric vehicles would create a false and unfair equivalence.” The groups claim that under the latest proposals, automakers could meet the emission targets without selling any EVs.

Some hope: The latest compromise text from the Irish presidency makes clear that the EU’s capitals are still divided over the new vehicle category. Italy has consistently pushed for biofuels to be used more by the sector and reflected in the emissions legislation. Germany is an ally in wanting alternative fuels to be included. Other countries say the new category raises “concerns on environmental integrity.”

SCOOP — COUNCIL COMPROMISE TEXT ON IAA: The latest compromise draft from the Irish presidency would allow more foreign-produced vehicles and components to qualify as Made in Europe under the Industrial Accelerator Act.

Vehicle assembly: The latest text would allow vehicles assembled in Switzerland, the U.K. and the three non-EU members of the European Economic Area to qualify as Made in Europe.

Does it matter? The United Kingdom is the only major manufacturer included, as spotted by our German colleague Romanus Otte. Some vans are produced in Switzerland, but Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein boast no manufacturing or assembly factories for passenger cars.

Called out: The latest text highlights the importance of creating a uniform Buy European approach across the EU, arguing the IAA will keep third countries — hi, China — from “forum-shopping” and “exploiting the specific circumstances” of different capitals. Beijing has frequently used a divide-and-conquer approach when it comes to negotiating with the EU through economic investments in different member countries. We have the story here.

RALLY FOR IAA: In Strasbourg, meanwhile, EU factory workers from Italy, Belgium, France and Germany rallied on the freezing esplanade in front of the Parliament building on Tuesday in support of the IAA.

A labor cause: Our colleague Francesca Micheletti was on the scene, reporting that the group held iconic labor symbols like red flags and union signs protesting manufacturing job losses across the bloc. “We need clear Made in EU rules, colleagues, and we will fight for them,” Christiane Benner, head of Germany’s IG Metall trade union, said in a speech.

Faces in the crowd: The three lead lawmakers on the file in the Parliament all appeared at the rally, along with French presidential candidate Raphaël Glucksmann, prompting a flurry of selfies.

EPP skeptical: Inside the well-heated Parliament, lawmakers are deep in talks on the proposal. A vote on a common negotiating position has been given a new tentative deadline of Dec. 14. Some center-right MEPs are far from convinced, however: Jörgen Warborn, a Swedish member of the European People’s Party who co-authored an amendment to reject the bill last month, doubted the mid-December date. “I don’t know if this will hold,” he told POLITICO.

Trade fallout: Warborn said his reservations about the IAA — the impact of the proposal on the bloc’s trade relations — had convinced him to propose its rejection. “We are closing markets with our best allies,” said Warborn, who is the EPP’s coordinator in the Parliament’s trade committee.

TRUCKS

SCOOP — BRITISH TRUCKERS FEAR EU BANS: A British truck driver has been wrongly banned from the EU because of failings in the bloc’s new digital border controls, according to the haulage firm that employs them, raising concerns many more may be affected. The problem is linked to the EU’s Entry/Exit System, which digitally records when non-EU nationals enter and leave the bloc.

Tip of the iceberg: With drivers frequently reporting inconsistent border control checks across the continent, the Road Haulage Association trade body fears thousands more drivers could be at risk of being wrongly banned. Sophie Inge has more here.
