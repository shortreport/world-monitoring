Ceasefire collapse will extend cycle of low-level conflict with Afghanistan

 
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:
 
 
 
 
  • Taliban-affiliated militants and the Pakistani military used an April ceasefire to prepare for renewed fighting, as publicly evidenced by the recent attack on a major Pakistani military installation.
  • The Taliban regime in Afghanistan wants a pause in hostilities, but it has little leverage over its affiliate, the Tehreek-e-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), and, for domestic political reasons, must retaliate against Pakistani cross-border strikes.
  • The TTP and the Pakistani military have no strategy other than coercive action against each other, ensuring that the conflict will continue.
 
 
 
 

Both sides used the ceasefire to prepare for the next round of fighting. A TTP splinter group called the Jamaat-ul-Ahrar has claimed responsibility for the attack on the Pakistani Rangers headquarters in Karachi that killed three people. Although Jamaat-ul-Ahrar has existed since 2014, it operates as a subsidiary of the TTP and has never shown the ability to carry out an attack so far from Afghanistan. Islamabad does not distinguish between the two groups. Pakistan’s strategy to contain the TTP is inherently escalatory. It centers on strikes inside Afghanistan to pressure the Taliban regime in Kabul to rein in the TTP; however, that approach leads to the involvement of Afghan government forces in the attacks on the Pakistani military.

The ceasefire that China mediated in April rested on a shaky premise: that the Taliban regime in Kabul would prevent the TTP from finding sanctuary in Afghanistan. In the end, all sides viewed the ceasefire as little more than a pause in the conflict. Pakistan saw an opportunity to preempt future TTP attacks. The Taliban’s claim that the TTP had accepted the ceasefire was never endorsed by the TTP. By May, both sides had resumed skirmishing, with Pakistani soldiers attacking TTP encampments along the border. The ground fighting intensified through June. The Karachi attack indicates the TTP now treats the ceasefire as defunct and shows little concern about the Taliban regime’s position. An open attack on a heavily protected military installation in Pakistan’s largest city made it impossible for the government to downplay the incident, prompting the airstrikes that followed.

Kabul and Islamabad lack the resources for more than renewed skirmishing. The current round of fighting is unlikely to escalate further, but the TTP will probably resume its attacks on Pakistani security targets. Once those attacks cross a certain threshold—such as bombings in major cities—they will likely trigger limited clashes between Pakistani and Taliban forces. That pattern has held for the past two years and will probably continue (see: Resource constraints will limit conflict with Afghanistan despite intensifying skirmishes, 27 February 2026).  

Of the three parties, the Taliban-led Afghan government is the strongest supporter of a ceasefire but has the least influence over the conflict. Kabul cannot afford conflict when its economy is reeling from severe drought, external funders such as Qatar face financial strain from the Iran war, and the government seeks stronger ties with China—one of the country’s few foreign investors. The Afghan government has limited leverage over the TTP, but it also cannot appear to be backing down to Pakistan. Nonetheless, its response this week was largely symbolic; Pakistan claimed that only four drones were launched. Pakistan also cannot afford a larger conflict. It faces fiscal constraints under its IMF loan regime, along with a hostile India, an unstable border with Iran, and domestic insurgencies.

Pakistan and the TTP see no alternatives to military action. Pakistan has limited its response to TTP attacks to airstrikes and economic sanctions. While these measures have damaged Afghanistan’s economy, they have also hardened Afghan opinion against Pakistan. Any goodwill that the Taliban once held toward Islamabad because of years of covert Pakistani support has evaporated. The Pakistani military has also not been able to target the TTP directly, and its repeated strikes on the Taliban-led Afghan government have made diplomacy ineffective.

The TTP, by contrast, believes that its strategy of targeting Pakistani security targets and courting disaffected groups inside Pakistan is succeeding. It has gained support among Pashtuns, the ethnic group in northwest Pakistan that is related to the TTP’s tribal base, and is supporting Baloch insurgents in southwest Pakistan (see: Military will struggle to contain escalating insurgency in Balochistan, 6 February 2026). The group has also expanded its ability to carry out suicide attacks beyond the northwest border region to the rest of the country.

Explanation of Political Trajectories (ie, “Trajectories”)

Eurasia Group's (EG) Political Trajectories are indicators of the net impact of political factors on the macro business environment in a country over specific timeframes. For each country, two Trajectories are provided, a short-term (6 months) and a long term assessment (24 months). Trajectories are assigned by Eurasia Group’s country analysts through a structured qualitative process. Each Trajectory represents a net assessment, indicating how political changes in a country impact the macro business environment, defined as the economic and investment climate in the country. Once initiated, Trajectories are updated both periodically and as events warrant. Trajectories may be suspended from time to time if there is no active analyst opinion or analyst coverage, but the opinion or coverage is expected to resume. Trajectories can be:

Positive: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a positive impact on the macro business environment

Neutral: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a neutral or negligible impact on the macro business environment

Negative: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a negative impact on the macro business environment

Disclaimers: Trajectories are solely for informational purposes and should not be relied upon as investment advice. Trajectories do not indicate asset price movements, and have no value in forecasting market prices. Trajectories are based on data and analysis we deem to be reliable; they are not guaranteed as to accuracy or completeness. The Trajectories indicate the political outlook in a single country, over time. Trajectories do not address sector-specific or city/regional political risks that are often different than the country-level outlook. Trajectories represent an analyst’s net qualitative assessment of a country's political outlook. EG sales, analytic staff and products may provide analysis reflecting different opinions than those expressed by the Trajectories.
