DRIVING THE DAY

BRUSSELS TAKES ON “WEAPONIZED” MIGRATION: The European Commission is weighing legal measures to help EU countries respond faster to sudden arrivals of large numbers of migrants, including coordinated returns of groups of people, three EU officials and diplomats told Playbook.

The plans, which could be trailed in Ursula von der Leyen’s State of the Union speech Wednesday, are a response not just to the crisis in the Spanish exclave of Ceuta but also recent cases of deliberate or “weaponized” migration into Baltic states via Belarus, the officials said.

The push is part of a broader Commission effort to show that it’s tightening control over irregular migration as right-wing parties gain ground. The Berlaymont points to falling asylum numbers, a beefed-up EU border force and fast-tracked talks on boosting the EU’s partnership with Morocco.

Von der Leyen floated the “one grouped return” initiative at a lunch with EU ambassadors on Friday and received “generally positive vibes” from the room, per one diplomat, granted anonymity to speak frankly about the confidential meeting.

The Commission would provide legal guidance on when countries could temporarily suspend asylum applications in certain circumstances. “We can provide the legal texts and interpretations, but the member states decide what they want to do,” an EU official said.

But there’s a legal risk: The diplomat warned the plan could become a “legal nightmare” if it breached the principle of “non-refoulement” — the rule barring states from sending people to places where they could face serious harm.

The EU official said countries could invoke the Geneva Convention, which allows states to protect their borders from “invasion,” adding “there are court cases ongoing in Lithuania on this point.”

Von der Leyen is also expected to cite falling asylum applications, promote deals with non-EU countries and revive calls to triple the staff of the EU’s border agency, Frontex, the diplomats said.

Here again, there could be opposition: “Von der Leyen floated to basically expand the border force to 30,000,” added the diplomat cited above. “There’s quite a lot of skepticism about this … You’d have to pull people off national border forces.”

Amid the tub-thumping, the EU’s Dublin system risks breaking down, a second diplomat warned. The rules generally make the country of first entry responsible for an asylum claim — and require it to take back applicants who move elsewhere. The diplomat accused Italy of flouting them. “A lot of people are very concerned.”

The bottom line: Though asylum numbers are down, the political pressure on migration is still sky-high. “We have to be clear that migration is still a top issue for people,” European People’s Party chief Manfred Weber said in a statement ahead of the speech.

ARCTIC STRATEGY

EUROPE GOES NORTH: What are European Council President António Costa, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas, International Partnerships Commissioner Jozef Síkela and a bunch of European leaders doing in Rovaniemi — Finland’s official hometown of Santa Claus — in September?

Not getting a head start on Christmas … but rather putting the finishing touches to the EU’s Arctic strategy.

Who’s there: The European Arctic Summit gets underway today, following a working dinner last night hosted by Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo. Among those attending: Germany’s Friedrich Merz, Denmark’s Mette Frederiksen, Romania’s Nicușor Dan and leaders from the Baltics.

The High North has shot up Brussels’ agenda — something Ursula von der Leyen underscored during her visit to Greenland last week. And it’s not just geopolitics: Around half a million EU citizens live in the Arctic region, while security, critical raw materials, connectivity and climate are all increasingly part of the equation.

Expect a show of force: Today’s program includes a visit to the Lapland Air Wing and a military demonstration while leaders are expected to commit in a statement to keeping the Arctic a region of peace and stability — while acknowledging Russia as a long-term threat to Euro-Atlantic security.

“The Arctic is on the front line of global power competition,” the bloc’s top diplomat Kaja Kallas told Playbook, pointing to new shipping routes and an increasingly assertive Russia and China. “We must devote sufficient funds, forces and focus to the region.”

A hint is in the name: The unveiling of the Commission’s revamped Arctic strategy is penciled in for Oct. 20 — but the strategy is already getting a broader label. “Northern Neighborhood” will be added to the title, reflecting Brussels’ push for closer engagement with partners including Greenland, Iceland, Norway and Canada.

Speaking of partners … The EU’s goal in Rovaniemi is to get a final reality check from the countries most exposed to developments in the region before the strategy lands. Síkela told Playbook the summit is “a unique opportunity to gather input from top European leaders” and turn the EU’s offer into “a more strategic, more focused and more visible presence in the region.”

The Arctic is expected to feature prominently in von der Leyen’s State of the Union on Wednesday, potentially offering a preview of what’s coming in October.

That could make the speech worth watching for another reason. Kallas’ team has done much of the work on the Arctic file. If Ursula unveils a big Arctic initiative from the Strasbourg stage, watch who gets the credit.

LABOUR MOBILITY

ON THE MOVE: The Commission will tomorrow unveil the Fair Labour Mobility Package, designed to make it easier for people to work across borders while giving national authorities more tools to crack down on fraud, exploitation and bogus paperwork. The Commission will shoulder most of the up-front costs of implementing the package, with the aim of ultimately generating substantial savings for governments, workers and employers by cutting administrative burdens, an official told Zoya Sheftalovich.

Among the proposals is a skills portability initiative to make it easier to take qualifications with you across the EU. That would cut the time authorities have to assess qualifications for those in regulated professions — such as doctors, nurses, architects and civil engineers — from 120 days to 30, the Commission official said.

There’s also a new European Social Security Pass. Workers and employers could request documents such as the European Health Insurance Card and unemployment forms online, store them in the EU Digital Identity Wallet and have them verified instantly. The idea is to make life easier for the roughly 14 million EU citizens living in another member country and 2 million cross-border workers — and harder for fraudsters.

The European Labour Authority will get a stronger role coordinating cross-border inspections, helping crack down on businesses that are exploiting workers.

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Mostly overcast with some light rain. High of 23C.

TAPPED OUT: The EU is awash with environmental rules, and Belgium may finally be making some progress on the most basic green gesture of all — serving free tap water. In the interests of hydration and mild public nuisance, Zoya Sheftalovich visited 11 lunch and coffee spots in Brussels to check whether they’re getting on board.

Gold stars all around: At Exki, Le Pain Quotidien, Bouche, SUN, Mok, Cafe de la Presse, Wide Awake and LOFT, tap water was freely available — and in some cases, filtered! The canteen in the Berlaymont has filtered water, though on the day Zoya visited there were no glasses. Not to be defeated, Zoya braved the side eyes and sipped from a soup bowl.

Dry response: At Leopold, she was told it was illegal in Belgium to offer free tap water unless you need it for medical reasons. Spoiler alert: That’s not correct, but it makes for a convincing line. At ASAP, water was refused on her first visit, then begrudgingly proffered a second time after some pushing.

Bonus round for the jet-setters: Zaventem Airport has free filtered water (BYO bottle), but at Charleroi you’ll have to pay (plus it costs money to use the loo).

What the EU rules say: The EU’s Drinking Water Directive says countries should encourage restaurants and cafes to provide tap water either for free or for a low service fee. But there’s no explicit right to free water, and Belgium has not introduced rules mandating it either.
