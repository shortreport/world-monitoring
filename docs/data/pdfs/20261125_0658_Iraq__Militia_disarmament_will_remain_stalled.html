Iraq will avoid major clash with militias as Iran keeps disarmament in check
24 Nov 2026 16:56 EST
Discover more on the Portal
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:
  • An Iran-brokered compromise to the standoff between its proxy militia Kataib Hezbollah (KH) and the Iraqi government over control of a strategic area near Baghdad will avert short-term security shocks, including major attacks on energy infrastructure.
  • Further US sanctions on Iraq remain unlikely, even if Iraqi Prime Minister Ali Zaidi is likely to miss the June 2027 disarmament deadline, as he retains support in Washington and the region.
  • Iraq's rapprochement with Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries will remain limited as Iran-backed Iraqi Shia militias keep Gulf states wary and the dispute with Kuwait persists.

Iran has defused the Jurf Al-Sakhr standoff on favorable terms. After Zaidi mobilized security forces and gave KH, one of Tehran’s main Iraqi proxies, an ultimatum to surrender control of Jurf Al-Sakhr, Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) Quds Force commander Esmail Qaani rushed to Baghdad to defuse the standoff. He brokered a compromise that lets KH redeploy, most likely to the Syrian border, and hands control of the area southwest of Baghdad to the US-backed Counter Terrorism Service and the Iran-backed Popular Mobilization Forces.

Iran’s intervention shows that Tehran wants to balance keeping a favorable peace in Iraq and ensuring its Iraqi proxies protect its economic interests there. It also suggests a willingness to work with Zaidi if its red lines—no disarmament or aggressive efforts to shut down the cross-border flow of goods, money, or arms—are respected, which he is likely to do.

The Jurf Al-Sakhr compromise shows the limits of Zaidi's efforts to rein in the Shia militias, with the deadline for his disarmament and integration drive pushed back from September to 30 June 2027 (see: Increased oil exports will give Iraq short-term fiscal relief, 04 September 2026). It also highlights Iraq's importance to Iran following its loss of influence in Syria and Lebanon. Tehran has reaffirmed Iraq’s value by naming IRGC Quds Force field commander Abdolreza Masgarian as its next ambassador to the country.

The deal avoids short-term instability without resolving state-militia tensions. KH is an IRGC vanguard group that has led attacks on Gulf states during the war and has openly defied Zaidi on disarmament, citing solidarity with Iran in its conflict with Israel and the US. It has also hit back at his efforts to shut down its revenue schemes at Iraq's borders and airports. On 8 November, a KH drone attack on a liquefied petroleum gas storage facility at the Khor Mor gas field forced a complete production halt.

Zaidi wants to show Iraqis, Washington, and the Gulf states that he will not back down from confronting the Tehran-backed militias as predecessor Mustafa Al-Kadhimi did when he took on KH in 2020-2022. Unlike Kadhimi, Zaidi has the support of Faiq Zaidan, the head of the judiciary, who helped Zaidi secure power and backs the crackdown. Zaidi can arrest and imprison KH members, but he prefers a negotiated settlement. Armed confrontation could quickly turn into street battles in Baghdad and destabilize his government.

Provided the US does not seek to cut Iran-Iraq cross-border traffic, Zaidi can likely maintain the same degree of stability in Iraq that prevailed under his predecessor. That approach requires him to balance good ties with the Trump administration while cutting face-saving deals with Tehran that respect Iranian red lines. This will help contain short-term security shocks, including further attacks on energy infrastructure. Although Iraq has avoided confrontation for now, Baghdad would likely be drawn in if the US and Iran return to major escalation.

Washington will likely tolerate missed disarmament deadline without new sanctions. The Treasury Department is unlikely to impose major new sanctions on Iraq despite doubts over Zaidi's ability to deliver on disarmament commitments. Trump likes Zaidi personally, as does special envoy for Iraq and Syria Tom Barrack, who has praised the prime minister's efforts to raise oil exports via Turkey and strengthen ties with the Gulf states. Barrack played a central role in securing a contract to develop the Hamrin oil field in Salahaddin Province, north of Baghdad, for the Texas-based HKN Energy. In late October, he also brokered a deal between Iraq and Qatar's UCC Holding, owned by Syrian-born brothers Moutaz and Ramez Al-Khayyat, to transport up to 14 million barrels to the port of Baniyas over three months.

Iran-backed militias will cap Gulf-Iraq cooperation despite UAE enthusiasm for Zaidi. Abu Dhabi has been the most eager to embrace him. It has launched a logistics corridor between UAE hubs such as Khalifa Port and Jebel Ali and Iraq's Umm Qasr Port. The Abu Dhabi National Oil Company has become one of the largest buyers of discounted Iraqi crude, as Iraq has no tanker fleet of its own and the Strait of Hormuz crisis has hit it hard. Abu Dhabi is also engaging powerful groups such as Asaib Ahl Al-Haq, a militia that has moved away from Tehran's orbit and become a key component of the Iraqi government.

But major Gulf investments in pipeline, fiber-optic, and rail projects in Iraq will not advance without resolution of Baghdad's maritime dispute with Kuwait, which KH and other groups also threaten directly. During Zaidi's October visit to Saudi Arabia, he and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman focused on the threat from Iran's proxies and the growing Houthi presence in Iraq, which shares a long border with the kingdom. Riyadh largely kept Zaidi's delegation out of the room for fear that Iran loyalists had infiltrated it.
