DRIVING THE DAY

GERMANY AND SPAIN GO HEAD-TO-HEAD OVER IAA: Less than 24 hours after Germany’s pitch for the Industrial Accelerator Act (read about that here), Spain has laid out its vision for which non-EU countries and sectors should qualify under local-content rules. We scooped this position paper too.

Not a first: The national disagreement mirrors a rift in the European People’s Party where Spanish and German delegates clash over which countries should be considered a trusted partner under the industrial scheme.

What Spain wants: Madrid wants a three-tier Made in Europe framework, which it hopes will strike a balance between France’s position limiting third-country participation and Germany’s more open approach. Under Spain’s plan, the 27 EU countries form the core tier, with trusted partners like Canada, Japan and other countries getting varying levels of access. Spain put forward its three-tiered structure during Thursday’s meeting of national industry ministers.

What Germany wants: Germany, the bloc’s export powerhouse, is pushing for a broad “Made with Europe” group in line with the original Commission proposal. That foresees admitting partners that have free-trade or public procurement agreements with the EU, or are in a customs union with the bloc. The eligible pool could run to as many as 80 countries.

The French position: Paris wants to ensure that local content requirements bolster European production and industry, with third countries granted access on a reciprocal basis, according to a readout of Thursday’s Industry Council seen by Jordyn. “In the future, it will still be possible to have products with some of their added value coming from elsewhere, but production must take place in Europe in order to qualify for public support,” French Industry Minister Sébastien Martin told reporters ahead of the meeting.

A club forming? No capitals signed onto Berlin’s position paper, but the readout suggested that a loose coalition is forming. Germany, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Italy and Luxembourg emphasized including third countries based on existing free trade agreements.

Differing approaches: Poland raised concerns about ensuring the IAA doesn’t put EU countries that are further behind in the green transition at a disadvantage, according to the readout. Romania wants a sector-specific approach, allowing only the 27 member states to qualify in areas like steel and aluminum while third-country partners would be allowed in the automotive sector.

Not again, Rome! Italy used the meeting for yet another push to advocate for e-fuels, saying the IAA’s automotive index needed to be expanded to include vehicles powered by the fuel. Rome is the EU’s staunchest biofuels proponent, pushing for them to be a permanent solution for automakers in meeting broader emission targets.

Up next: The Irish presidency said the Council has made significant progress on the file and expects to put forward a new compromise proposal by mid-October. The goal is to reach a general approach by November, Industry Minister Peter Burke said during a doorstop.

RAIL

LYON-TURIN GETS A PARIS CHECK-IN: The French energy ministry will meet TELT President Daniel Bursaux and Director General Maurizio Bufalini in Paris on Monday, following initial contacts at the Franco-Italian summit in Antibes, according to one French official.

The bigger project: TELT is building the cross-border section of the Lyon-Turin rail link, centered on a 57.5-kilometer base tunnel through the Alps. The project has been in the works for roughly three decades and has repeatedly been delayed, largely over financing and political disputes. It is meant to shift freight from road to rail and dramatically increase rail capacity between France and Italy.

Why it matters: But Lyon-Turin is relevant well beyond passenger and freight rail. Brussels and national governments are increasingly looking at major cross-border rail infrastructure through a resilience and military mobility lens. Brussels and national governments are increasingly looking at major cross-border rail infrastructure through a resilience and military mobility lens, particularly where projects can add capacity for heavy freight and strategic cross-border movements. The link is among the projects eligible to receive support under the next Connecting Europe Facility, the EU budget’s main funding envelope for transport and energy infrastructure for 2028-2034.

Still controversial: The project remains politically contentious, especially over financing, environmental damage and whether the existing line could handle more traffic instead. Supporters argue the new tunnel is needed to unlock much greater freight capacity; opponents dispute the environmental and economic case.

TRUCKS

DIESEL THREAT HANGS OVER EUROPE’S TRUCKERS: A potential 90-day U.S. ban on diesel exports could quickly become a road-freight problem for Europe, which has grown increasingly reliant on American fuel supplies.

Why haulers care: Diesel still powers much of Europe’s heavy road transport, so tighter supply would feed directly into fuel bills for truck operators and could push up freight costs more broadly. The U.S. supplied more than half of EU and U.K. diesel imports last month, our colleague Ben Munster reports.

Bad timing: Europe’s room for maneuver is already limited, as supplies from Russia disappeared after sanctions and imports from Middle Eastern producers were disrupted after the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

And a ban could backfire: Donald Trump’s idea is to keep more diesel at home in an effort to push down U.S. prices, but refiners and analysts warn that restricting exports could instead force U.S. refineries to cut runs once storage fills up. That would mean less diesel, gasoline and jet fuel overall — potentially sending prices back up after any initial relief.

Europe splits on the risk: Some European officials have downplayed the likelihood or impact of a U.S. export ban, but the political tone is hardening. French right-wing leader Marine Le Pen warned at the POLITICO Playbook Paris Live event on Thursday that a ban would have “immeasurable” consequences. If the White House goes ahead, road freight would be among the first sectors to feel it through higher operating costs and pressure on diesel availability.

Macron says no: French President Emmanuel Macron said on Thursday he told Trump that a diesel export ban was a bad idea. “I think all the experts around him and all the American refiners can only tell him the same thing,” Macron said during a prime-time interview. “This decision would be a bad one — not just for the rest of the world, but for the U.S. economy as well.”

MARITIME

BLACK SEA SHIPPING AT AGRIFISH: Transport will creep into next week’s Agrifish Council through the fallout from the war in Ukraine, with ministers discussing market pressure as attacks on ships and port infrastructure have effectively shut down the main Black Sea grain export routes.

Ukraine joins: Agriculture Minister Taras Vysotskyi will take part in the discussion, while Kyiv has separately signaled it would be open to a truce on grain exports — a potentially significant development for shipping flows and security.

WASHINGTON DRAWS ITS SHIPPING LINES: The U.S. is easing pressure on Chinese shipping for now, while hardening its opposition to the International Maritime Organization’s climate plans — a split-screen approach that says plenty about where Washington’s maritime priorities lie.

China fees pushed back: The U.S. and China have agreed to extend their Busan trade truce by two months, from Nov. 10 to Jan. 10. The original deal had also suspended U.S. Section 301 measures targeting China’s maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors, including fees on Chinese-owned, -operated and -built vessels. That points to those measures remaining frozen during the extension, although USTR has not yet published the formal implementing notice.

A temporary reprieve: The vessel charges had been among Washington’s most aggressive tools against Chinese shipbuilding dominance. Before being suspended, the measures included fees based on ship tonnage or container capacity, potentially running into very large sums per U.S. voyage.

Meanwhile at the IMO: On shipping emissions, Washington is moving in the opposite direction. Trump used his speech to the U.N. General Assembly this week to attack the IMO’s Net-Zero Framework, casting it as a global carbon tax and saying: “There is no global government — and while I am president, there will be no global taxes.”
