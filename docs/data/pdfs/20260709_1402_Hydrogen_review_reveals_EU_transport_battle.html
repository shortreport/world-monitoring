DRIVING THE DAY

HYDROGEN REVIEW REVEALS TRANSPORT BATTLE: The Commission’s review of hydrogen rules hides a battle over the fuels meant to decarbonize aviation and shipping, with producers and transport groups pulling in opposite directions.

The fight: Eight major trade associations have written to the Commission on Wednesday urging it to fast-track a targeted revision of the rules that determine when hydrogen — and the e-methanol, renewable ammonia and e-kerosene made from it — can officially count as renewable.

Why now? The current rules will become significantly stricter over the next few years. From 2028, new hydrogen projects will no longer be allowed to rely on electricity from existing energy sources and will instead have to support new renewable generation. Then, from 2030, producers will have to prove they are making hydrogen only in the exact hours that renewable electricity is being generated, rather than balancing production over the course of a month.

Buy us time: The coalition is asking Brussels to push both deadlines back, arguing many flagship hydrogen projects are racing against regulatory deadlines because complementary legislation, permitting procedures and EU funding programs have all been delayed. Hydrogen Europe told Martina it expects the Commission’s review to deliver that targeted extension.

Why ships and planes care: The association also argues the extension would make the hydrogen-derived fuels airlines and shipowners will increasingly need under ReFuelEU Aviation and FuelEU Maritime cheaper and easier to produce. Without it, the association warns, many of the projects expected to supply those fuels may never be built — leaving transport with ambitious decarbonization targets but too little renewable fuel to meet them.

Not so fast: Environmental group Transport & Environment says producers are focusing on the wrong problem. “The lack of e-fuel volumes is not caused by [these] rules,” the NGO’s hydrogen policy officer, Marion Revest, told Martina. “The bigger issue is the lack of bankable demand for these fuels in aviation and shipping.” Rather than weakening production rules, the NGO argues Brussels should double down on FuelEU Maritime and ReFuelEU Aviation, giving investors the confidence to sign long-term fuel purchase agreements.

TRANSPORT BUDGET

TRANSPORT STAYS IN THE EU’S GREEN RULEBOOK: Transport will remain one of the sectors covered by the EU’s “do no significant harm” rules, or DNSH, under the next long-term budget, even as the Commission proposes reducing the number of activities subject to environmental screening, according to draft guidelines seen by our colleague Gregorio Sorgi.

Reminder: The activities not listed in the draft would automatically be considered compliant with the DNSH principle, while those that remain covered would have to meet specific environmental criteria — or qualify for one of the proposed exemptions.

Roads and vehicles: The draft includes the manufacturing, acquisition and leasing of fossil-fuel passenger cars and vans, as well as the construction and expansion of roads, among the activities subject to DNSH criteria, while proposing a series of carve-outs for emergency vehicles, public transport infrastructure, road safety projects and small-scale interventions.

Shipping, rail and aviation: The draft also covers the manufacturing, acquisition and leasing of conventional fossil-fuel vessels, non-zero-emission rail rolling stock and aircraft that do not meet the best-in-class ICAO CO2 standards. Exemptions are proposed for hybrid and dual-fuel ships, certain inland navigation vessels, workboats, and emergency and research aircraft, while new airports would remain subject to specific conditions.

AVIATION

AIRPORTS LEAVE EES TALKS DISAPPOINTED: Europe’s airports say they left a meeting with the Commission “disappointed and frustrated” after Brussels refused to grant broader flexibility for the rollout of the Entry/Exit System (EES), despite acknowledging long queues and operational disruption.

No breakthrough: Airport trade body ACI Europe told Martina it presented “overwhelming” evidence from airport CEOs showing that the new biometric border checks are already causing unacceptable waiting times, missed flights and wider disruption across the travel sector. While the Commission acknowledged the difficulties, ACI says it offered no new measures beyond continuing to work with member countries. The Commission did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The ask: Airports insist they are not seeking to scrap the Entry/Exit System. Instead, they want border authorities to be able to fully suspend passenger registration whenever congestion becomes unmanageable during the July-August peak season, rather than merely suspending the collection of biometric data. They also want the existing partial flexibility — which expires in September — to be extended, arguing that September remains part of the peak travel season.

The problem: According to ACI Europe, the biggest bottlenecks are not airports themselves but unstable national IT systems, the central EES platform, non-functioning self-service kiosks, chronic shortages of border guards and the slow rollout of the passenger pre-registration app. It also argues the Commission should accept that the system will need greater operational flexibility while those technical problems are fixed. Without it, the association warns, waiting times are likely to worsen as passenger volumes climb through the summer.

9 COUNTRIES SEEK LONGER EES FLEXIBILITY: Nine European countries are urging the Commission to extend emergency flexibilities in the rollout of the Entry/Exit System (EES), warning the bloc is not yet ready to phase out the current safeguards.

In a joint letter seen by POLITICO, Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal and Switzerland told Internal Affairs Commissioner Magnus Brunner on Tuesady that the EES’s first months of operation have exposed “significant difficulties” that cannot be underestimated, although they reaffirmed their support for the new border system.

RYANAIR LOSES ITALIAN COVID AID CHALLENGE: Ryanair has lost its latest attempt to overturn Italy’s COVID-era airline bailout after the EU’s General Court ruled that the compensation scheme complied with EU state aid rules.

The case: The Irish low-cost carrier challenged the European Commission’s approval of a €130 million Italian scheme that compensated airlines holding an Italian operating licence for losses suffered during the first wave of the pandemic. Ryanair argued the measure discriminated against airlines based elsewhere in the EU.

Court says no: The General Court found that limiting the scheme to airlines with an Italian licence was justified because those carriers were the ones most heavily affected by Italy’s COVID-19 travel restrictions. Judges also rejected Ryanair’s claims that the scheme violated the EU’s principles of non-discrimination, freedom of establishment and freedom to provide services.

MARITIME

EU OFFICIAL SIGNALS NO CHANGE TO SHIPPING ETS SCOPE: There are no plans to change the scope of the EU’s shipping emissions trading system, but the bloc is preparing new support for cleaner maritime fuels while hinting at an expansion of carbon pricing to international aviation, an EU official said on Wednesday.

Shipping stays the course: The official said Brussels intends to maintain the current ETS coverage for maritime transport — including 50 percent of emissions from international voyages — while exploring new support for sustainable maritime fuels. The review will also take into account any future global carbon pricing agreement at the International Maritime Organization and look at ways to prevent carbon leakage, where shipping companies reroute cargo through non-EU ports to reduce their exposure to the EU carbon market.

Aviation next: The official said Brussels is legally required to examine extending the ETS to all departing international flights after CORSIA, the global aviation carbon offsetting scheme run by the U.N.’s International Civil Aviation Organization, failed to meet the conditions set out in the last ETS reform. But rather than abandoning the global system, the official said CORSIA remains “the first-best solution,” with the EU instead looking at ways to address competitive distortions — particularly with neighboring countries.

MILITARY MOBILITY

MEPS AIM TO WRAP UP MILITARY MOBILITY BY YEAR-END: Parliament’s lead negotiators on the Military Mobility Regulation said they expect talks with member countries to conclude by the end of the year and the new framework to become fully operational in 2028.

Eastern focus: Co-rapporteur Michał Szczerba told journalists on Tuesday that he expects the biggest infrastructure investments under the new framework will be concentrated on the EU’s eastern flank, particularly in Poland and the Baltic states, where three of the bloc’s four military mobility corridors converge.

Business wants in: The other co-rapporteur, Roberts Zīle, said private transport and logistics companies have told MEPs they are willing to support military movements during crises, provided they are compensated when military traffic takes priority over commercial operations. Parliament has therefore included compensation for civilian operators in its negotiating mandate, although it remains unclear who would ultimately foot the bill.

Next steps: The first trilogue is scheduled for July 16. Zīle said he hopes negotiations can be wrapped up during Ireland’s Council presidency, but added that Lithuania, which takes over the presidency afterward, is equally keen to finalize the file if more time is needed.
