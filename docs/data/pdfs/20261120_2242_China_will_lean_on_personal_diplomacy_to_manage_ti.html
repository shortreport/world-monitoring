China will lean on personal diplomacy to manage ties with Trump
20 Nov 2026 08:34 EST
Discover more on the Portal
 

summary
  • Presidents Donald Trump and Xi Jinping emphasized leader-level engagement at APEC, but the summit produced no extension of the trade truce and Beijing will likely wait until early next year before retaliating against new US export controls.
  • New US chip rules will cloud Southeast Asia’s data center outlook, though they are unlikely to derail the wider buildout.
  • Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum will pursue a limited rapprochement with China after APEC, while her meeting with Trump did little to advance US-Mexico trade talks.
  • Economic strain alone won’t force Russian President Vladimir Putin to compromise on the war in Ukraine.
  • Countries and sectors in this edition of the Daily Brief include the US, Brazil, China, Colombia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, Russia, Senegal, Singapore, Thailand, Ukraine, Venezuela, defense, geoeconomics, and geo-technology.

the top line
China, US—China will lean on personal diplomacy to manage relations with Trump
  • On 19 November, Trump and Xi met on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Both sides emphasized the importance of leader-level engagement to maintain bilateral stability.
  • Xi stressed the importance of “constructively managing differences that could not be immediately resolved” to prevent escalation, which suggests that the talks addressed new US export controls imposed following the Nvidia scandal. Beijing will likely wait until early next year to see how the Trump administration intends to enforce export controls before issuing countermeasures.
  • APEC produced no announcement of an extension of the trade truce, which expires on 10 January. Both sides are expected to continue talks before the G20 in mid-December. Trump also used the bilateral meeting to push China to commit to agricultural purchases, likely as a precondition for extending the truce.

ASEAN, US, Geo-technology—US chip restrictions cloud ASEAN’s data center outlook
  • The US Commerce Department released an interim final rule on 16 November that imposes end-use controls on overseas data centers that provide “significant” computing power to China and other restricted countries. It also introduced new licensing requirements for advanced chip exports to data centers in countries including Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand.
  • The new limitations could moderately affect the region’s appeal for non-US data center investors, adding to existing headwinds from tightening regulations. But Washington’s new rule is unlikely to derail the wider data center buildout, especially given the strong role of US hyperscalers and growth of non-Chinese demand.
  • ASEAN countries will collaborate with US investigations and make policy changes to avoid more chip restrictions. There is a risk, though, that countries do not address US concerns sufficiently and further reports of chip diversion via the region result in even harsher measures by Washington.

Mexico, China, US—Sheinbaum will pursue a limited rapprochement with China after APEC
  • Sheinbaum used her 17 November meeting with Xi at APEC as a launching pad to rebuild ties with Beijing, with securing Chinese technology for domestic priorities as her primary goal and expanding exports to China as a secondary one.
  • Chinese suppliers of solar equipment and hospital technology will find opportunities in Mexico, though suppliers of manufacturing inputs will encounter constraints imposed by US-Mexico-Canada Agreement rules of origin. At the same time, Mexico will tighten enforcement of existing tariffs on Chinese products and screening of foreign acquisitions through the recently approved foreign direct investment bill.
  • The Sheinbaum-Trump meeting did little to advance US-Mexico trade talks as progress has stalled on Mexico’s push to secure Section 232 tariff exemptions. A limited side agreement that grants tariff relief is likely in the short term given Mexico’s concessions on more than 54 non-tariff trade barriers.

Russia, Ukraine, US—Economic strain alone won’t force Putin to compromise
  • Russia’s stagnating economy will keep deteriorating next year amid a widening fiscal gap and declining non-military output, but it will not collapse. The federal deficit is now expected at 3.2% of GDP this year, twice the 1.6% written into the 2026 budget law (see chart below), and defense accounts for more than a third of all spending.
  • The Kremlin judges that Ukraine and its European backers face more acute strain relative to their resources, and that Russia can finance the war for at least another year. It will raise taxes again if needed, tolerate higher inflation, and lean on state banks to absorb government debt.
  • Neither economic hardship nor the prospect of renewed business with the US and Europe will make President Vladimir Putin downgrade his war aims. The Kremlin is likely to respond by taking greater risks, making Putin an even more challenging counterpart for Washington.

Geoeconomics—Bond yields stay high as persistent inflation outweighs lower US-Iran escalation risks
  • Following the start of the Iran war, the correlation between Brent crude prices and US treasuries rose sharply amid expectations that higher oil prices would prompt tighter monetary policy.
  • Until October the correlation stayed high as shifts in oil prices amid Iran war disruptions remained the dominant driver of moves in US bond yields.
  • But since October that correlation has broken down. The fall in crude prices amid a recovery in oil flows through the Gulf and reduced expectations of US-Iran escalation following Republicans' poor performance at the midterms has provided little relief for US bond yields. Instead, signs of persistent price pressures and more hawkish comments from several Federal Reserve governors have kept bond yields high.
  • EG expects a continued standoff in the US-Iran conflict through the end of this year, keeping Brent in a $80-100 range.

US, Defense—Reconciliation 3.0 looks mostly dead, leaving defense spending to talks with Democrats
  • Senate Republicans are retreating from hopes to pass a party-line reconciliation bill to increase defense spending amid strong signals that they can’t reach the requisite 50 votes in the Senate.
  • Plan B for Republicans on defense is to seek Democratic cooperation. This would result in spending far lower than the White House’s $1.5 trillion request. Expect these negotiations not to begin until after the Senate leadership election on 1 December and to continue into the next Congress, with Democrats in the majority demanding increases to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and other welfare programs in return.
  • Most of the key programs that the White House has planned to fund with reconciliation spending—such as Golden Dome and munitions production—can still proceed without it, as the Senate’s defense appropriations package has moved funding for them into the appropriations budget.

Friday Insights—The AI boom is also a financial stability story

In a week with conflicting signals about the viability of the current AI investment boom, Friday Insights, our weekly digest of research insights on the intersection of politics, macro, and markets, looks at AI-related research that shows that the Fed is open-minded about the size and direction of the productivity impact of AI; that AI’s current impact on US growth is running at least as much through stock market wealth as through real investment; and that US hyperscalers are turning to euro bond markets to fund capex, creating new interdependencies.

  • The Fed is modeling both AI boom and bust (Opening remarks at the Federal Reserve's Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference): Fed Chair Kevin Warsh sets out three scenarios for AI, without committing to any specific path: a broad productivity boom, above-trend growth with unemployment above levels consistent with full employment , and a disappointment in which valuations fall and growth slows after heavy capex.
  • AI's boost to US growth runs through wealth as much as investment (The AI Boom's Two Engines): Jason Furman finds the data center buildout has added only about 0.3% to US GDP growth in Q3/2026, because roughly three-quarters of the equipment is imported; we note that a slowdown would hit GDP and exports of the tech producers abroad. The wealth effect from AI stocks is likely a bigger direct driver of US demand growth, and is susceptible to reversal.
  • US hyperscalers are exporting AI risk to European investors (Big tech's growing presence in the euro-denominated bond market): US hyperscalers, increasingly turning to credit markets to fund AI capex, already account for close to 8% of euro investment-grade bonds maturing after ten years. The ECB warns this limits diversification for European investors and leaves portfolios more exposed to an AI shock, with insurers and pension funds already overweight, and with long-dated bonds funding fast-depreciating assets.
  • Louder bubble warnings still carry a signal (Crying Wolf: Media Warnings and the Predictability of Financial Bubbles): Using LLMs to read 170 years of The Economist, the authors find the press routinely overstates crash risk, but spikes in its index flag bubble months 74% of the time. Applied to September 2026, the index puts the odds that AI is a bubble at up to 97%, higher than at the peak of the dot-com boom.

Brazil—Lula’s Finance and chief of staff picks will define whether the confidence crisis deepens: Lula’s picks for Finance and the chief of staff’s office will define whether the confidence crisis deepens. The US dollar has gained more than 6% against the real in November. Without credible signals on containing mandatory spending, stabilizing debt, and lowering the neutral interest rate, economic and political conditions may deteriorate more sharply early in Lula’s fourth term. Vice President Geraldo Alckmin is the most likely next finance minister, ahead of Planning Minister Bruno Moretti, Finance Minister Dario Durigan, and Piaui Governor Rafael Fonteles. Alckmin would deliver the strongest fiscal course correction. Former ministers Camilo Santana and Fernando Haddad are the two front-runners for chief of staff, a choice close to a toss-up. Lula is likely to balance profiles, making an Alckmin-Santana pairing more likely. That would signal an early fiscal course correction, but would carry more risk in fiscal management over time. Read more here and register here for our Brazil update conference call on 24 November at 2:00 PM ET. Reach out to discuss: brazilresearch@eurasiagroup.net.

Colombia—Retreat on unpopular measures likely to help secure broader tax package: In a presentation to the joint economic committees in Congress on 18 November, Finance Minister Miguel Gomez walked back proposals to raise VAT on some food, prepaid health plans, and agro-industrial output, as well as to reduce the corporate tax rate. The administration is trying to preserve the original revenue target (2.35% of GDP next year, rising to 4.09% in 2030) while diluting or removing the measures most likely to trigger political and social backlash. Despite demonstrations scheduled by opposition groups, the administration is likely to secure congressional approval for most of the package, as most lawmakers recognize the severity of the fiscal situation. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: latinamerica@eurasiagroup.net.

US, Venezuela—Hegseth’s visit deepens ties beyond the economy: During a visit to Caracas on 18 November, US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced and signed an agreement with Interim President Delcy Rodriguez’s administration that reestablishes military cooperation between the US and Venezuela. The announcement deepens the normalization of relations between the two sides and expands the cooperation beyond energy and debt restructuring. The US will remain supportive of Rodriguez, including in its forthcoming debt restructuring effort. However, pressure for new elections is substantial, with a new vote likely by the first half of 2028. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: latinamerica@eurasiagroup.net.

Senegal—Faye’s Kiiraay will rally voters around budget passage: On 19 November, President Bassirou Diomaye Faye passed the 2026 supplementary budget by decree, bypassing a PASTEF-dominated national assembly that had voted down the bill on 3 November. Kiiraay, the party Faye founded in July, has never contested an election and holds no seats in parliament, making the budget dispute a useful rallying point ahead of local and legislative elections expected in early 2027. By passing the revised budget by decree, Faye can take credit for any subsequent spending that supports the economy. The passing of the revised 2026 bill by decree now sets a precedent for the passage of the 2027 bill by the same procedure. Read more here. Reach out to discuss: africa@eurasiagroup.net.
