DRIVING THE DAY

EXCLUSIVE — COSTA’S DEALMAKER SETS HIS SIGHTS ON NEW YORK: The chief of staff to European Council President António Costa is eyeing a move to head the EU’s delegation at the United Nations next year, four EU officials and diplomats told Playbook and Jacopo Barigazzi.

Pedro Lourtie, a Portuguese diplomat who’s been at Costa’s side since 2024, has applied for the prestigious role, which will become vacant next year, the officials said. It’s one of a slew of high-profile jobs up for grabs in the EU’s diplomatic network, including head of delegation in Saudi Arabia and deputy head of the bloc’s office in China.

On the record: Lourtie himself and a spokesperson for Costa declined to comment.

Central figure: As chief of staff to the European Council president, Lourtie forms a duo with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s chief of staff, Bjoern Seibert, and is in charge of delicate preparatory talks with national capitals ahead of gatherings of EU leaders. He also carries out sensitive missions for his boss — such as when he opened a communication channel with counterparts in Moscow with an eye to ending the war in Ukraine, only to have EU leaders object to the idea.

Exit stage left? Lourtie’s potential departure isn’t linked to that controversial Russia outreach, two of the officials said. If awarded the U.N. job, Lourtie would be leaving before the end of Costa’s two-and-a-half-year term in office, which expires in May next year.

It could work out perfectly, because that coincides with Portugal becoming a non-permanent member of the U.N. Security Council for 2027-2028. Having a Portuguese diplomat representing the EU at the U.N. could help to advance the bloc’s interests.

Next cab off the rank: Lourtie’s deputy, David Oppenheimer, is well placed to succeed him, officials said.

Another important change is expected at the Council of the EU, the legislative body that represents member states: France’s Thérèse Blanchet, its secretary-general, is expected to retire. A decision on her replacement is also expected early next year.

Normal: Council presidents switching their chiefs of staff during their term isn’t unheard of, one diplomat said, pointing to the example of former Council President Herman Van Rompuy, who appointed Didier Seeuws for the final two years of his five years in office.

Smooth sailing: EU leaders must vote on the renewal of Council presidents, though traditionally they have decided to continue with the person already in place.

BIG AND BREAKING

SPANISH AFTERSHOCKS: Defeat for Pedro Sánchez in Spain’s snap election could destabilize the EU by putting the Socialists and Democrats on the back foot in Brussels and pushing them into a more confrontational stance, Max Griera reports. (More on the fallout from Spain’s snap election lower down.)

STRINGS ATTACHED: The Commission is expected to propose sector-specific “safeguard clauses” in future accession treaties — an addition pushed by cabinets earlier this week to the pre-enlargement review prepared by enlargement service DG ENEST and Commissioner Marta Kos, which is due for approval by the College of Commissioners today.

Translation: Joining the club could come with more strings attached, including trade restrictions more commonly associated with countries outside it, according to a draft seen by Playbook’s Gerardo Fortuna. The approach would be applied “as appropriate” first to Montenegro’s accession arrangements, but is mostly aimed at addressing Kyiv’s huge agricultural sector.

ALL EYES ON CHINA: Ditte Juul Jørgensen, director-general of the Commission’s trade department, will update EU ambassadors this morning on the state of EU-China talks following trips to Beijing and Washington. Her boss, Maroš Šefčovič, will debrief envoys on Sunday after his own two-day visit to China, Camille Gijs and Carlo Martuscelli report in Morning Trade (for subscribers).

That’s after France and Berlin jointly argued in a letter and position paper that the European Commission needs tools to be able to hit back harder and faster against Beijing in a trade dispute, Hans von der Burchard and Carlo report.

TRY AGAIN: Romania’s President Nicușor Dan has asked senior diplomat Luca Niculescu to form a government in Bucharest — after his three previous nominees for PM failed to break the deadlock.

SPAIN DENTS HOPES FOR A QUICK BUDGET DEAL

A NEW WRENCH IN THE BUDGET WORKS: Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s decision to call a snap election makes it less likely that the EU will strike a deal on its long-term budget by the end of the year, two EU diplomats and an EU official told Playbook.

Bad timing: Madrid is among the most prominent voices in the “friends of cohesion,” a group of 17 countries that wants to expand the bloc’s long-term budget, the Multiannual Financial Framework. That’s unlikely to change regardless of who wins, but the horse-trading required to form a government could make it harder for the winner to sign off on a tricky EU budget compromise, the officials said.

“It’s not going to do any good for the MFF,” said an official from the European Commission granted anonymity to speak frankly on a sensitive topic.

The Nov. 29 election will be “distracting” and won’t “incentivize finding compromise,” an EU diplomat said. Spain could still be trying to form a government when EU leaders are due to hold crunch talks on a final deal in December, making a big breakthrough even less likely than it already was, said a second diplomat.

António Costa had hoped to have an MFF deal in writing before a succession of major elections next year. France holds the first round of its presidential contest in April, followed by parliamentary elections in Greece and Poland. Spain’s vote brings forward that timeline of potential disruption, at a moment when leaders seem as far apart as ever.

Glass half full: That said, a victorious Spanish leader — if they’re able to form a government — could actually have a positive influence on MFF talks, the second diplomat said. They could have a “new and positive influence on the European stage. And sometimes it’s easier to play that role after an election.”

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

EU lawmakers have called for a €2 billion investment fund for the European Critical Raw Materials Centre, but what is that? The European Commission announced plans last year to open the center to help ensure the EU has reliable access to supplies of critical raw materials such as lithium and cobalt. It would be asked to monitor and assess the need for those materials, as well as to coordinate joint purchasing and stockpiling. The Commission has yet to publish a formal proposal setting out how that would work.

TESTING TIMES

PANIC AT THE BERLAYMONT: Spare a thought for the Commission’s temporary and contract agents. More than 3,000 of them sat an internal test to secure the ultimate Eurocrat prize: to become fully fledged EU officials. A whopping 46% failed, Gerardo Fortuna reports.

But the candidates don’t yet know if they’re among that group. They were told the overall failure rate but not whether they had passed or flunked the exam, three EU officials told Playbook. The result: what one official described as “pure anxiety.”

Canteen confidential: If you happened to eavesdrop over lunch in the Berlaymont last week (like we did), chances are you heard about little else. Confidence wasn’t exactly overflowing. “I flunked for sure,” one official told Playbook when asked about the test.

Blame the brain test: Making things particularly nerve-wracking was the first appearance in this test of the infamous cognitive assessment, which has been criticized by non-native English speakers for not being properly translated into French and German.

Last Chance Saloon: For those whose contracts are about to expire, this could have been their final shot at getting a full-time job. If their contract runs out before the next internal competition — and there’s no date for one yet — they’re back on the outside.
