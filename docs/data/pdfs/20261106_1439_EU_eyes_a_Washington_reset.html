START HERE: EU leaders will discuss who should speak for Europe in Ukraine peace talks at next week’s summit in Dublin, European Council President António Costa said in a letter to Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni last night.

Meloni had told Costa yesterday that the various formats for Ukraine talks are making Europe seem “all over the place” and insisted the issue could “no longer be postponed.” Costa responded that he would “make time for this discussion” at the upcoming leaders’ summit, adding that the EU’s interests “need to be represented by its institutions.”

Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski told Playbook’s Nick Vinocur yesterday that he agrees with Meloni’s assessment, adding: “We need to have a legitimate representation of Europe in the process because we pay for this war.”

DRIVING THE DAY

A TALE OF TWO HOUSES: EU lawmakers and officials in Brussels are hoping to turn the Democrats’ midterm wins into a diplomatic opening with Washington, laying the groundwork for cooperation on everything from Russian sanctions to artificial intelligence and a transatlantic forum.

A fresh start: The “blue tsunami” earlier this week gave Democrats control of Congress, bringing a wave of new faces to Washington. Due to retirements, deaths and campaigns for other offices, at least 99 incoming members of Congress are newcomers — a record, according to NPR.

Out of the freezer: The chair of the European Parliament’s U.S. delegation, Brando Benifei said there’s “a lot of space with this new Congress to upgrade our level of cooperation,” telling me EU lawmakers could “push through projects that were frozen before.”

Top of the wish list: The implementation of Russian sanctions, U.S. intel sharing and PURL weapons is how a European diplomat posted to Washington listed the priorities to my colleague Daniella Cheslow. The EU’s top diplomat Kaja Kallas urged the new Congress to put “more pressure” on Moscow. “Let’s see what happens now,” she mused.

Another big target is … the Transatlantic Legislators’ Dialogue, an inter-parliamentary exchange between MEPs and members of Congress. Benifei said “institutionalizing” the group is now back on the table, while a senior EU official said Brussels was planning to gauge interest on Capitol Hill for a beefed-up version of the forum. The official was granted anonymity, like others, to discuss the internal plans.

Planting seeds: “The fact that we have now such a new Congress provides some diplomatic opportunities for the EU delegation in Washington and the European embassies there,” a second EU diplomat said. Laying the diplomatic groundwork now will bear fruit in six months, a year — or longer, the diplomat argued.

There is cautious optimism in Brussels that a Democratic-controlled Congress could prove a more receptive partner. “The relationship with Congress under Speaker Mike Johnson was not bad, but there were tensions,” the official said, adding the EU would wait to see how much things change once Hakeem Jeffries is sworn in as speaker in January.

Other targets: There will be “new opportunities” to work together on AI legislation, Benifei predicted, singling out the House Committee on Ways and Means, which has trade jurisdiction, as an important partner to help blunt Trump’s tariff threats. (A Democrat will take the reins of the committee from a Republican in 2027.)

Quick reality check: European officials are under no illusion that a Democratic Congress will automatically mean a return to the Joe Biden-era transatlantic relationship. A second senior EU official said they expect the Democrats to have their hands full with domestic politics.

But the midterms could open channels for cooperation in specific areas that have been closed off for years. And for Brussels, even an incremental thaw in relations is welcome after two years in Trump’s freezer.

EPP’S BALKAN SCHISM

THE EPP’S TWO BLACK SHEEP: Since parting ways with Viktor Orbán, the European People’s Party has tolerated two of its more troublesome national figures. Now, the EPP is weighing up whether to kick out the party of Serbia’s Aleksandar Vučić, while appearing to turn a blind eye to the friendly fire of Slovenia’s Janez Janša.

Vučić in the balance: The EPP is set to decide at its Dec. 7–8 Political Assembly whether to take a tougher line on Vučić’s Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), after launching a scrutiny process in September 2025 into whether the party still respects the political family’s fundamental values, Max Griera and Nektaria Stamouli report.

“Things are not looking good for SNS,” said a senior EPP official. “They had promised to fix certain things and there was a timeline for actions and commitments, but they were not met.” The official was granted anonymity to discuss internal group deliberations.

The choice ahead: Some EPP members argue Serbia shouldn’t be isolated and SNS should remain in the fold, albeit under closer scrutiny. Another senior EPP insider said there were now two options: impose conditions under which the SNS can remain in the EPP, or kick the party out. An EPP spokesperson said the process is “ongoing.”

Looking the other way: Recent barbs from the Slovenian prime minister have been met with a more muted response. On Saturday, Janša took to social media to accuse fellow EPP national leaders Donald Tusk and Péter Magyar of rule-of-law abuses, drawing praise from senior figures in the far-right Patriots for Europe.

Little reaction: “No one has noticed much,” said one lawmaker. Polish EPP Vice-Chair Andrzej Halicki told POLITICO he found Janša’s comments “strange,” saying they showed a “lack of information.” EPP chief Manfred Weber declined to comment.

Words and deeds: The EPP has shown a high tolerance for outbursts, so long as members vote in line with the party and avoid causing problems in the European Council, with ongoing support of Ursula von der Leyen’s Commission part of the deal. It took the EPP years to part ways with Orbán.

A reliable ally: Janša “is tough in words, but in the end, he does cooperate,” said a senior EPP insider. A Slovenian EPP vice-chair, MEP Romana Tomc, is also widely liked in Brussels. “She’s fun,” said another EPP lawmaker.

THE COST OF (NOT) LEAVING SCHUMAN

COMMISSION TO PAY €300 MILLION TO STAY PUT: The Commission is set to pay over €300 million in fees for not moving out of seven buildings that it sold in 2024. Part of that money gets added to the bloc’s annual budget, worth almost €200 billion, which is expected to be signed off on Tuesday by the European Parliament and national governments.

The Commission sold 23 buildings in the affluent Schuman neighborhood for €900 million in 2024, to cut costs and reduce office space, as more staff worked from home. This coincided with a controversial plan to relocate hundreds of Eurocrats to the cheaper Northern Quarter.

But some of these savings have been offset by €255 million in fees the Commission paid to extend its stay in seven of the sold buildings, including the Madou Plaza Tower, which hosts DG COMP. The EU executive is also on the hook to pay the rest of the money — €24.6 million in 2027 and €25.6 million in 2028 — to prolong its stay, according to a Commission spokesperson.

On the attack: The head of the Renouveau & Démocratie trade union representing EU staffers, Cristiano Sebastiani, slammed the Commission’s strategy and asked for clarification as to whether “these payments would enable the purchaser to recover the purchase price within only a few years.”

But the EU executive pushed back. A Commission spokesperson told Gregorio Sorgi that prolonging its stay in some buildings allowed “margin to negotiate new rental contracts” and to “pursue its long-term building policy adequately and on cost-effective grounds.”

DASHBOARD

Germany is under pressure from the U.S. to pay more for new medicines, with talks expected in Berlin in coming days, trade colleagues Camille Gijs and Caroline Hug report. Berlin has been mulling proposals to avoid tariffs as it awaits the results of an investigation by the Trump administration, which argues that U.S. citizens are subsidizing medical research for countries in Europe through higher drug prices.

The results of a POLITICO Poll suggest respondents would be open to paying more, in return for faster access to new medicines. When asked to choose between price and access, a majority of respondents in Spain, Germany and France, as well as the U.K. and Canada, favored higher prices.

Timeleft: A dining app that matches you with five strangers based on a personality algorithm for a blind dinner at a mystery local restaurant. Not technically a dating app but certainly used as one, in our experience.

The Love Doctor: A new Instagram page that allows participants to fill out a questionnaire citing their green flags, red flags and guilty pleasures. The people behind the page then decide who might be a good fit for each other.
