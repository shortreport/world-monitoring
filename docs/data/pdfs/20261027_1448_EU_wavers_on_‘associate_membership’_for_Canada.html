DRIVING THE DAY

NO “ASSOCIATE MEMBERSHIP” DEAL FOR CANADA: EU countries are split over whether to offer Canada “associate member” status in the bloc, days before leaders fly to Montréal for a summit to mark a new era in EU-Canada relations, four EU officials and diplomats told me, Zoya Sheftalovich and Sebastian Starcevic.

Brussels vs. capitals: The disagreement — which persisted after talks among EU ambassadors on Monday — highlights tension between Brussels and EU capitals, which were caught off guard when European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen first floated the idea during her State of the European Union speech in September.

Berlin opposed: While several countries showed “skepticism” yesterday about using the associate membership phrasing in the joint EU-Canada statement, Germany is still leading the push to have the term fully removed from the text, said two EU diplomats, granted anonymity to discuss the sensitive talks. A spokesperson for Berlin in Brussels didn’t immediately reply to a request for comment.

What to watch: The European Council, in charge of drafting the joint declaration, will circulate a new draft text today and invite countries to propose last-minute tweaks.

The key question: Will Germany dig in and veto the term? That might please Canada, which had proposed “alliance for the future” as a preferred way of describing its relationship with the EU.

But dropping it would amount to a backdown for von der Leyen. A compromise proposed by Italy would define the arrangement in the final text as an associate membership “as proposed by the European Commission,” or similar, allowing capitals to distance themselves from the term without nixing it. Two officials said they expected this longer formulation to be included in the draft text circulated today.

That’s not all: Countries are still ironing out joint language on financial support for Ukraine — a major priority for the EU, which is seeking additional contributions for Kyiv from Canada and the United Kingdom.

Fine print: Regardless of what ends up in the joint statement, the EU isn’t offering Canada a new partnership status right away. This week’s summit is about agreeing on areas, such as critical raw materials and Arctic security, where cooperation can be deepened. The next EU-Canada summit, which will take place in Europe in about a year, will determine what’s possible — and indeed how to describe the new relationship.

The bottom line: With U.S. midterm elections just over a week away, Brussels and Ottawa will be wary of triggering the ire of President Donald Trump while he’s at a campaign podium. Any hint of a formal EU-Canada partnership in the offing — including an EU proposal of associate membership for Canada — could prove just the trigger.

QUEBEC WANTS IN: Meanwhile, the new separatist-led government of Quebec is treating this week’s summit as the start of a wider diplomatic push to get the Canadian province in the room whenever Ottawa and Brussels strike deals that concern its powers.

The defense partnership: The military and industrial relationship between Ottawa and Europe is accelerating, although European officials, diplomats and analysts describe the link as a welcome with conditions, report Laura Kayali, Mike Blanchfield, Jacopo Barigazzi and Mason Boycott-Owen.

A NEW-LOOK COMMISSION?

THE GREAT COMMISSION SHAKE-UP (KIND OF): Long-awaited recommendations for reforming the Commission are now in, drawn up by a group led by former Commission Secretary-General Catherine Day. Playbook has read her 35-page document and spoken to several EU officials … and here are four takeaways.

1. DGs (but not agencies) can breathe easy: There will be no merging of Commission departments — at least, not yet. The report warns that reorganizing DGs would cause “a lot of upheaval, which is not necessary, we think,” a senior official told Gerardo Fortuna. Cue a collective sigh of relief in the Berlaymont, where several DGs feared the chopping block. EU agencies aren’t so lucky, with the group suggesting a moratorium on creating new ones and a review of existing bodies, with mergers and transfers of responsibilities a possibility.

2. All roads lead to the SecGen: The Commission’s Secretariat-General would gain more power to coordinate departments and settle disputes. Given that it’s an administrative hub under the authority of the Commission president, its beefed-up role would amount to quite a power shift. Cristiano Sebastiani, of staff union Renouveau & Démocratie, warned that such centralization would need safeguards against abuses of power.

3. No cuts, but keep your bags packed: No staff reductions or reopening of the EU Staff Regulations are in the recommendations. Instead, the advisers want to redeploy officials as needed and enforce five-year rotations for senior managers. But Sebastiani warned that compulsory moves without guaranteed new posts could leave managers vulnerable.

4. AI, meet omnibus: The advisers want artificial intelligence to be used to spot overlapping rules, compare national legislation and slash paperwork. They also propose reviewing the EU’s entire rule book every five years — with a little help from AI to weed out obsolete legislation and unnecessary reporting requirements.

What now: These are proposals from an external advisory group, not binding decisions by von der Leyen or Budget Commissioner Piotr Serafin. But they are seen by officials as being influential on the final report, due by the end of the year.

ONE IN, ONE OUT

EXCLUSIVE — COMMISSION EYES TAX RESHUFFLE: The European Commission is exploring whether to use revenue from a global corporate tax arrangement targeting large multinationals to finance the EU’s next seven-year budget, four EU diplomats with knowledge of the proceedings told Gregorio Sorgi. They were granted anonymity to discuss the confidential plans.

Own resources, à la carte: The idea, which was backed by French President Emmanuel Macron in a leaders’ meeting in October, could facilitate an agreement on new EU-wide taxes, known as own resources. After more than a year of negotiations, EU governments are still arguing over five proposed new taxes that the Commission estimates would generate €65 billion per year.

European Council President António Costa’s advisers and the Commission are discussing whether to drop or tweak some of the proposed levies to make them more palatable to EU governments. A corporate tax, known as CORE, has emerged as the most unpopular option and is particularly toxic in Germany.

CORE victim: One of the solutions under consideration involves replacing CORE with a contribution to the EU budget based on the OECD’s global minimum tax deal.

The agreement, which was implemented across the EU, compels countries to charge a minimum corporate tax rate of 15 percent and to levy a “top up” tax on foreign companies that manage to sidestep the minimum rate. European capitals could funnel part of the revenue from the “top up” tax to the bloc’s budget, the diplomats said.

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Showers in the afternoon, top of 18C.

ZEN AND THE ART OF EU REGIONS: With DG REGIO removed from the chopping block (for now) and cohesion funding still facing an uncertain future, Europe’s regions could use a little peace of mind. And where better to find it than at the European Committee of the Regions?

Holy symphony! The Committee is hosting the WuLe Symphony Orchestra, billed as the world’s largest symphony orchestra made up entirely of monks, which is performing for free at 3 p.m. as part of a day of cultural events that kicks off at 10 a.m. Founded and conducted by Maestro Shi Wule, the orchestra blends Chinese musical traditions with Western classical music, spreading a message of peace and dialogue.

It’s a real thing: Brussels is a stop on the orchestra’s 2026 European tour, ahead of performances at the Berlin Philharmonie on Oct. 29 and Milan’s Teatro degli Arcimboldi on Nov. 2. No ticket prices here, no budget negotiations either … just (inner) harmony. For once.

GREAT PUMPKIN POLICY: Halloween is coming and the Commission’s DG SANTE has some advice for Brussels’ pumpkin carvers: Don’t waste your jack-o’-lantern. The health department suggests decorating pumpkins instead of carving them, so they stay fresh enough to cook. Turn the flesh into soup, risotto or cake, and roast the seeds for a snack. One warning: Make sure your pumpkin is still edible and hasn’t spoiled.
