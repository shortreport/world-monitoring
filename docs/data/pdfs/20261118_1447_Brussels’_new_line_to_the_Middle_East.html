DRIVING THE DAY

ŠUICA STEPS UP: The European Commission is taking a more prominent role in the EU’s Middle East diplomacy as Kaja Kallas’ direct channel to Israel remains frozen — illustrating a wider question over who gets to speak for the EU abroad, Zoya Sheftalovich and Nick Vinocur report.

This week, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Mediterranean Commissioner Dubravka Šuica are heading to Syria and Jordan, while Kallas hosts a Mediterranean security forum in Brussels.

Calendar scrape: Šuica has met Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar or spoken with him by phone at least four times this year, and in February she attended U.S. President Donald Trump’s Board of Peace for Gaza as an observer. Kallas has been iced out by Israel since June.

The rupture came after reports that Kallas compared Israel’s treatment of Palestinians to apartheid-era South Africa. Sa’ar said he would “sever all contact” with the EU’s top diplomat until she apologized. The decision to cut off contact “relates solely” to Kallas and Israel continues to engage with other EU officials, institutions and countries, a spokesperson for Sa’ar said.

Better days: Before that, the two were in frequent contact, sometimes exchanging WhatsApp messages multiple times a day, according to a person familiar with their communications who was granted anonymity to discuss the private exchanges. “It’s a problem if the Israelis don’t want to speak to her,” said one EU diplomat.

This tug-of-war over EU diplomacy helps to “undermine the EU as a credible global actor,” liberal EU lawmaker and chair of the European Parliament’s Development Committee Barry Andrews told Playbook.

Kallas is “open for dialogue and stands ready to engage with the next government of Israel,” a Commission spokesperson said. “She continues to be in touch with politicians from across the political spectrum.”

EU officials say Kallas and the European External Action Service are not being cut out of the region’s work, and that Kallas and Šuica work closely together. The Commission and the EEAS have overlapping roles across the region, with the Commission handling much of the EU’s aid and economic support and Kallas leading political and security work. The EU cannot dictate whom Israel chooses to speak with, one official said: “It’s a matter of personal preference.”

Zoom out: Yet Šuica taking the reins on communicating with Israel brings yet another arm of the EU’s foreign policy closer to von der Leyen. Leaders last week shut Kallas out of the negotiating team for future Ukraine peace talks, opting instead for national security advisers from five EU countries, plus the Commission and Council.

BIG AND BREAKING

GOING THROUGH THE MOTIONS: European diplomats and officials are skeptical that Vladimir Putin is serious about negotiating an end to the war in Ukraine, yet they’re sticking with the process being pushed by Washington — partly out of fear that Donald Trump could cut a deal with Putin and walk away if the talks collapse. Tim Ross and Nick Vinocur lay out the latest on the peace talks.

UKRAINE STANDOFF: Marine Le Pen and Volodymyr Zelenskyy say they want to meet, but can’t seem to agree on a venue. The French presidential front-runner has dismissed an invitation to visit Kyiv: “She doesn’t like the idea of going there for a photo op,” an adviser told my colleagues in Paris.

DEPORTATION DIVIDE: Commission Executive Vice President Teresa Ribera has criticized plans by five EU countries for a migration deportation hub in Rwanda, saying in a personal capacity that Europe shouldn’t emulate Trump or El Salvador’s Nayib Bukele. Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner defended the EU taking a “fair and firm” stance on unauthorized migration, telling Playbook: “Our policy is delivering results” and that “return hubs are part of this European approach.”

ETHICS PUSH: The Irish Council presidency wants the European Parliament to approve an expert panel to advise the EU ethics body before its first meeting, according to a letter seen by Max Griera. The move puts pressure on Parliament President Roberta Metsola and group leaders as the right-wing majority pushes to kill the body, with a decision expected in the coming weeks.

MONTENEGRO HITS A SPEED BUMP

FRONT-RUNNER, WITH AN ASTERISK: The Commission is on an enlargement high and Montenegro is the star pupil. But the Council offers a less rosy picture.

The Berlaymont’s happy: The Commission touts Montenegro as the country closest to EU membership, with a turbocharged timetable to match: accession talks wrapped up by December and a treaty potentially ready by June 2027. “Montenegro is approaching the finish line,” Ursula von der Leyen said this week.

Cross the Schuman roundabout, though … and the finish line looks further away. At a Council working party meeting yesterday evening, EU countries agreed to provisionally close just one of three accession chapters that were supposed to be ready before a meeting between the EU and Montenegro on Thursday. Regional policy got the nod; transport and energy didn’t.

That’s a bump in the Commission’s fast-track timetable. To keep things moving, the Irish presidency is considering an extra conference in November, rather than leaving eight chapters — plus two handled separately — to December.

The main issue? Croatia. Zagreb has a list of unresolved issues with Podgorica. PM Andrej Plenković made his displeasure known at the last EU summit, getting a commitment to close all chapters by year’s end scrubbed from the conclusions.

“We sorted out … two out of eight prerequisites we asked for,” a Croatian government official told Playbook, adding that Zagreb is taking a constructive approach.

The European Parliament is nervous: With the Council visibly less enthusiastic, MEP Thomas Waitz, chair of Parliament’s delegation for Montenegro, warned that “bilateral disputes must not block” the country’s EU path.

That’s just the warm-up: Even if Montenegro gets through the chapter-closing obstacle course, there’s a bigger headache waiting: ratification. An accession treaty needs approval from every member state. “You need just one country [against] and everything can blow up,” an EU official told Playbook.

“It’s a bumpy road ahead,” an EU diplomat added. In France, ratification would require either a referendum or a three-fifths majority in both houses of parliament. And that’s before factoring in a potential Marine Le Pen presidency. “It’s beyond Le Pen: There might be issues in many member states,” the diplomat warned.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen has said the EU should have a tech sovereignty fund to reduce the bloc’s reliance on U.S. and Chinese technology … but what would such a fund do? The idea is to invest — “several hundred million euros,” according to the Commission’s tech chief Henna Virkkunen — in companies active in AI, quantum technology and chips. A full proposal is expected to be unveiled at the European Council next week.

THE PLACE NO ONE WANTS TO WORK

IT’S GETTING LONELY IN LUXEMBOURG: Brussels often gets a bad rep from EU staffers, but it could be worse. That’s according to officials at the EU’s Luxembourg-based institutions who say they simply can’t get people to move there, Mari Eccles writes in.

It “remains a challenge,” the Court of Justice of the EU’s registrar (that’s the chief administrator) Vittorio Di Bucci told MEPs. “Recruitment is not all that popular for Luxembourg.” Chief among the reasons for that is the cost of living, he said. Figures from Eurostat last year showed it’s the priciest EU country for food and drink.

The European Court of Auditors echoed the complaint. “Attracting and retaining qualified staff in Luxembourg remains difficult,” said Zacharias Kolias, the ECA secretary-general.

Falling apart: It probably doesn’t help that the ECA’s “aging” offices aren’t in a great state. “We face unexpected problems like serious water infiltration,” Kolias said, adding that the court was deciding whether to bulldoze the buildings.

The carrot: Di Bucci said the Court of Justice of the EU provides allowances for staff on lower salaries and has made outreach efforts “to try and convince people to come to Luxembourg.” But so far, he said, that doesn’t seem to be paying off.

They’re missing out: “Luxembourg stands out in ways few places can,” spokesperson for the Grand Duchy Yves Hoffmann told Mari, citing “world-class infrastructure, free public transport, European public schools, and a vibrant multilingual, multicultural scene.” He added: “Add in a family-friendly quality of life, and it’s no surprise that expat families consistently rank Luxembourg as one of the most livable places in the world.”

8 MORE THINGS GETTING US TALKING

OPEN FOR BIDS: Commission Executive Vice President Roxana Mînzatu will today announce that applications are open for the EU’s €5.36 billion Erasmus+ fund for education, training, youth and sport projects in 2027. Brits can now apply as an associated country per the terms of the deal struck earlier this year, but sports bodies that allow Russian athletes to compete under national symbols are ineligible.

HANDS OFF OUR CHIPS: The Netherlands is pushing back against American efforts to tighten semiconductor export controls on China, warning that Washington’s bid to dictate rules for Dutch firms such as ASML is unacceptable.

NEW PATRIOT: Slovenian MEP Branko Grims, expelled from the EPP in July for breaching its far-right firewall, has joined the Patriots for Europe, bringing the group to 86 MEPs — ahead of the ECR’s 84.

BREXIT’S BAD BOYS ARE BACK: Andy Burnham’s attempts to reignite the debate about the U.K.’s relationship with the EU have given a new lease of life to veteran Euroskeptic campaigners.

TOXIC TRADE-OFF: Danish Environment Minister Maria Reumert Gjerding wrote to MEPs last night urging them to reject parts of the EU’s Omnibus simplification package that could allow the use of chemicals that pose a risk to public health, according to a letter seen by Playbook.

SUPERBUG SURGE: Drug-resistant bloodstream infections caused by “Klebsiella pneumoniae” jumped 72% between 2019 and 2025, the EU’s disease agency has warned.

WATCHING THE WATCHDOGS: Ireland has appointed former Twitter lobbyist Sinéad McSweeney to the role of Online Safety Commissioner — a move that could reignite criticism of the country’s approach to regulating Big Tech, my colleagues on Morning Tech report (for subscribers).

KILL SWITCH: The European Commission is weighing whether a WTO-era trade rule can become its fast-response weapon against China while the “trade bazooka” remains unused, Camille Gijs writes (for subscribers).

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Light showers. High of 11C.

A HANDS-ON TOUR: Back to the miniature building I mentioned at the top … A tactile model of the Europa building — where EU leaders meet and occasionally clash — will be unveiled today, allowing blind and partially sighted visitors to explore its architecture through touch.

The model will stay at the Council, although its exact spot hasn’t been decided yet, a Council official told Playbook. The plan is to put it somewhere accessible to all visitors, helping visually impaired people get their bearings.

Before you ask about the bill: It was donated by Spain’s ONCE Social Group, Europe’s largest organization for blind people, which already runs a museum of miniature world monuments. European Council President António Costa, Council Secretary-General Thérèse Blanchet and Spanish Secretary of State for European Affairs Fernando Mariano Sampedro Marcos will attend the unveiling.

A timely reminder: The European Disability Forum, whose representatives will attend, welcomed the initiative but had a message for leaders. “This is important as they discuss the next EU budget, where they risk cutting funding to programs that support persons with disabilities and their organizations,” its President Gunta Anca told Playbook.

LAST ORDERS: One of the EU quarter’s most beloved Irish pubs, Kitty O’Shea’s has lost its license after a visit from the Belgian food safety agency, AFSCA/FAVV. “Following several inspections, issues related to pest presence were identified at the establishment,” Hélène Bonte, a spokesperson for the authority, told Mari, adding that the pub currently “does not have an authorization” to operate. She didn’t confirm if, or when, the boozer — which has been closed for the past two weeks — might reopen, and repeated attempts to contact Kitty’s have gone unanswered.

Bitter end: It’s the second time this year the pub has closed its doors. Back in February, its management told Playbook that it was pre-emptively closing over a “minor” gas leak and to carry out repairs — and not because of the “shortcomings” noticed by AFSCA at the time. All in all, it’s been a bad year for the city’s Irish pubs, after the legendary karaoke spot De Valera’s in Flagey shut up shop.

THE HOLY GRAIL OF BELGIAN BEER HITS SUPERMARKETS: Belgium’s notoriously hard-to-get Westvleteren Trappist beer goes on sale at Colruyt today, for the first time since 2011. Just 10,000 six-bottle mixed Trappist packs are available. Last time, shoppers lined up from dawn and stocks vanished almost instantly. May the best beer hunter win.
