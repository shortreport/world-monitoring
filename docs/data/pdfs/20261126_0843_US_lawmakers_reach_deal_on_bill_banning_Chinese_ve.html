US lawmakers reach deal on bill banning Chinese vehicles, upping chances for passage this year
By Oliver Ward, Daniel Desrochers · Nov 26, 2026, 12:42 AM · View in your browser

Congress is looking increasingly likely to pass bipartisan legislation this year banning the import of Chinese autos, after the bill’s Senate backers won over key parts of the auto industry.

The Connected Vehicle Security Act, sponsored by Sens. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) and Elissa Slotkin (D-Mich.), would ban U.S. sales of cars that are made with components from adversaries like China and Russia. Moreno and Slotkin are aiming to pass the legislation by a voice vote in December, and are working with House colleagues to fast track the measure in that chamber, as well. Senate leadership from both parties have already signaled support for the bill, according to a person familiar with the conversations, and Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.) helped advance the measure out of the Senate Commerce Committee earlier this year.

Getting European and U.S. car companies, who had concerns about parts of the legislation, to line up behind it greatly improves the odds of that happening.

According to the same person and another familiar with the talks, the lawmakers agreed to tweak language in the bill to address concerns from several European automakers and their defenders, including GOP Sen. Rand Paul, whose state of Kentucky is an auto manufacturing hub and has courted more than $10 billion in investment from international automakers, according to Autos Drive America.

Discussions are at the “one yard line,” said one of the people, with the substance of the changes resolved and only wording and text left to iron out. Like others in this story, they were granted anonymity to share details of the closed-door discussions.

A spokesperson for the Alliance for Automotive Innovation, which advocates for many of the largest automakers, including Mercedes, Volvo, GM, Ford and Stellantis told POLITICO the organization is “very supportive” of the bill.

Winning over the Kentucky senator, as well as others like Alabama Sen. Katie Britt, whose state is home to a Mercedes plant, is crucial to getting the unanimous consent in the Senate to pass the legislation by voice vote — a necessity given the end-of-year floor time crunch, as lawmakers race to complete votes on must-pass bills before this Congress expires.

Passing the legislation would be another sign of Capitol Hill’s hardening position on China, in defiance of President Donald Trump, who has defended Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang amid a Chinese espionage scandal as he pushes to do more business with Beijing — and even mused about allowing Chinese car companies to build plants and sell their vehicles in the U.S. And it could further heighten U.S.-China tensions as the administration continues to negotiate another extension to the two country’s trade truce, which is due to expire on Jan. 10.

Chinese vehicles are already effectively blocked from the U.S. market by steep 100% tariffs, first imposed by] Trump and raised by former President Joe Biden, as well as Commerce Department restrictions on vehicle components. But some in the domestic auto industry have been pushing Congress to codify the blockade to provide greater certainty and help firms make long-term sourcing decisions.

Moreno, Slotkin and the bill’s other House and Senate backers have been negotiating with German manufacturer Mercedes, as well as advocacy groups that represent them, on provisions in the bill defining what qualifies as a Chinese vehicle, said the two people familiar and three others briefed on the talks. The original language banned sales of cars made by companies with more than 15% Chinese ownership. Chinese investors own nearly 80% of Volvo and 20% of Mercedes.

Paul told Reuters in September that he thought the bill unfairly singled out Mercedes, with its criteria for determining Chinese ownership.

Paul and Britt’s offices did not respond to a question on whether they would support the legislation. The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment on its position on the bill.

A GOP staff member who worked on the legislation and a second person familiar with the deal said that under the agreement reached with lawmakers, neither company would be blocked from selling cars in the U.S. The bill will now include more flexibility for companies with silent shareholders, like Mercedes, according to the people. Volvo has already been able to secure federal authorization to sell cars in the U.S. despite its China ties.

A Mercedes-Benz spokesperson declined to comment on whether a deal has been reached, but said the company “applauds the constructive discussions with key U.S. lawmakers including Senator Moreno, Senator Slotkin and [Senate Commerce Committee] Chairman [Ted] Cruz (R-Texas).”

A Volvo Cars spokesperson reiterated to POLITICO that the company has “specific authorization to continue to sell connected vehicles in the U.S.”

“We continue to monitor developments closely and we are actively engaged with policymakers on the potential business and consumer impacts of this U.S. legislation,” they added.

Lawmakers also kept batteries out of the list of components covered by the legislation and opted not to tighten restrictions on battery technologies, despite calls from parts of the domestic industry to do so. Ford Motor Co. resisted efforts for tighter curbs on batteries, which could have affected the U.S. automaker’s partnership with Chinese battery manufacturer CATL to license its technology.

Lawmakers will not include language on batteries in the final version of the bill, four of the people said. The language in the bill instead focuses only on battery management systems, which should leave Ford’s partnership unaffected.

Ford declined to comment.

Negotiators also agreed to include a sunset provision for the vehicle ban, after some environmentally-minded lawmakers expressed concern that indefinitely banning Chinese cars, including the country’s affordable electric vehicles, could slow decarbonization efforts, according to three of the people

This is “a freight train that’s moving,” said one of the people familiar with the discussions. “Slotkin and Moreno are putting people in rooms to make this stuff happen.”
