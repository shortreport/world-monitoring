DRIVING THE DAY

EXCLUSIVE — WESTERN BALKANS TELL VDL TO SORT IT OUT: Western Balkan countries hoping to join the EU will use a visit by Ursula von der Leyen to tell her they deserve to be considered much closer to joining the bloc.

“She can make that case,” Kosovo Prime Minister Albin Kurti told Playbook, calling on the European Commission president to publicly back candidate status — formal recognition of Kosovo’s path toward EU membership. “We trust that our performance has earned us a merited step forward, and that step is candidacy.”

No consolation prizes, please: While Brussels is working on ways to bring countries closer to the EU before they formally join, Kurti warned against this becoming a road to nowhere. “It must be a pathway to full EU membership, not a parallel process or a substitute.”

Here’s the problem: The Commission is readying more defined plans, including indicative timelines, for countries closest to joining. Albania, Montenegro, Ukraine and Moldova are to be given a clear route to the finish line — while others will be told to keep driving.

Welcome to the waiting room: That includes Kosovo and North Macedonia, both on von der Leyen’s itinerary alongside frontrunners Albania and Montenegro. Her challenge is to convince them Brussels isn’t creating a second tier.

Skopje wants more too: “We deserve to be in a much more advanced position than we are right now,” North Macedonia’s Foreign Minister Timčo Mucunski told POLITICO’s Sebastian Starcevic. His gripe: Skopje has heard promises before. “Within five years, you’ll become an EU member state,” he recalled being told.

Enter the sweetener: Von der Leyen is expected to point to a forthcoming review of benefits for those further back in the queue. “The president’s message will be — if you deliver on reforms, we will deliver on our side of the bargain,” a senior Commission official said, granted anonymity to speak frankly. “The more reforms they do, the more access to EU funds they get.”

Kosovo’s case: Kurti argues his country is doing its part, pointing to its alignment with EU foreign policy, including Russia sanctions. “We are not asking for shortcuts or a back door,” he said.

But there are two sides to the story: An internal Commission assessment seen by Playbook’s Gabriel Gavin estimates that Kosovo has completed only 24-26% of its Growth Plan commitments, which let aspiring members unlock EU cash and some economic benefits as they reform. Countries need to deliver before they can get the perks, another EU official said.

BUDGET BLOCKAGE

PARLIAMENT DRAGS ITS FEET: The Greens’ budget negotiator has warned that turf wars and resistance to change in the European Parliament are threatening to delay a deal on the next seven-year budget. “We’re so far behind that we won’t even have a Parliament position by the end of next year if we continue at the same pace,” Damian Boeselager told Gregorio Sorgi.

Party leaders will meet today to discuss ways to break the deadlock. Officials complain that the overlap between three different parliamentary committees — covering budget, agriculture and regional policy — has led to delays in negotiations over the National and Regional Partnership Plans (NRPPs), which include farmers’ subsidies and regional payouts and cover almost half of total spending.

Parliament risks missing its pledge to agree on a position on the budget by November and start negotiations with governments early next year.

Pointing the finger: “Some agricultural MEPs in [the] negotiation team on NRPP threaten the extreme to dictate their ideas into the law. I have one simple message to those destructive forces: it’s not going to work,” Boeselager said.

Negotiations aren’t any easier in the Council. In a fresh set of numbers expected next week, EU governments are bracing for a budget that’s more than €100 billion smaller than set out in the last negotiating document in June, four EU diplomats with knowledge of the discussions told Playbook.

Who stands to lose: Countries including Italy, Spain and Poland are lobbying Ireland, which holds the six-month rotating presidency of the Council of the EU, to refrain from such cuts. “I expect there will be cuts that will make Friends of Cohesion unhappy,” said one of the diplomats, referring to a group of countries pushing for a bigger budget.

Another thorny issue in Council involves funding the outermost regions including French Guiana, the Azores and the Canary Islands. France’s Emmanuel Macron, Spain’s Pedro Sánchez and Portugal’s Luis Montenegro asked for “adequate EU funding” for those regions in a letter seen by Playbook.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The European Commission is considering big changes to its civil service exam board, the European Personnel Selection Office … but what is EPSO? It’s an EU body responsible for selecting talented candidates for jobs in the institutions and agencies. It does this by conducting tests (known as competitions) for both generalists and specialists. Some 170,000 people applied to take a generalist test earlier this year, for which fewer than 1,500 roles could be available.

PARTY WATCH

THE LITTLE-KNOWN BODY HELPING DECIDE ESN’S FUTURE: The Committee of Independent Eminent Persons meets Thursday to start discussions on whether to deregister and defund the far-right Europe of Sovereign Nations party, the European home of Alternative for Germany.

What? The obscure committee advises the equally obscure Authority for European Political Parties, which scrutinizes financing and compliance with EU values. In July, the European Parliament approved a probe into the ESN over allegations that it breached the bloc’s values, including with anti-immigration and anti-LGBT rhetoric. The committee has two months to issue an opinion.

Each EU institution has two people on the committee: The Council has German law professor Pia Lange and Lithuania’s Linas Kontrimas, who ran to be an MEP in 2019. The Commission put forward ex-Commissioner Martine Reicherts and Alexander Italianer, former Commission sec-gen and deputy head of cabinet to José Manuel Barroso. The Parliament has Luca Vanoni (who has been a visiting professor at think tank MCC) and Anna Pacześniak, who sits on the scientific council of the Foundation for European Progressive Studies.

But ESN is worried about neutrality, saying “people with clear political affiliations … have been appointed to the committee,” according to Alexander Sell, a vice president. “In a true democracy, party representatives should not be involved in decisions regarding the funding of their political rivals.”

Nothing to see: Spokespeople and officials from the institutions insisted all appointments are in line with the law, which forbids sitting EU officials, commissioners and MEPs from being members, as well as employees of political parties and foundations. There’s no cooling-off period before joining the committee, the Parliament press service said.

Greens next: Sell said he hoped the authority would use the same process on the European Greens following allegations of antisemitism brought by a citizens’ group. Antisemitism accusations are dealt with “swiftly and seriously,” the European Greens said.

NEW KID ON THE BLOCK: Something about the Brussels bubble and its highly paid, highly caffeinated officials has Belgian entrepreneurs seeing dollar signs.

This week, Mondday became the latest in a string of new specialty coffee places to open just a stone’s throw from the Schuman roundabout, on Rue Froissart, branching out from its first store on the Chaussée d’Ixelles. A hyper-cool industrial space sparsely furnished with stainless steel tables and chairs, it’s unapologetically aimed at the same international market that works out at ANIMO and gets their flat whites at Bouche — right across the road.

Costly coffee: An Americano comes in at €3.50, an iced blueberry matcha at €6.50, fluffy crema di pistacchio pancakes at €14.50 and an English breakfast at €19.50. But, ironically for a spot where many of its clients won’t be paying on their own dime, Mondday hasn’t quite cracked printing receipts for expenses — but it will email them to you if you scribble down your address.

OFF THE RAILS: Railway enthusiasts have a rare chance to own part of Brussels’ historic infrastructure, with national operator SNCB putting the former station house at Uccle-Calevoet up for sale online. The building, which the agent boasts offers great rail access to the city, could be yours for just €400,000.
