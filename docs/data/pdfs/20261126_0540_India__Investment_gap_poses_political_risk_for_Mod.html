India

Modi’s speech to business leaders reflects frustration over weak private investment. At a meeting of the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FICCI) on 19 November, Prime Minister Narendra Modi criticized business leaders for failing to raise wages, expand hiring, and invest in new capacity, even after his government cut corporate tax rates and improved the business environment.

  • Despite strong profits and factory utilization rates of about 75%, Indian companies have hesitated to invest until they see a meaningful recovery in consumption. Global uncertainty and unpredictable policy have also weighed on private investment. Private capital expenditure is showing signs of recovery after nearly two decades, but it remains concentrated in power and renewables, AI infrastructure, and manufacturing sectors such as metals, chemicals, electronics, and defense.
  • Data from the Center for Monitoring Indian Economy indicate that since the corporate tax rate fell from 30% to 22% in 2019, profits of non-financial companies in the Nifty 500 index have risen by an average of 18% per year. Over the same period, wages increased only 10% annually, while job growth lagged at 6.5%. With unemployment a key electoral issue, Modi appears to believe he is bearing the political cost of companies’ failure to expand.
  • In his speech to FICCI, Modi also urged Indian companies to produce goods of higher quality and at greater scale to seize opportunities created by the free trade agreements (FTAs) India is negotiating. FTAs with the EU, Canada, and the US, which are due to be announced in December, will give Indian companies broader access to export markets (see: Canada, EU trade deals on track for December, 29 October 2026).
 

Revived Quad will have a limited agenda. On 17 November, the leaders of Australia, India, Japan, and the US met on the sidelines of the ASEAN summit for the first Quad leaders’ summit since September 2024. The meeting suggests renewed US interest in the grouping, but President Donald Trump is not personally invested in it. As a result, most progress will occur at the foreign ministers’ level.

  • The Quad has narrowed its agenda since 2024. At the latest summit, the grouping continued to deepen cooperation on maritime surveillance. The joint statement also pointed to continued focus on AI, cybersecurity, critical minerals, and undersea cables. The only new initiative was a Quad business leaders’ forum, which India will host in early 2027.
  • Another Quad priority will be coordination across a growing number of bilateral supply chain initiatives, rather than direct four-way coordination (see: Foreign ministers meeting points to Quad revival with more modest agenda, 26 May 2026). India and Japan are deepening ties faster than the other members. On 19 November, they signed an agreement allowing Japan to test its missiles on Indian ranges starting in 2027.
 

Caste tensions pose a challenge for BJP ahead of state polls. With youth protests led by the Cockroach Janata Party movement fading, Modi’s main concern ahead of state elections in February and March next year is growing discontent among Hindu upper castes with the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). Upper-caste voters have already started to turn against the BJP in by-elections, although opposition parties have not yet benefitted from that shift.

  • Modi built his electoral success by combining the BJP’s traditional upper-caste base with emerging lower-middle castes, known as Other Backward Classes (OBCs). However, that coalition is now fraying because many upper-caste voters believe the government favors the more numerous OBCs. A census due next year will probably intensify that tension. It is likely to show that OBCs account for about half the population, which would increase pressure on the government to raise the current 27% OBC quota for government jobs and public college admissions, largely at the expense of upper castes.
  • Of the five state elections early next year, Uttar Pradesh, India’s most populous state, will draw the closest attention. The state is central to the BJP’s dominance in northern India, but the vote will be close. Private polls indicate that, for now, the BJP would narrowly retain Uttar Pradesh, but it remains vulnerable (see: Upper-caste defections over job quotas threaten Modi’s electoral coalition, 25 November 2026).
Pakistan

Long-term trajectory:

PPP will forestall creation of new provinces. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s government is considering a referendum to create 12 to 15 new provinces from Pakistan’s existing four. The government has not presented a formal proposal, but ministers have indicated that discussions are underway. Defence Minister Khawaja Asif expects the process to advance in early 2027.

  • The Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP), Sharif’s junior coalition partner, poses the main obstacle. The party opposes any move to divide Sindh, its power base. Sharif may not need PPP support to bring the proposal to Parliament. However, any change to provincial boundaries requires a two-thirds majority in both the National Assembly and the Senate, as well as in each affected provincial assembly. Because the PPP controls the Sindh provincial assembly, it can block any effort to divide the province, even if voters approve a referendum. The party would probably also leave the governing coalition if Sindh were divided.
  • The push to create new provinces is not new. Proponents argue that greater decentralization could improve governance. Sharif has long supported the idea and backed a 2012 proposal to divide Punjab, his political base, into three provinces. The latest push comes from Army Chief Asim Munir, who sees new provinces as a way to weaken political parties and increase the army’s leverage over them.
Sri Lanka

Long-term trajectory:

Budget maintains fiscal discipline despite expansionary measures. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, who also serves as finance minister, unveiled Sri Lanka’s 2027 budget on 12 November. The budget offers limited tax relief to salaried workers through revised tax brackets. Dissanayake also said the government will spend 2 trillion rupees ($6 billion) on capital expenditure next year. However, the government’s ability to execute infrastructure projects remains limited. It had spent less than 30% of its 2026 capital expenditure allocation by September.

  • Dissanayake continues to maintain fiscal discipline. Although the overall budget deficit is projected to widen to 4.5% of GDP in 2027, up from 3.7% this year, the budget preserves a primary surplus of 2.3% of GDP.
  • Sri Lanka remains on track to complete its four-year $2.9 billion Extended Fund Facility in March. Dissanayake has appointed a high-level committee to assess whether Colombo needs another IMF program, possibly a shorter Stand-By Arrangement. The country is seeking $2.1 billion in foreign loans next year to repay debt and fund the budget.
  • The government is officially targeting 7% growth in 2027, although it would likely welcome 5%. The IMF projects much lower growth of 3.7%. Higher energy prices and the effects of El Niño will continue to weigh on Sri Lanka’s economy. High interest rates will also constrain growth. The central bank raised rates by 25 basis points to 9% on 20 November to curb inflation, which rose to 8.5% in October.
Bangladesh

Rahman’s government remains secure amid unrest. Prime Minister Tarique Rahman’s government faces unrest from both garment workers and supporters of ousted Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina’s banned Awami League party. Since 14 November, Awami League-backed violence across Bangladesh has killed four people, injured dozens, and torched several buildings. Violence continues even as the government deployed additional paramilitary forces to contain tensions.

  • New Delhi appears to have restrained the Awami League during the February general elections, but deteriorating India-Bangladesh ties now mean Indian authorities are no longer keeping the party’s activities in check. With Hasina planning to return to Bangladesh on 16 December after more than two years in exile in India, sporadic violence will likely persist in December.
  • The government has largely defused the garment workers’ strike by raising the minimum monthly wage by 36% and introducing food subsidies for workers. Dhaka also suspects Awami League involvement in the workers' protests.
