DRIVING THE DAY

KALLAS STANDS HER GROUND: Kaja Kallas is fighting for a role in upcoming Ukraine peace talks after she and the bloc’s foreign ministers were sidelined from the negotiating team selected by EU leaders.

The decision to exclude the bloc’s chief diplomat from the negotiating team is raising new questions about her role and the relevance of the EU’s foreign policy corps more broadly, at a time when leaders and their advisers are increasingly taking control of major international matters.

It’s in the job description: “Take out the EU treaties and read the treaties. Who is dealing with security and foreign policy issues?” Kallas said yesterday, defending her role as the bloc’s top foreign affairs official.

Hey, what about us? Foreign ministers gathering in Brussels for a monthly meeting argued they should have a more active role in the Ukraine talks. “What many member states’ ministers said is that they don’t want to be debriefed about these things. They want to be … involved in these talks,” said Kallas.

Ministers will work in the coming weeks to establish a mandate for the negotiators, according to a senior EU official. “The ministers want to be involved in the substance,” the official said, adding that expertise about Russia and prior security deals resides with the European External Action Service and national foreign ministries.

But other officials said the job of drafting the mandate will fall to EU ambassadors, citing the need to move quickly to set out principles before talks resume in Abu Dhabi in the weeks ahead.

The tug-of-war feeds into reports of a power struggle between EU institutions over who controls the bloc’s foreign policy. In addition to leading on one of the most important foreign and security policy debates of recent decades, President Ursula von der Leyen’s Commission has now also largely taken over the management of the EU’s relationship with Israel.

Some EU diplomats point to Kallas’s criticism of U.S. President Donald Trump’s administration as part of the reason why she isn’t in the Ukraine negotiating team. Others say the team is deliberately comprised of national security officials answering to leaders, pointing out that U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has not been involved in recent rounds of talks. The diplomats were granted anonymity to speak frankly.

Stand back: “This is part of a mega-trend that’s hard to fight,” said one of the diplomats. “More and more international questions that were once handled by foreign ministers are now being handled by prime ministers or presidents.” (A case in point: Finnish President Alexander Stubb telling the FT this morning that European leaders should talk to Vladimir Putin directly.)

The key for Kallas and EU foreign ministers is to seek relevance by providing “geopolitical strategic perspective” on issues, even if they aren’t directly involved, the diplomat added.

EU TARGETS RUSSIAN BANKS AND CRYPTO

UPHILL BATTLE: The EU is gearing up to hit Russia with fresh sanctions against banks, crypto assets and shadow fleet vessels before the end of this year, three diplomats said. The push comes despite some countries saying they’ve reached the limit of what they can do and as Kyiv voices disappointment over missed opportunities.

Working through the pain: The 22nd package of sanctions, to be discussed in consultations starting today, is intended to show that Brussels is determined to keep pressuring Moscow amid ongoing Russia-Ukraine peace talks brokered by the U.S. The effort coincides with plans to give Kyiv a €45 billion loan to cover its needs for the coming year, with a Commission proposal to be shared with capitals on Wednesday.

Zoom in: The upcoming package will crack down on Russia’s use of cryptocurrencies to circumvent constraints on its traditional financial system. It will also seek to ban exports of raw materials that Russia needs to build drones and ballistic missiles, according to Ukraine’s sanctions envoy Vladyslav Vlasiuk.

Expected to be finalized in December, the 22nd package will follow fresh sanctions targeting Russian individuals including Tatyana Kim, the CEO of Russian e-commerce platform Wildberries and Russia’s richest woman. Those individual listings, which target more than 100 “entities,” were signed off by foreign ministers on Monday, in addition to new measures targeting Hamas.

But the new package also shows the limits of how far Europe is willing to go. A ban on offering maritime services to Russian ships, though ready, will not be approved due to persistent opposition from Greece, Malta and Cyprus. Ukraine is seeking a ban on alumina exported by Ireland’s Aughinish refinery, but Dublin — which holds the EU’s rotating presidency — won’t agree to sanctions. A ban on former Russian combatants entering the EU is also still stuck in process.

“A bit disappointing,” was how Vlasiuk described the stalled sanctions measures, adding he would like to see a ban on alumina exports and sanctions against the captains of shadow fleet vessels, as well as on businesspeople who Ukraine says support Russia’s war effort.

“Enforcement is not something that is done on paper,” he said, referring to a recent incident in which a Russian shadow fleet with a false flag was boarded by EU forces and then let go. “Sanctioning the ship captains would make a difference.”

MOVING ON: David O’Sullivan, the European Commission’s sanctions envoy, is retiring in the coming months, according to three EU officials. His replacement has yet to be named and it’s unclear whether Brussels will again nominate a senior official for the job. But the Commission is setting up a new Sanctions Office to coordinate policy on sanctions.

The European Central Bank’s Financial Stability Review comes out on Wednesday … but what is that? It’s a publication released twice a year that assesses the eurozone financial system’s potential risks and vulnerabilities. This time it will urge banks, tech companies and regulators to coordinate to strengthen the financial industry’s online defenses, as AI makes cyberattacks faster and more sophisticated.

COMMISSION CALLS FOR SPORTSMANSHIP

LET’S TALK ABOUT IT: Football bodies should settle their disputes “through dialogue rather than through lawyers,” Sport Commissioner Glenn Micallef told Zoya Sheftalovich after an organization representing non-elite teams asked Brussels to investigate whether European football’s governing body is abusing its dominance.

The Union of European Clubs has challenged the exclusivity of UEFA’s pact with another football body, European Football Clubs, arguing it allows leading clubs to entrench their advantage at smaller clubs’ expense. It wants the European Commission to investigate whether the deal breaks EU antitrust rules.

That might be a tough sell. The Commission’s competition department has historically been reluctant to open investigations into football cases, viewing them as politically toxic, according to current and former senior officials in the department. In sport, disputes are better resolved “by players in the sporting community” rather than their legal teams, Micallef said.

Time for new FIFA blood: Asked about Gianni Infantino seeking another term as FIFA president, Micallef said “it would be healthy for football and for FIFA” to have a conversation “about how football should be governed.” French football chief Philippe Diallo announced his candidacy for the top job yesterday.

Coming today: Micallef will unveil the Commission’s European Sport Model communication, setting out its vision for governance and money in sport, with initiatives covering ticket pricing, illegal streaming and volunteers.

THE VIEW FROM MADRID: Spain’s looming election is one of several pivotal votes across the EU in the next 12 months that could weaken the bloc’s ambition on climate issues. Center-right voters there want Spain to take action on climate change, but not to go further than other countries, according to a POLITICO Poll. BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Rain this morning, then sunny spells in the afternoon before showers return in the evening. High of 14C.

CLIMATE CHANGE, LIVE ON STAGE: Want a visual metaphor for global warming? Head to Etterbeek Town Hall, where an actual block of glacial ice will melt before your eyes during the opening of “Archive of Possible Loss” exhibition, at 6:30 p.m. While it happens, climate journalist Tine Hens will tell stories of disappearing species, accompanied by live music. STUDENTS TAKE TO THE STREETS: Up to 10,000 students are expected at Brussels Central Station at 10:30 a.m. to protest tuition hikes and education cuts. ULB and VUB students, trade unions and hundreds of Walloon metalworkers are joining the march. Expect a busy morning, particularly around the city center.

BRIDGERS DITCHES BRUSSELS: American singer Phoebe Bridgers has moved her concert in Brussels on Dec. 5 to Antwerp on Dec. 6 — and fans who were flying in for the show aren’t happy. Live Nation Belgium, the tour promoter, called it a “venue upgrade” in a statement, which will allow “Phoebe to bring the full production of the show to life.” (Antwerp’s AFAS Dome has a capacity of about 23,000 compared with Brussels’ Forest National’s 8,400.)
