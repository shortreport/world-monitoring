Iran’s deal proceeds would fall well short of its maximalist demands
10 Aug 2026 05:21 EDT
Discover more on the Portal
  • Iran's best-case first-year payout from any US deal tops out at $30 billion-$40 billion and would require a revival of the June memorandum of understanding (MOU) and monetary concessions that remain difficult to secure.
  • Tehran could secure up to $20 billion of frozen assets, $8 billion a year from a US oil-sales license, and $5-10 billion a year from Strait of Hormuz fees, but Washington could revoke the license quickly, and the 60-day congressional review window could end up stopping the frozen funds if fighting resumes.
  • Even in Tehran's best case, the proceeds from a deal would ease fiscal pressure but would not fully address the public’s deep economic grievances, so political control will still rest on force.

Iran's best-case first-year proceeds from any deal top out at $30 billion-$40 billion, far below headline claims. Tehran would need to revive the June MOU and secure the variety of financial incentives that it contained—including temporary sanctions relief for oil exports, the release of frozen overseas assets, and a (largely theoretical) $300 billion reconstruction fund. Both steps face challenges. At the moment, de-escalation between the US and Iran is likely to include an end to the US blockade in exchange for acknowledging Iran’s position in the Strait of Hormuz. Tehran will press to restore the MOU’s other incentives, as well as the right to levy fees and tolls on passing ships (see: De-escalation likely as US leans toward temporary fix to open strait, 4 August 2026).

Every revenue source carries a serious risk that Washington could shut it off, as it has done before. The US Treasury revoked June's oil-sales permission within 15 days. The 2023 frozen-asset release was reversed without any specific legislative act. Iran is likely to earn some revenue from its new position in the strait, but that amount will fall as traffic through the waterway declines (see: Strait of Hormuz significance for oil trade could be cut in half by 2030, 22 July 2026).

The war has cost Tehran more than any deal would return. Iranian officials estimate war damages of $270 billion, though any Iranian assessment likely overstates the impact to bolster compensation claims. For context, the 12-day war with Israel in June 2025 caused $24-35 billion in damages for Iran (6%-9% of GDP), and the IMF projects a 6.1% contraction this year in the country’s GDP ($24 billion). Overall, the war has imposed costs in lost output and physical damage well above what Tehran is likely to recover from any agreement.

Frozen assets face both a legal cap and a timing problem that could prevent funds from moving. Iran has demanded $24 billion, of which about $20 billion is broadly securable (see Table 2). The oft-cited $100 billion figure is Iran's total overseas assets and goes beyond the scope of any potential deal. The likely model is the 2023 Qatar channel: Money moves within weeks, is limited to approved imports, and could be re-frozen again instantly. Under US law, the president must submit any Iran nuclear deal to Congress, which may not approve it. During the 60-day review window, however, the administration cannot legally release funds or lift sanctions. The June MOU was signed and collapsed inside one such window. If successive ceasefires fail within that window, no deal may last long enough for the assets to move.

A US general license to export oil could add up to $8 billion a year but would require congressional action. For 15 days in June, the US Treasury permitted Iranian oil purchases in dollars, narrowing the discount on Iranian crude from $8-$10 below Brent to $2-$3 per barrel. A durable license would be worth up to $8 billion a year against Iran's $46 billion of 2025 oil income. That figure assumes the discount disappears entirely and exports return to their 2.2 million barrels per day (bpd) peak. Permanent relief requires both an act of Congress and a new UN Security Council resolution lifting sanctions.

Hormuz fee revenue would be well below Iran's stated demands and fall over time. Iran's reported request for a 5%-7% fee on cargo value would yield around $30 billion-$50 billion a year. That is a maximalist opening position, an order of magnitude above the Suez Canal's effective rate, and Tehran is very unlikely to obtain it. Assuming traffic recovers to half pre-war levels and Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) oil and gas flows fully return, net of the Yanbu and Fujairah bypass pipelines running at capacity, $5 billion-10 billion a year is the upper limit of what Tehran can hope to obtain. That total would include about $4 billion-$5 billion a year from a $1-per-barrel fee on 13 million bpd of oil, plus a similar amount from gas tankers and other shipping.

The likely outcome is even lower. Voluntary fees paid into a management account are more likely than per-ship tolls. Meanwhile, the base is eroding: Hormuz oil flows are likely to halve by 2030 as Gulf producers expand bypass pipelines. Shipowners already pay for Iranian risk via insurers: war-risk cover per Gulf voyage rose from $50,000-150,000 pre-war to $7.5-10 million, several times Iran's asking fee.

Tehran's best case eases fiscal pressure, but political control will likely still rest on force. The proceeds from a deal would go toward addressing the government's budget deficit and the effects of capital flight ($27 billion in 2025). Oil exports suggest Iran will meet or exceed its published budget target of 1.77 million bpd at $55 per barrel, unless the US blockade endures through the rest of the year. Exports have averaged 1.6 million bpd so far this year, at prices in the low $80s. The government also retains room for further austerity, subsidy cuts, and sales of state-owned companies. The published 2026 budget already cuts real spending by 38%.

Iran's main economic problems will outlast any US deal. The upside on offer will not fully address high unemployment, currency devaluation, inflation, or corruption. As was the case in January 2026, the Islamic Republic will therefore continue to lean on force to put down popular protests, whether they stem from deteriorating economic conditions or other causes (see: Protest will pose a persistent problem for Iran in 2026, 8 January 2026).
