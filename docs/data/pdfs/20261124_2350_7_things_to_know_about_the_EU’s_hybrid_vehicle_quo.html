7 things to know about the EU’s hybrid vehicle quotas
By Koen Verhelst · Nov 24, 2026, 3:49 PM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — The EU will restrict hybrid car imports from December in a bid to counter China’s growing dominance in the global automotive industry.

The curbs, formalized Tuesday, mark a new chapter in Europe’s trade relations with China as Brussels strives to narrow its €1-billion-a-day bilateral trade deficit. Hybrids account for only a small fraction of that imbalance, but the measures could serve as a test case for further negotiations between the EU and Beijing.

Since announcing the deal on hybrids, the EU has started investigations into global imports that will most likely lead to import curbs on plastics and chemicals. Other chemical products and the machine-building sector are also in the Commission’s sights.

Beijing welcomed the outcome on Tuesday, describing the deal as a “lawful, compliant, pragmatic and balanced arrangement.”

Here are seven things to know about the EU’s temporary quotas on hybrid cars:

1. Unusual origins

The measures stem from a deal EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič struck during an October visit to Beijing to cap Chinese hybrid car exports to Europe at 350,000 a year. That reverses the usual sequence of events: Trade restrictions of this kind are normally imposed following an investigation. Judging by the temporary measures, however, the Commission’s analytical work must have started months before Šefčovič traveled to China.

2. Quite a drop for China

Chinese exports to Europe have risen tenfold in recent years, the Commission calculated, comparing 2023 to the 12 months through June of this year. In absolute terms, China already sent almost 500,000 cars into the bloc between June 2025 and June 2026. Capping exports at 350,000 means a drop of almost 30%.

3. Better than duties

From Beijing’s perspective, a negotiated cap is preferable to facing EU import duties, such as those Brussels imposed on all-electric vehicles in 2024. In that case, imports are not capped, but different producers are charged a duty that simply ends up in Europe’s coffers. What’s more, the quota will be liberalized by 6% annually once long-term measures are in place.

4. EU’s spare capacity

Because trade measures must relate to the EU’s own industry, the Commission also looked at production capacity inside the bloc. The EU produced 5.5 million cars in 2025, against a capacity of 9 million. Over the last few years, capacity utilization rates have hovered around a measly 62% and profitability is dropping fast under pressure from rising imports.

5. It’s only the beginning

For the coming six months, the EU will allow 759,681 hybrids into the bloc. That’s because the measures can only be in force for 200 days before EU countries need to vote on them. And that vote matters, because if the Commission does not get support from 15 countries representing 65 percent of the bloc’s population, the import restrictions cannot be locked in. With sufficient support, they can remain in place for four years. As only Cyprus abstained last week in a non-binding vote, the majority appears to be assured.

6. The quotas apply to everyone, but not equally

China can use the biggest portion of the quotas, capped for 200 days at 191,780 vehicles, coming in at 25.2% of all EU imports under the safeguards. Japan is a close second, at 22.5%. Turkey (16.7%), the U.K. (13.9%) and Korea (8.9%) complete the top five. The quotas are already set per quarter, with a roll-over of unused quotas into the next quarter permitted.

7. Britain gains the most

While China loses out, Britain emerges as the biggest relative winner among hybrid exporters. Annualizing the 200-day quota, the U.K. could increase its exports to the EU by 31%, to 192,000 vehicles from 147,000 in the 12 months through June. South Korea, Japan, Turkey and the U.S. also have room to increase their exports above recent levels.
