DRIVING THE DAY

EMISSION TARGET DEBATE WIDENS: The EU institutions and the bloc’s largest economies are in a political battle over emission targets for automakers, and the heavy-duty trucking sector has dropped a political bomb into the fray — saying they also need flexibility on meeting the targets or risk facing massive fines.

What happened: The CEOs of Europe’s biggest truck manufacturers used Monday’s IAA Transportation conference in Hanover to call for a weakening of the sector’s 2030 emission targets. “We face billions of euros in penalties if the targets are missed under the current framework,” Karin Rådström, CEO of Daimler Trucks and head of ACEA’s commercial vehicle board, said at a press conference on Monday. According to the lobbying group’s estimate, the trucking industry faces €2.2 billion in penalties if it misses the 2030 target by 3 percentage points.

Reminder: Truckmakers must reduce their average CO2 emissions for new heavy-duty vehicles by 45 percent compared with 2025 levels by 2030. In March, the Council granted temporary flexibility for the manufacturers to meet the 2030 target but did not change the longer-term reduction goals. Around 7 million heavy-duty vehicles operate on EU roads, accounting for about a quarter of the bloc’s road-transport greenhouse gas emissions.

What they want: The emissions targets are set to be reviewed in 2027 but the grouping of seven manufacturers — including Volvo, Iveco and Ford — want that to happen sooner. The group also wants the deadline to be delayed by three years to 2033.

This sounds familiar: Passenger car manufacturers made similar pleas in 2024 and 2025, lobbying hard for the Commission to weaken emissions standards by arguing they faced billions in fines and risked giving up their competitive edge in making combustion engine vehicles. The executive caved at the end of 2025, proposing a package that watered down the 2035 de facto combustion engine ban.

More dialogue: The Commission made its decision on car emissions following a series of strategic dialogues between Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and the CEOs of Europe’s automakers, along with a handful of lobbying groups. Now the truck industry wants the same treatment. In an unlikely alliance, the International Road Transport Union and green NGO Transport & Environment wrote to von der Leyen earlier this month, calling on her to convene a similar dialogue.

Other problems: Heavy-duty truck manufacturers also want to see significant progress on building out charging infrastructure, speeding up grid connection timelines and reducing energy costs — all crucial in getting more electric trucks on the road. “We feel that there is a lack of sense of urgency. To switch to zero emission, customer needs infrastructure,” said Rådström. “Getting grid connection for a charging site can take up to seven years today in Europe, sometimes even longer.”

RAIL

EIB POURS €730M INTO EASTERN FLANK TRANSPORT: The European Investment Bank is backing two major transport upgrades in Romania and Lithuania, putting hundreds of millions of euros into rail capacity, military mobility and port infrastructure. It’s an effort by Brussels to look at transport through both economic and security lenses.

Romanian rail boost: The EIB has signed a €630 million financing deal with Romania for the upgrade of the Brașov-Sighișoara railway, part of a wider project costing around €2.25 billion. The work covers 113 kilometers of double-track electrified railway in central Romania and is intended to increase capacity for both passenger and freight traffic while cutting journey times and improving reliability and safety.

Why that line matters: Brașov-Sighișoara sits on the Rhine-Danube and Baltic Sea-Black Sea-Aegean Sea TEN-T corridors, making it part of the EU’s strategic cross-border transport network. The upgrade is designed to remove a bottleneck on those routes and improve Romania’s rail links with the wider European network.

Faster trains, more freight: Once completed, passenger trains will be able to run at up to 160 kilometers per hour, while freight trains will reach up to 120 kph. The upgraded line is expected to handle around 4 million passengers and 6 million metric tons of freight a year, with a capacity of 29 passenger and 22 freight trains per day. Longer and heavier freight trains will also be able to use the route, strengthening rail’s ability to compete with road haulage.

Interoperability push: The railway will also be equipped with the European Rail Traffic Management System, helping improve safety and cross-border interoperability. Stations and stops are set to be upgraded too, including accessibility improvements for passengers with disabilities and reduced mobility.

Baltic defense angle: The EIB is also providing €100 million to Klaipėda, Lithuania’s largest seaport, to strengthen its role as a hub for defense, energy and trade. The full cost of the project is around €201 million, and the rest of the funding is expected to come from EU, national and other sources.

Dual-use port: Parts of Klaipėda’s civilian cruise terminal will be adapted to meet NATO requirements so allied naval vessels can be supported more efficiently. The project is explicitly framed as dual use: strengthening military mobility while keeping the infrastructure available for civilian transport and the local economy. The whole project is due to be completed by the end of 2027.

Green infrastructure too: The same investment will fund shoreside electricity for ferries, green-hydrogen production and refueling infrastructure, low-emission port vessels, dredging, reinforced breakwaters and upgraded quays for offshore wind development. The port is also part of the EU’s core TEN-T network, giving the project both transport and energy-security relevance.

Why it matters: Taken together, the two deals show how EU transport financing is increasingly being asked to do several jobs at once: improve passenger connections, move more freight by rail, support the green transition and make critical infrastructure more useful in a security crisis.

CRACKS IN THE ATHENS METRO: The president of the Hellenic Metro, Christos Karadimas, submitted his resignation on Friday following legal warnings from residents of a central neighborhood in the Greek capital, our colleague Nektaria Stamouli reports. Structural cracks appeared in homes in the Kypseli neighborhood due to tunneling work for the extension of the metro. Athens Mayor Haris Doukas called a meeting with residents on Monday.

CARS

EXCLUSIVE — GROUP CALLS FOR STRONGER IAA: A coalition of 12 companies and organizations is calling for the Industrial Accelerator Act to have higher targets for low-carbon materials and clean tech manufacturing. The group is sending an open letter to EU competition chief Stéphane Séjourné and Parliament’s co-rapporteurs on the file.

New targets: The Commission’s proposed IAA draft requires public authorities to ensure that by 2029 a minimum share of steel, aluminum and other materials is low carbon. The group wants to significantly raise those quotas to 50% for aluminum and steel, compared with the Commission’s proposed 25%.

Get a move on: The letter also calls for the institutions to define what counts as “low-carbon” by early next year. That definition is of particular concern for automakers. The Commission proposed granting them flexibility on meeting emission targets so long as any extra emissions are offset with investments in low-carbon steel.

MILITARY MOBILITY

ARCTIC TRANSPORT MOVES INTO DEFENSE MODE: Following Monday’s summit in Finland, European countries are calling for a much bigger EU effort on military mobility and northern transport infrastructure, arguing that the Arctic’s changing security environment means roads, rail, ports and maritime capabilities can no longer be treated as purely civilian assets.

Military mobility first: The joint statement from the Rovaniemi Arctic Summit calls for more investment in military mobility, cross-border connectivity and infrastructure in the EU’s northernmost regions. Leaders say stronger transport links are now essential not only for civilian connectivity, but also for moving troops, equipment and supplies across Europe’s northern and eastern flanks.

Dual use by design: The countries also want transport infrastructure built and upgraded with both civilian and military needs in mind. The statement says dual-use infrastructure and protection of critical assets on land and at sea should become cross-cutting priorities, alongside better digital connectivity and satellite services.

Shipping in the spotlight: As Arctic sea routes become more accessible, the group says the EU must improve its ability to operate safely in northern waters. That includes stronger maritime awareness, better information sharing and more capacity for ships able to work in Arctic conditions.

Icebreakers are the obvious gap: The declaration stops short of naming a common EU icebreaker fleet, but the capability is crucial to its call for safe and secure Arctic shipping. At the summit, leaders discussed expanding Europe’s icebreaking capacity, with Finland pushing the idea of closer European cooperation and potentially joint use or ownership of icebreakers.
