WHILE YOU WERE SLEEPING: Donald Trump’s Republican Party suffered a night of crushing losses in the U.S. midterm elections as Democrats swept back to power in both branches of Congress, POLITICO’s global politics bureau chief Jack Blanchard writes from Washington.

Blue tsunami: Voters across America delivered a devastating verdict on the first two years of Trump 2.0, handing Republicans dramatic losses right across the electoral map. Dems secured the U.S. Senate and are poised to win the House of Representatives, where latest projections suggest they will hold a commanding majority.

FAFO: The result is nothing short of a catastrophe for Trump, who had urged voters to pretend he was on the ballot. The result can thus be seen as a referendum on his premiership — and the message from America was crystal clear. This was a GOP defeat of historic proportions, with Dems winning Senate races in states they haven’t won in decades, even Kansas.

Trump promised to bring down the cost of living, then launched a deeply unpopular war that sent gas prices through the roof. It really is as simple as that. But he wasn’t apologetic: “I wasn’t on the ballot — if I was on the ballot, we’d have won easily,” Trump told POLITICO’s Dasha Burns in a call to our livestreamed election night show. The decision to go to war in Iran, Trump told her, was “absolutely the correct one.”

Coming attractions: The president now faces two years of political deadlock in Washington, where he will have to cut deals with Democrats to get things done. He also faces two years of aggressive congressional inquiries into his administration as Democrats take control of powerful House and Senate committees. But get ready — because there’s surely a reaction brewing.

How will that impact Europe? Some seasoned observers think it’s not only possible that Trump takes out his frustration on the continent but highly likely. Yet a Democrat-controlled Congress could help to give Ukraine some more breathing space. Jamie Dettmer lays out five things European capitals need to monitor here. We’ll have more on what it means through the day, so stay tuned.

DRIVING THE DAY

BRUSSELS GETS TO SET THE TABLE: After months of demanding a role in Ukraine talks, this afternoon Brussels hosts officials from Kyiv, NATO, the U.S. and the E3 powers — France, Germany and the U.K. — to discuss Ukraine’s reconstruction and prepare for next week’s peace talks with Russia in Abu Dhabi, three EU officials and diplomats told Nick Vinocur.

The meeting itself represents a win for the EU, which has long pushed for a presence in the negotiations — despite internal clashes about who should lead such discussions. “Europe must [have] and wants to play a role in this process,” German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said yesterday after meeting Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Berlin.

Now the EU not only has a seat — it’s providing the table for others to sit around. “Brussels is the right place to discuss Ukraine’s security guarantees and reconstruction,” said David McAllister, chair of the European Parliament’s foreign affairs committee.

The German conservative lawmaker, pointing out that the EU and its members are Ukraine’s “largest providers of economic, financial, humanitarian and military support,” argued that the meeting should deliver “concrete proposals” on how to shore up Ukraine’s economy in the longer term.

“It’s a very good initiative,” echoed Thijs Reuten, spokesperson on Ukraine for the center-left Socialists and Democrats group in the Parliament.

Guest list: For the European side, Bjoern Seibert, chief of staff to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, will represent the Commission, while Anna-Maria Boura — fresh from holding the pen on an EU-Canada joint declaration — will represent the European Council, according to two EU officials granted anonymity to discuss the sensitive negotiations. Neither the U.S. nor Ukraine has named its negotiators.

The huddle in Brussels hints at more positive dynamics between Washington and Brussels after weeks of Donald Trump appearing to side with Vladimir Putin by signing a deal to purchase Russian diesel and blasting Zelenskyy.

But the good vibes only go so far. The EU has rejected an idea floated by American negotiators, first reported by the Wall Street Journal, to restart European purchases of Russian gas via a pipeline through Ukraine. And Europeans are still fuming about U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff calling Putin a “humanitarian” — especially in the wake of strikes on an aid hub in southern Ukraine Monday that killed 31 people.

Resetting the clocks: “It’s about what Europe wants,” said the S&D’s Reuten. “It cannot be the case that we start a negotiation process based on the maximalist demands of one side and disregard the legitimate demands of the other. There needs to be a balance.”

FUNDING UKRAINE

UNPACKING CANADA’S UKRAINE PLEDGE: When Mark Carney said Canada would contribute €500 million to Ukraine at the conclusion of the EU-Canada summit, EU officials wondered what he was talking about.

The Canadian PM presented it as his country’s “initial participation” in the EU’s Ukraine €90 billion support loan. But that cash has nothing to do with the €90 billion — and is instead money to help close Ukraine’s projected budget shortfall in 2027, a senior EU official told Zoya Sheftalovich. “We aren’t talking about the same money,” the official said.

Reminder: The EU last year agreed a €90 billion loan for Ukraine for 2026 and 2027, intended to cover about two-thirds of its funding needs. Other countries, including Canada and the U.K., were expected to provide the remaining third — although much of that money has yet to materialize. To join that loan, Ottawa first needs to agree financing terms with the EU, which it has yet to do (though Carney said it intends to).

Two pots: Ukraine has since said it needs an additional €70 billion in 2027, on top of the money that’s coming from the support loan, and the EU wants partners outside the bloc to contribute. The €500 million is a welcome start, but Brussels wants more from Canada, as well as from allies including Japan, the U.K. and Norway, per a Commission official granted anonymity to discuss negotiations around the funding.

Ottawa’s pledge of €500 million is “a concrete demonstration of our close cooperation and shared commitment to supporting Ukraine,” a Commission spokesperson told Playbook. “It also underscores the importance of international burden-sharing in addressing Ukraine’s substantial financing needs.”

Tight timeline: The Commission is preparing a proposal on Ukraine financing ahead of a Nov. 26 summit at which the EU’s long-term budget and Kyiv’s needs will be discussed, the senior EU official said, adding that Brussels wants clarity over its partners’ contributions ahead of that.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The Eurogroup, the informal forum of eurozone finance ministers, said the deadline for nominations to replace Christine Lagarde as European Central Bank president is Nov. 6 … but who makes the final choice? Eurozone finance ministers will discuss the candidates at a meeting three days after that deadline and make a recommendation. But the ultimate decision will be taken by the European Council’s eurozone members. The leaders’ choice will be put to the ECB Governing Council and the European Parliament, which will give their opinions but can’t block the candidate from getting the job.

THE ROW OVER MOVING NORTH

SAFETY FEARS OR STIGMA? The head of an NGO dealing with homelessness has said Commission staff don’t want to relocate to the area near Gare du Nord because they might “encounter vulnerable homeless people on their way to and from work,” Mari Eccles reports.

Freek Spinnewijn, director at FEANTSA, wrote on LinkedIn that a recent intervention by a union representing EU staffers resisting the move north equated “street homelessness with disease, violence, and crime.”

The context: Cristiano Sebastiani, of the Renouveau et Démocratie EU staff union, wrote to Commission chiefs in September saying there was “serious concern” among EU agency staffers about the relocation. “The health, safety and security of staff must … remain a primary responsibility of the institution and be placed at the heart of any decision concerning their place of work,” he argued.

Among those leaving the EU Quarter are the European Education and Culture Executive Agency (EACEA) and the European Research Executive Agency, as well as some Commission lower management and admin staff.

“What I find unhelpful,” Spinnewijn told Playbook, “is the way vulnerable homeless people are implicitly pictured as a nuisance, a danger or a source of disease.” He added that the area already hosts the most offices in Brussels after the European Quarter. “Referring to the staff working there as second-class employees is a bit much, no?”

But, but, but … Sebastiani told Mari: “Had our communication been intended in any way to express a wish to avoid contact with homeless people, it would plainly have been reprehensible and unacceptable. That was not the case.” Employees he represents are “expatriate staff who work very late into the evening,” he said, pointing out that the entrance door of a building occupied by an EU agency “had previously been struck by a bullet.”
