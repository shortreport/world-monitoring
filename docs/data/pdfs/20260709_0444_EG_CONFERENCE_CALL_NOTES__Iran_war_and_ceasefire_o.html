Scenario Update: Shaky Peace Holds, but Risk Is Rising 

We have revised our scenario probabilities. The basecase — a shaky ceasefire holding — drops from 65% to 60%, while the risk of renewed escalation that closes the Strait of Hormuz rises from 25% to 35%. The key driver is not a US desire to resume hostilities, but Iran's active effort to maximize and institutionalize its leverage over the strait. 

The current skirmishing — Iranian attacks on tankers, US kinetic strikes on Iranian coastal military targets — is inside the base case. Both sides remain wary of general hostilities. However, the memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed several weeks ago left Iran's status in the strait unresolved, and Tehran is actively testing how far its influence extends. For more, please see Skirmishing will continue as Iran pushes its luck in Hormuz, 8 July 2026. 

 

Iran's Strait Strategy: Tolls, Leverage, and the Limits of Deterrence 

Iran's goal is to cement control over the Strait of Hormuz and monetize that control in peacetime. It is attempting to force all shipping onto the northern Iranian route through the Strait, using VHF radio warnings and attacks on vessels using the southern Omani route as coercion. Roughly 25% of shipping has used the Iranian route; the remainder has used the Omani route, the central waterway, or transited dark with Automatic Identification System (AIS) transponders off. 

Iran is unlikely to achieve a formal per-ship tolling system — the US, China, and regional states all oppose it publicly and privately. But Iran is likely to extract something, be it a fee or monetary arrangement that falls short of formal tolls. Discussions between Iran, Oman, and regional states on institutionalizing strait management are ongoing, with no resolution in sight. 

Iran's calculus is that the US is deterred from resuming full hostilities, the financial windfall from the MOU has not materialized (frozen funds have not been released; oil sanctions waiver had limited impact), and the prize of durable strait control is large enough to justify continued brinksmanship. 

The critical tripwire is a US blockade of Iranian ports. If that red line is crossed, Iran would likely respond with a sharp escalation in strait disruptions. 

Strait Traffic: Jagged Recovery, Constrained Outlook 

Traffic through the strait has reached 30–50 ships per day, consistent with Eurasia Group's prior call of 30–50% of pre-war flows within several weeks of a ceasefire. Recovery has been jagged due to several factors: 

  • Mines: An estimated 80–100 Iranian mines remain in the central waterway; JMAIC elevated the threat level to severe and the International Maritime Organization (IMO) is urging ships away from that route. 

  • Iranian aggression: Attacks on vessels using the Omani route continue. 

  • Omani route limitations: Narrow passage, shallow depths, strong currents. 

Our updated call is that traffic recovers to 70–80% of pre-war levels by end of September, assuming the shaky peace holds. 

GCC: Detente Now, Deterrence & Diversification Later 

The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states are operating under a "three Ds" framework: detente, deterrence, and diversification. In the near term (one to two years), they lack deterrent capability and must pursue bilateral arrangements with Iran, possibly including financial arrangements. Financial concessions — possibly including Iranian access to UAE financial markets — are likely, but the reported $10 billion UAE asset unfreeze cited by Reuters has not been confirmed and is doubted by speakers. 

A GCC summit in Bahrain, attended by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, produced a joint position holding the line against Iranian financial extortion. That coordination is real but fragile. 

Qatar is the most vulnerable GCC state: it has no pipeline alternative to the strait for LNG exports, and Iran appears to be exploiting that vulnerability deliberately. The UAE has largely stopped being targeted, consistent with speculation about quiet bilateral arrangements, though no financial transaction has been confirmed. 

Saudi-UAE Friction: A Price War Takes Shape 

Saudi Arabia recently cut the official selling price of Arab Light crude by the largest margin in approximately 26 years; the UAE reduced the price of its Murban crude; and both countries are loading more tankers than two weeks ago. As this price war continues we expect the Emiratis to have the upper hand, as the break-even point for their budget is much lower than that for Saudi Arabia’s. 

Beyond oil markets, the Saudi-UAE relationship is deteriorating more broadly, with reports of Saudi Arabia withholding financial transactions with the UAE signaling growing friction. Structurally, the two countries are on diverging strategic tracks — the UAE aligned with what we term the Abraham Accords coalition, Saudi Arabia part of a broader Islamic coalition balancing against Israeli regional influence as well as Iran (please see Competing coalitions will reconfigure the MENA region in 2026, 16 January 2026). We expect more volatility in this relationship in coming months. 

Saudi Arabia is unlikely to take any kinetic action against Iran; Riyadh pushed back hard against US requests to use Saudi airspace for military operations. Their east-west pipeline (5–7 million barrels per day capacity) also gives them an alternative to the strait and reduces their incentive to escalate. 

Oil Price Outlook: Band of $65–$80, With Upside Risk 

Brent was at $79/barrel at time of call, up sharply from the low $70s. Our basecase price band remains $65–$80/barrel. Key variables: 

  • China’s demand: Chinese crude imports have fallen from approximately 11 million barrels per day to approximately 6 million barrels per day. A recent report indicated China may be relaxing its ban on refined product exports and increasing crude imports — this would put upward pressure on prices. 

  • Inventory buffers: US Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) is at its lowest level since the early 1980s. European and Japanese SPRs also significantly reduced. China's buffer is uncertain but likely still substantial. 

  • Escalation scenario: A full strait closure would produce sharp upward price movement. US strikes on Iranian civilian infrastructure (power stations, desalination plants) would likely trigger Iranian retaliation against regional energy infrastructure — Saudi export facilities, UAE oil fields, Kuwaiti and Iraqi production facilities. 

Any renewed conflict is expected to be short, followed by another round of de-escalation, returning to roughly the current status quo. 

Key Watch Items 

  1. US resumption of Iranian port blockade — the single clearest trigger for aggressive Iranian escalation in the strait. 

  1. Iranian success in redirecting shipping to its northern route — if skirmishing succeeds in pushing more ships onto Iran's preferred route, it materially strengthens Iran's leverage and changes the strait control outlook. 

  1. China crude import recovery — the key demand-side variable for oil prices; early signals suggest a possible uptick. 

  1. Lebanon/Hezbollah escalation — Iran and Hezbollah have incentive to undermine the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire; this remains a potential Achilles heel for the broader MOU, though it is not currently the live flashpoint. 

  1. Israeli elections (expected by October 27) — Netanyahu faces approximately 60% odds of losing office (please see Likely opposition win would diminish threat of Israeli strikes on Iran, 2 July 2026); domestic pressure may incentivize escalatory moves against Iran or in Lebanon, contingent on US posture. 

  1. Saudi-UAE relationship — financial transaction restrictions and competing oil pricing strategies signal a deteriorating relationship with implications for regional cohesion, OPEC+ dynamics, and post-war trade route development. 

What Clients Are Asking 

Iranian route adoption by shippers: Clients asked whether Iran is succeeding in forcing ships onto its northern route. Speakers noted that roughly 25% of traffic has used the Iranian route to date, but acknowledged that continued attacks on the Omani route could push that share higher — a meaningful win for Iran if it materializes. 

LNG vulnerability and Qatar's exposure: Multiple clients raised Qatar's specific vulnerability given the absence of pipeline alternatives for LNG. Speakers confirmed Qatar is deliberately slowing LNG shipments pending clarity on Iran's strait posture, and that Iran appears to be exploiting Qatar's lack of alternatives as leverage. 

European and NATO involvement: Clients asked whether European nations would step in to help secure the strait. Speakers assessed European involvement as unlikely absent a durable end to active hostilities, and noted that the US being the sole external provider of air defense to the GCC increases miscalculation risk. 

Israel as an escalatory wildcard: Clients asked whether Netanyahu would use current turbulence to escalate against Iran. Speakers assessed that Netanyahu is unlikely to act against Trump's explicit opposition, but flagged that Trump's floor of support for Israel is durable — and that Netanyahu may calculate his downside risk with Trump is limited, especially if the MOU collapses further. 

Oil price in a breakdown scenario: Clients asked whether a renewed conflict would push prices above wartime levels given depleted inventories. Speakers declined to give a specific ceiling but noted that US SPR depletion, reduced Chinese stockpiles, and the risk of Iranian retaliation against regional energy infrastructure would all amplify upward price pressure relative to the prior conflict.

Explanation of Political Trajectories (ie, “Trajectories”)

Eurasia Group's (EG) Political Trajectories are indicators of the net impact of political factors on the macro business environment in a country over specific timeframes. For each country, two Trajectories are provided, a short-term (6 months) and a long term assessment (24 months). Trajectories are assigned by Eurasia Group’s country analysts through a structured qualitative process. Each Trajectory represents a net assessment, indicating how political changes in a country impact the macro business environment, defined as the economic and investment climate in the country. Once initiated, Trajectories are updated both periodically and as events warrant. Trajectories may be suspended from time to time if there is no active analyst opinion or analyst coverage, but the opinion or coverage is expected to resume. Trajectories can be:

Positive: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a positive impact on the macro business environment

Neutral: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a neutral or negligible impact on the macro business environment

Negative: Political factors in the country are anticipated to have a negative impact on the macro business environment

Disclaimers: Trajectories are solely for informational purposes and should not be relied upon as investment advice. Trajectories do not indicate asset price movements, and have no value in forecasting market prices. Trajectories are based on data and analysis we deem to be reliable; they are not guaranteed as to accuracy or completeness. The Trajectories indicate the political outlook in a single country, over time. Trajectories do not address sector-specific or city/regional political risks that are often different than the country-level outlook. Trajectories represent an analyst’s net qualitative assessment of a country's political outlook. EG sales, analytic staff and products may provide analysis reflecting different opinions than those expressed by the Trajectories.
