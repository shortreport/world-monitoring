DRIVING THE DAY

EPP WANTS BROAD COALITION ON EMISSIONS STANDARDS: That’s according to German MEP Jens Gieseke, the rapporteur for TRAN which is giving an opinion on the regulation. “This is why life is not easy,” he quipped to Jordyn in Strasbourg on Tuesday.

Catch up: In its December 2025 Automotive Package, the Commission proposed watering down the 2035 combustion engine ban to a 90% reduction target, with automakers offsetting the extra emissions with investments in green steel and alternative fuels. The European Parliament is now negotiating the revamped regulation, with ENVI leading talks and TRAN and ITRE giving opinions.

Try again: A vote on amendments was scheduled for Monday in the TRAN Committee but was postponed by two weeks because the political groups could not come to a compromise, Gieseke said.

Yes, but: The compromise amendments sent to shadow rapporteurs ahead of a meeting on Tuesday mirrored the draft proposal put forward by Italian EPP MEP Massimiliano Salini, the Parliament’s lead negotiator on the file. His proposal goes further than industry requests, setting an effective target of a 73% reduction including the various offsets.

Right-wing support: The amendments were welcomed by far-right groups in the shadow rapporteur meeting, with the Patriots for Europe wanting even more flexibilities, a person familiar with the discussions told POLITICO.

What’s on the table: The EPP did offer some concessions — such as barring vehicles running on alternative fuels from being eligible for super credits — but those are likely to be rejected, the person added. For his part, Gieseke said he would be open to a compromise so long as it lowers the 2035 emissions target to 80%, averages emissions over five years instead of the currently proposed three and introduces a new car category that would count Vehicles running Exclusively on Eligible Fuels (VEEF) as zero-emission.

Car summit: The discussions set the stage for the EPP’s summit with the automotive industry today. The agenda includes opening remarks from EPP chief Manfred Weber. In a position paper obtained by POLITICO ahead of the talks, the EPP took a more conciliatory tone toward the CO2 targets, calling the Commission’s proposal a “solid basis for technological neutrality.” While the ban “must be corrected in a sensible way,” the paper said that “high emission reduction targets shall ensure that the transition to battery-electric vehicles keeps rolling.”

AVIATION

EXCLUSIVE: COMMISSION CALLED TO MAINTAIN STATE AID FOR SMALL AIRPORTS: Airport lobby ACI Europe is urging the Commission to allow state aid to small airports for at least another decade. The group sent a letter — obtained by Tommaso — to Competition Commissioner Teresa Ribera on Monday, arguing that smaller airports handling fewer than 1 million passengers per year are still seeing nearly 32% less traffic than pre-Covid levels.

Connectivity matters: Passenger traffic at European airports is now 7.7% above 2019 levels, ACI said. The widening gap between big and small airports points to “lasting and structural economic and financial difficulties for these airports” and is already leading to a loss of connectivity, the letter warns.

Aid limits: In a draft revision of the General Block Exemption Regulation — the EU’s main state aid framework — scooped by our colleague Francesca Micheletti in August, the Commission proposed restricting investment aid to airports handling more than 500,000 passengers a year.

Give them a decade: “More than ever, airports require longer-term certainty,” says the ACI Europe letter, which asks Ribera to allow operating aid to all airports handling fewer than 1 million passengers a year until 2037.

AIRLINE LOBBY REOPENS FIRE OVER ETS SCOPE EXTENSION: Airline lobby IATA is taking aim at the Commission’s proposal to extend the geographical scope of the Emissions Trading System to cover flights from Europe to the Middle East.

Raising costs: The proposal “would increase EU ETS compliance costs by 40% to €280 billion over the 2027-2040 period,” IATA said Tuesday.

Happy birthday, CORSIA: The airline lobby attacked the Commission proposal on the 10th anniversary of the adoption of the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation by the U.N.’s aviation agency ICAO. The agency also marked the day by stressing that CORSIA should be “the sole global measure addressing emissions from international aviation.”

Double trouble? IATA also released a full position paper on the ETS revision proposal. According to the document, an expanded ETS would introduce a “revenue-generating charge to the same emissions” already covered by CORSIA. An IATA spokesperson told Tommaso that the Commission proposal “would mean subjecting the same emissions to multiple carbon regimes.”

The Commission rejected that interpretation. A Commission official, who was granted anonymity to discuss the matter freely, said that “the ETS and CORSIA have worked alongside each other for years, and the ETS already contains provisions to ensure that airlines only pay once for their emissions.”

The proposal, the official added, “introduces a mechanism ensuring that there is no double payment on extra-European routes covered by the EU ETS and CORSIA,” allowing airlines operating those routes to deduct their CORSIA offsetting costs.

RAIL

GERMANY PUTS ITS RAIL REBUILD ON A FIVE-YEAR CLOCK: Berlin has unveiled its first Infraplan, a rolling five-year plan meant to spell out which parts of Germany’s rail network should be renewed, expanded or upgraded through 2030. It will give the government clearer targets for holding state-owned infrastructure manager DB InfraGO to account and will be updated every year.

Why Europe cares: A big chunk of the program concerns lines that connect Germany with neighboring countries. Among the largest are the Hamburg-Lübeck-Puttgarden route toward Denmark, valued at about €8 billion; the Emmerich-Oberhausen corridor toward the Netherlands at €4.8 billion; and several large projects on the Karlsruhe-Basel axis toward Switzerland. The Angermünde-Szczecin connection with Poland, meanwhile, is listed at €759 million and is due in 2027.

2030 is not the finish line: The document covers the next five years, but some of the biggest projects will take much longer. Emmerich-Oberhausen, for example, is currently scheduled for completion in 2038, while some Swiss-facing projects also run into the 2030s.

Still a work in progress: The government says this first edition is only the starting point. A separate Zielnetz 2035 — Berlin’s blueprint for what the rail network should look like by 2035 — is still being drawn up, with key results expected in early 2027. Once that is ready, future versions of the Infraplan are supposed to rank projects according to that longer-term vision.

And more teeth are coming: From 2027, the plan is also supposed to incorporate a new multiyear agreement between the government and DB InfraGO spelling out funding and performance obligations. Berlin ultimately wants to tie the whole system to a dedicated rail infrastructure fund, so that priority projects are backed by funding commitments lasting more than a single annual budget cycle.

CARS

ŠEFČOVIČ IN THE DRIVING SEAT: Maroš Šefčovič flies today to Beijing for a visit the EU hopes will help address the bloc’s €1-billion-a-day trade deficit with China, Camille Gijs reports.

Hitting the road: Crunch negotiations between the European Commission and China are zeroing in on cars, four people with knowledge of the situation told us. Brussels is eyeing a commitment from Beijing on stemming hybrid car imports, one of the people said, making it a potential deliverable for Šefčovič’s two-day visit.

‘Proof of concept’: Options include voluntary export restraints or other measures such as price undertakings. A sectoral agreement would then serve as a template for other industries facing similar pressures, one EU diplomat told Carlo Martuscelli. Think chemicals or machinery.

But don’t go too far: The latest version of European Council conclusions, dated Oct. 5 and seen by our colleague Jacopo Barigazzi, still doesn’t mention China — even though it is expected to be top of mind when EU leaders meet in Brussels next week. EU ambassadors will discuss the conclusions today. Read the full story here.

MILITARY MOBILITY

MILITARY MOBILITY DEAL PRESSURE: EU leaders are set to call on Parliament and Council negotiators to wrap up the bloc’s military mobility regulation by the end of the year, according to draft conclusions for next week’s European Council seen by Jacopo.

Where things stand: Negotiators have made progress on the emergency mechanism, but big political questions remain unresolved, including the Solidarity Pool for identifying and sharing scarce dual-use transport assets, according to trilogue documents obtained by POLITICO.
