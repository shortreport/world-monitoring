DRIVING THE DAY

THE EU’S OTHER TOP DIPLOMAT: A migration crisis on Spanish territory and wars in the Middle East have given the EU’s Mediterranean Commissioner Dubravka Šuica one of the toughest briefs of any member of the bloc’s executive — and transformed her into a key lieutenant of Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

“No one could have predicted how quickly events unfolded since the start of the mandate,” the Croatian commissioner told Playbook in an interview on a windy morning from her hometown of Dubrovnik. “[Bashar] Assad was still there in Syria. There was no war between Lebanon and Israel.” And now, it’s the crisis in the Spanish exclave of Ceuta, which has left around 75 people dead and sent shock waves through the EU.

Still friends: EU and Morocco remain strong partners, Šuica said — despite the events of the past few days. One incident shouldn’t define the relationship, she said, adding that the EU cooperated with Tunisia to curb illegal migration by about 80 percent and should follow that model with Rabat.

Battlefields, not beaches: The gig as Mediterranean commissioner sounded uncontroversial when it was first announced, conjuring up images of island-hopping diplomacy and trips to Europe’s sunniest capitals. But it has turned into a geopolitically heavy portfolio, with Šuica becoming von der Leyen’s go-to emissary on high-profile missions, from Washington to Tel Aviv.

Mending fences: Šuica was dispatched to Israel in June to meet with Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar, just as Sa’ar was engaged in a bitter dispute with the EU’s top diplomat Kaja Kallas over reports she had likened Israel’s treatment of Palestinians to apartheid. Kallas’ predecessor Josep Borrell accused the Commission and Šuica of lacking solidarity with Kallas by not canceling the trip and the visit fed into a broader institutional clash between the Commission and the EU’s diplomatic service.

Big picture: “Without Israel, you can’t do anything,” Šuica said, referring to the need to get aid into Gaza. “We need the approval of Israeli authorities. And this is why we have to keep channels open … They are there and we need to work with them.” Her Middle East missions are all coordinated “around a table” with Kallas and other commissioners, she pointed out.

Trump gamble: Šuica also jetted off to Washington in February to attend a meeting of U.S. President Donald Trump’s controversial Board of Peace. Her presence as an observer — at von der Leyen’s request — ruffled feathers in Brussels and other capitals, which felt the EU shouldn’t give legitimacy to Trump’s initiative.

Worth it: Šuica admitted that hearing the criticism wasn’t easy for her, but insisted the trip was a “good move from our side” because it led to Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner attending the EU’s Palestine Donor Group aid conference last month. “The Americans came, they wanted to coordinate with us,” she said, adding that helping Gaza was the only goal.

Pragmatic diplomacy: Šuica is a former mayor of Dubrovnik, which managed to remain an independent republic for centuries, despite being surrounded by powerful empires. She mused that the city’s history of shrewd diplomacy runs in her blood, arguing the EU needs to be similarly pragmatic on the world stage.

For example: EU members are right to be outraged by the deaths of thousands of Palestinians, she said. “What happened in Gaza, of course, nobody can justify this.” But she urged the bloc, which is deadlocked on suspending its association agreement with Israel, “to be calm” and avoid moves that could be politicized in Israel’s election in October. “It’s not our role to interfere in their elections.”

SOCIALISTS’ CHINA STANCE

SMART, NOT SOFT: MEP Javi López has defended the Socialists and Democrats from accusations the group is too soft on Beijing. “It’s not about being harder or softer, it’s about how to be smarter in defense of European interests,” the Spanish S&D lawmaker and European Parliament vice president told Zoya Sheftalovich.

Trip recap: López spoke to Playbook after joining an eight-member Foreign Affairs Committee delegation to China last month, during which MEPs met Foreign Minister Wang Yi and other senior officials. The talks were frank, López said. “We think the only way … is with dialogue, through negotiations, to respect each other, and not a confrontational approach that doesn’t serve the European interest.”

Sino-Russian ties: The delegation raised China’s role in the war on Ukraine, dual-use goods reaching Russia and the need for Beijing to use its influence on the Kremlin to help secure peace. Chinese officials told the MEPs that Beijing wasn’t a party to the war, didn’t sell weapons to Russia, supported dialogue and believed Europe should be involved in negotiations.

Report still TBC: Playbook reported a couple of weeks ago that the S&D was pushing to soften a critical Parliament report on China, including by dropping a description of Beijing as a “strategic rival.” Critics accused the group of caving in to a threat from Beijing of “consequences” if the report passed unchanged — a warning the S&D said it wasn’t aware of.

“Values-based realism”: Europe should cooperate with China where interests align and compete where they don’t, López said. The EU must be “principled but pragmatic, without being naïve,” he added, describing the S&D approach as “values-based realism.”

About that report: It’s still being hashed out, López said, adding he had no further information on the roughly nine-month delay, beyond that talks with the lead MEP on the file remained constructive.
