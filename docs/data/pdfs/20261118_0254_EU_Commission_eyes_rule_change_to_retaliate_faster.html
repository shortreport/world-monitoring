EU Commission eyes rule change to retaliate faster against China
By Camille Gijs · Nov 17, 2026, 6:50 PM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — The European Commission is considering changes to its trade enforcement rules that would allow Brussels to respond more swiftly to serious economic threats as it develops a new instrument aimed primarily at China, three people with knowledge of the talks told POLITICO.

The Enforcement Regulation allows the EU to impose countermeasures in unresolved trade disputes at the World Trade Organization. It was revamped in 2021 when the WTO’s highest appeals court stopped working after Washington blocked new appointments. The changes allow the EU to respond when a trading partner appeals a WTO panel ruling into a legal void, whether by imposing tariffs, setting quotas or restricting trade in services.

Now Brussels is exploring whether the regulation could be tweaked to allow a broader, quicker response to actions by trading partners that harm EU interests, the people said.

The work follows a request last month by France and Germany for a new “second strike” tool that would allow the bloc to address economic threats more promptly and effectively.

Other names are being considered for the new instrument, as the bloc wants to ensure it doesn’t borrow from nuclear jargon and draws attention instead to its responsive nature.

Political appetite to respond more forcefully to China has grown across the bloc amid concerns about the EU’s €1-billion-a-day trade deficit with the world’s leading exporter. At their October summit, EU leaders tasked the European Commission with “urgently” developing new instruments to redress those “imbalances.”

The new instrument would be part of a broader trade defense package and could still change before the Commission’s formal proposal, currently scheduled for Dec. 9.

Last month, EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič struck a deal with Beijing that would cap annual imports of Chinese hybrid cars. He’s now looking to replicate such agreements with chemicals and machinery, sectors heavily impacted by Chinese overcapacity, during the next rounds of negotiations with Beijing in January and March.

Too slow, too toxic

The EU already has a powerful tool to respond to economic bullying: the Anti-Coercion Instrument, which allows Brussels to impose tariffs or embargoes on goods from countries that oppress the European Union.

The process, now deemed too heavy, gives member countries a say at two stages: first in determining whether economic coercion has taken place, then to weigh in on the Commission’s proposed response.

The Anti-Coercion Instrument, also known as the trade “bazooka,” has never been used.

The European Parliament in January requested that the EU executive use the Anti-Coercion Instrument after President Donald Trump threatened to annex Greenland, a Danish territory. But Brussels ultimately held off while it pursued talks, and Trump later softened on the threat.

The episode revived questions about how quickly and credibly the bloc could deploy its economic arsenal in a crisis.

“There is an idea of an emergency reaction toward China based on this French-German paper. Now it’s a question of how to do it, with different options. One would be to modernize the ACI and change the decision-making process. Another would be to change the Enforcement Regulation,” said Bernd Lange, chair of the European Parliament’s trade committee.

Lange, who helped negotiate both instruments, said the Enforcement Regulation was designed to enforce WTO decisions and cautioned that using it for an emergency response, without a WTO dispute behind it, could be “a bit tricky.”

The European Commission declined to comment.
