China’s electric aircraft threaten Europe’s aviation ambitions
By Tommaso Lecca · Nov 25, 2026, 6:00 AM · View in your browser

BRUSSELS — The unveiling of an electric aircraft by China’s state-owned planemaker is sparking fears among European developers that Beijing could turn its battery supremacy into an advantage in the race to build the next generation of aircraft.

The CE-25A, developed by COMAC with Chinese battery-maker CATL and other partners, is a 19-seater electric aircraft designed for routes of up to 400 kilometers. It completed its first piloted flight earlier this month but is still far from carrying passengers: COMAC has applied for a type certificate from China’s aviation regulator, the first step toward commercial service.

The flight matters beyond aviation. CATL already supplies automakers around the world, including Volkswagen, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Tesla and Ford, as well as domestic brands such as BYD, MG and the Geely Group, which includes Polestar and Volvo.

If Chinese suppliers can apply that battery expertise to aircraft technology, European manufacturers may soon find themselves relying on Chinese know-how and components for future aircraft in a sector where Europe has long been a major force.

The announcement of the new electric plane “changes everything … because [the Chinese] don’t need us anymore,” said Eloa Guillotin, CEO of France-based Beyond Aero, which is developing a hydrogen-electric business jet.

“COMAC’s progress is not a surprise,” Airbus said in an emailed statement, “as developing an electric aircraft of this scale was part of their strategic plan.” The European planemaker said its “ambition focuses on significantly larger aircraft.”

COMAC’s other commercial aircraft, powered by conventional jet fuel, operate in Asia and still rely on Western technology, including engines made by CFM International, a joint venture between U.S.’s GE Aerospace and France’s Safran. Its narrow-body C919 is also seeking EU approval to carry passengers in Europe. The CE-25A points to a different possibility: an aircraft project built with domestic partners and a battery industry China already leads.

“In a conventional aircraft, the strategic and critical part is the engine, and that remains a Western stronghold,” said Marina Efthymiou, professor of aviation and lead of the Sustainable Aviation Hub at Dublin City University. “In an electric or hybrid-electric aircraft, a large part of that strategic weight shifts to the battery, the motors and the power electronics. Those are the parts of the supply chain China dominates.”

She added: “China will own the electric powertrain that every future aircraft, from air taxis to hybrid regional airliners, will depend on.”

First cars, then planes

While competition from Chinese rivals is already eroding Europe’s leadership in the car industry, now the European aircraft manufacturing sector is feeling the heat, too.

But Efthymiou urged caution in comparing it to cars: “We have to be careful with the analogy.”

“In aviation the binding constraint is certification, and certification is slow by design,” she added, referring to the regulatory process an aircraft must undergo before entering commercial service.

Even after being certified by the Chinese regulator, the new e-plane would need approval from the European Aviation Safety Agency before entering commercial service in Europe. COMAC has been seeking the same from EASA since 2019 for its jet fuel-powered, narrow-body C919.

“An electric aircraft with a novel battery and distributed propulsion will not have an easier path abroad than a conventional narrow-body with Western engines,” Efthymiou added.

Squeezed between China and the U.S.

The European Union, which aims to become climate neutral by 2050, has been pushing for years to electrify aviation, which is responsible for around 2.5% of global CO2 emissions.

In 2020, EASA became the first regulator worldwide to issue a type certificate for a fully electric aircraft, the Pipistrel Velis Electro. The e-plane, a two-seater used for pilot training, was produced by a Slovenian manufacturer.

Europe’s difficulty in retaining its most innovative companies is another factor worrying the industry.

According to Antoine Toulemont, EU affairs adviser at Aura Aero, a French electric aircraft developer, the situation for European planemakers “is going to be worse than for the automotive [sector]” since the EU is losing important players.

For example, Pipistrel was bought by U.S. industrial giant Textron in 2022 for over $200 million. And Toulemont cited Heart Aerospace, a company founded in Sweden that relocated to California last year. Heart completed the first flight of a battery-electric demonstrator in August.

According to Efthymiou: “Europe offers research grants that are valuable but small, fragmented across member states, and poorly matched to the late-stage capital an aircraft program needs.” This, despite “European willingness to have zero direct emission aircraft,” said Beyond Aero’s Guillotin.

“In the U.S., they don’t have that much political intention to build electric aircraft, but they have the funding capital to fund startups,” Guillotin said. “In China, they have both.”

If the EU lags behind in electric aviation, it could take a market-driven approach without distinguishing between domestic and foreign technology as long as it helps achieve its decarbonization goals.

But this approach would create new dependencies, Toulemont warned, arguing that Chinese and U.S. manufacturers would prioritize domestic demand for zero-emission planes.

“You do not really own your decarbonation goals if you don’t produce in Europe,” he said.

Europe’s response

COMAC’s maiden electric flight has made the European industry take notice.

The continent’s aircraft manufacturers insist they can keep pace with advances in electric aviation while extending the range of electric aircraft through hydrogen propulsion.

Airbus guaranteed its intention “to deepen our collaboration with [German aircraft engine manufacturer] MTU early 2027, through the creation of a joint venture dedicated to the development and commercialisation of a fully electric fuel cell propulsion system based on hydrogen,” the company said in an emailed statement.

Aircraft developers Aura Aero and Væridion reaffirmed that they expect their regional hybrid-electric aircraft models to be carrying passengers by 2030.
