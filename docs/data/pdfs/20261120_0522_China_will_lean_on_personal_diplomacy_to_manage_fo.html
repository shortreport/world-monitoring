US-China

China will lean on personal diplomacy to manage foreign relations. On 19 November, presidents Donald Trump and Xi Jinping met on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Both sides emphasized the importance of leader-level engagement to maintain bilateral stability.

  • Xi stressed the importance of “constructively managing differences that could not be immediately resolved” to prevent escalation. The statement suggests that the talks addressed new export controls imposed by the US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security following the Nvidia scandal. Beijing will likely use summit meetings and existing channels to convey its red lines on export controls, while pursuing an extension of the trade truce separately.
  • The Bureau of Industry and Security published an interim final rule on 16 November that imposes license requirements to close a loophole that currently allows Chinese firms to access computing power remotely. Beijing will likely wait until early next year to see how the Trump administration intends to enforce it before issuing countermeasures.
  • APEC produced no announcement of an extension of the trade truce. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Vice Premier He Lifeng did not meet in person. Their most recent phone call revealed disagreements over the conditions for extending the truce. Both sides are expected to continue talks before the G20 in mid-December. Trump also used the bilateral meeting to push China to commit to agricultural purchases, likely as a precondition for extending the truce.
Foreign Policy

Beijing achieved its major goals at APEC. China successfully leveraged the summit to strengthen trade and investment ties with other APEC members, deepen trilateral cooperation with Russia and the US, and diplomatically isolate Japan. Xi put on a friendly face in his bilateral meetings with nearly every leader in attendance—Japanese Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae was a notable exception—while seeking to showcase China’s technological prowess. He also promoted China’s vision for an “orderly multipolar world and inclusive economic globalization” while minimizing discussion of China’s swelling trade imbalances or its growing tech competition with the US.

  • Beijing will likely seek to build on the inaugural China-Russia-US trilateral summit. Xi’s three-way meeting with Trump and President Vladimir Putin was heavy on optics and light on deliverables. It produced a short joint statement focused on UN reform, increasing production of refined oil products, and nonproliferation. Nevertheless, China will welcome additional trilateral engagements before Trump leaves office.
  • Canada-China ties will continue to deepen. Xi and Foreign Minister Wang Yi both met with their Canadian counterparts, continuing a diplomatic thaw that began over the last year, lowering tariffs, increasing direct flights, and deepening financial sector cooperation. However, the gains were modest as Ottawa is wary of moving too far from Washington.
  • Japan-China ties will remain in a deep freeze. Takaichi received a notably frostier reception than other visiting leaders: She was greeted with little fanfare and denied a meeting with Xi. This treatment indicates that Beijing has no interest in repairing ties with Tokyo, likely for as long as Takaichi remains in office.
Taiwan

Taiwan’s smooth, low-profile APEC participation signals cross-strait stability. Stan Shih Chen-jung, co-founder of electronics hardware firm Acer, led Taiwan’s APEC delegation. Taipei was worried that China would downgrade its treatment at the gathering as part of Beijing’s broader pattern of diplomatic coercion. However, Shih’s delegation did not report any instances of Chinese interference with their participation. Xi greeted Shih with a handshake at the dinner gala on 18 November.

  • Beijing accommodated Taiwan’s participation despite its clear dislike of President William Lai. Shih’s delegation said it was in frequent communication with the Chinese side, which gave “moderate consideration” to Taiwanese demands regarding gala seating and photo taking.
  • Shih’s participation largely accomplished Lai’s political objectives. The two main ones were to raise Taiwan’s international profile and promote its critical role in the tech industry and supply chain resilience. According to the delegation, Shih engaged with at least 13 APEC members, including brief interactions with US officials.
  • Minor diplomatic slights did not materially detract from Taiwan’s participation. Taiwanese media highlighted the email-only invitation, the limited welcoming entourage, and Shih’s placement in a back corner of the group photo, but these issues did not prevent the delegation from advancing its objectives.
Politics

Tighter party discipline will slow the rollout of supportive economic policies. Since the Fifth Plenum concluded, the People's Daily has published a series of articles, mostly by Politburo members, on the implementation of its recent decision on strict party governance. Several extend disciplinary scrutiny to local economic issues, holding officials accountable for debt and wasteful investment. Local governments will likely become more cautious, slowing the rollout of supportive economic policies.

  • Officials will be assessed on debt metrics as well as growth outcomes. Vice Premier He Lifeng criticized officials’ fixation on GDP, vanity projects, subsidy competition for investment, and statistical fraud. He also called for leadership handovers to record debt and unresolved liabilities, so officials’ borrowing histories will follow them to their next posts.
  • Closer supervision will favor caution over initiative. Politburo member Li Hongzhong called for discipline commissions to report at least every six months on the local leaders they inspect. A Central Party School commentary demanded swift, faithful execution of central decisions and condemned delay. Officials face pressure to act, but they also face exposure if they make mistakes.
  • Past disciplinary campaigns suggest paralysis is likely to result before Beijing adjusts. After the 2014 to 2015 anticorruption push, local inaction became widespread enough that Beijing sent State Council inspection teams and later adopted rules that tolerated some official mistakes. The next watchpoint is whether the December Central Economic Work Conference offers similar flexibility. Without it, local follow-through onon central directives to support the economy will likely lag in early 2027, even as discipline gradually curbs new hidden debt.
Graphic of the Week
  • China’s October investment data fell short of consensus expectations, underscoring that the slowdown extends beyond property. Infrastructure investment fell 5.1% in January–October from the same period a year earlier, while manufacturing investment declined 2.9%. Electronics remained a bright spot, but the broader deterioration suggests that industrial strength and government support have not been enough to offset weak domestic demand.
