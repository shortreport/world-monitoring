DRIVING THE DAY

EXCLUSIVE — MOROCCO MAKES A CHINA THREAT: Morocco’s hopes of being included as a trusted partner in the Industrial Accelerator Act are vanishing, leading Rabat to threaten a “Plan B,” Industry Minister Ryad Mezzour told Jordyn in an exclusive interview. The minister was in town this week for a 48-hour lobbying blitz, meeting with France, Denmark, Italy, Lithuania and Sweden.

Plan B: The strategy would see Rabat cozying up to the very country the IAA is designed to keep out: China. “We need to find new trusted partners for our development. Nobody is excluded, including the Chinese,” Mezzour said.

All about cars: The automotive industry is Morocco’s top industrial employer with 85% of the country’s cars exported to Europe. Renault and Stellantis have extensive manufacturing there, along with a slew of local parts suppliers. But those investments are a lot less appealing if vehicles produced in the country do not qualify for public subsidies or meet local content requirements under the IAA.

Chinese boon: Chinese carmakers have announced over €10 billion in investments in Morocco — largely for electric vehicles and batteries — making the country one of China’s top automotive investment destinations, according to a report from consulting firm AlixPartners. Those investments are part of what worries the EU about including Morocco in a definition of Made in Europe, fearing it would allow Chinese carmakers access to the EU market.

Local market squeeze: Excluding Morocco from Made in Europe means Rabat will be courting even more Chinese investments, which Mezzour said could undercut Europe’s automakers present in the local market.

Fading hopes of being in the IAA: “We are less and less confident that the European doors will be open to Moroccan cars in the future,” Mezzour said. While the one-on-one meetings went well, with the minister saying that diplomats appeared to understand Morocco’s position, the end result is the same — the EU’s interests win out over those of Morocco. Mezzour said one official told him: “We need to protect ourselves, and you’re collateral damage.”

Rabat-Brussels friction: Mezzour warned that being excluded from the IAA would upend Morocco’s relations with the EU. Since the measure was first proposed, the discussions have been going in the “wrong direction for us,” he said. “It’s been a long agony for the sector.”

Read more: Jordyn has the whole story here.

It’s not just Morocco: Morocco is far from the EU’s only trading partner worried about being left in the cold. Turkey also has vehicle production and is similarly seen as a potential destination for the Chinese to circumvent the regulation. Nearly 812,000 vehicles crossed from the EU into Turkey in 2025, and during the same year, Turkey exported 798,000 vehicles back to the EU. Its Trade Minister Ömer Bolat was in Brussels last month to advocate for Turkey’s inclusion as a trusted partner.

More lobbying: Bolat is in Germany today at an automotive summit to again plead for Turkey to be included in the Made in Europe club.

MARITIME

EU SOFTENS STANCE ON GLOBAL SHIPPING EMISSIONS DEAL: The EU is shifting its position on a global shipping decarbonization deal ahead of talks at the International Maritime Organization that have so far failed to produce a breakthrough, according to a Council document obtained by Martina and dated Nov. 3.

Easing in: Brussels would accept a “smoother” start to yearly cuts in the greenhouse gas intensity of ship fuels, as long as reductions steepen later to reach net zero by or around 2050, according to the compromise text drawn up by the Irish Council presidency. It is also open to a range of 2040 targets, provided they remain consistent with reaching net zero and do not undermine the IMO’s 2023 climate strategy.

More ways to comply: Ships could also pay into emissions-cutting projects at an IMO-set price, provided that does not become a way to avoid cutting emissions directly and that the projects deliver real, lasting reductions that are not counted twice. The EU would also consider bonus credits for ships using zero- and near-zero-emission fuels, as long as the incentive is time-limited.

But one red line remains: The document is categorical that Brussels would not accept a deal requiring the EU to scrap or suspend its own maritime Emissions Trading System, or one that undermines its right to maintain regional measures.

Greek warning: Greek shipowners accused Brussels of taking an overly rigid line and argued that protecting the EU’s own regional system is limiting room for compromise at the IMO.

Caveat: EU governments agreed their position on Nov. 13, meaning the final mandate may differ from this Nov. 3 draft.

MULTIMODAL TRANSPORT

COUNCIL PUTS MORE GUARDRAILS AROUND MULTIMODAL: The Irish presidency is further defining which ticketing platforms, journeys and commercial practices would fall under the EU’s proposed multimodal booking rules, according to a compromise text dated Oct. 30 and obtained by Martina.

Cities largely out: The rules would not apply to daily urban, suburban or metropolitan commuting, even across borders. But regional or cross-border services serving a city would remain covered where their main purpose is not commuting.

Who counts as big: The compromise introduces clearer thresholds for consumer-facing booking platforms: a provider would have “significant market presence” at EU level if it accounts for at least 10% of ticket sales by value or volume in a transport mode, rising to 50% at national level. The Commission would translate those percentages into absolute thresholds using ticket-sales and Eurostat data, among other sources.

But no forced deals: Fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory conditions would not amount to an obligation for major booking platforms to conclude commercial agreements with operators or business users. Public service contracts would also be protected from those commercial-agreement rules.

Sponsored results stay: Booking platforms could accept payment to promote certain transport products, but users would have to be able to opt out throughout the booking process. Sponsored results would also have to be clearly labeled and displayed proportionately alongside other options.

Protect the data: New wording would shield trade secrets and commercially sensitive information when platforms share booking and sales data, while data requested by public transport authorities would have to be necessary, proportionate, anonymized and kept away from commercial use unless the platform agrees otherwise.

Enforcement gets more structure: National regulators would cooperate through a network on cross-border enforcement, with the Commission able to provide guidance on fair commercial conditions and assist regulators overseeing platforms with significant EU-wide market presence.

And AI gets a look-in: Five years after the rules start applying, the Commission would have to assess the role of AI in the ticketing market, alongside how the fair-commercial-conditions rules are working.

Aviation carve-out: The presidency also clarifies that airline tickets sold under code-share or interlinking agreements would count as the product of the marketing carrier only, rather than as a multi-operator product — keeping standard airline partnerships from automatically falling into that category.

One later start: The regulation would generally apply 24 months after entering into force, but the provisions on displaying transport emissions would not kick in until Dec. 2, 2030.

CARS

THE CAMPAIGN TO SAVE RADIOS IN CARS: The radio industry is campaigning to prevent the EU’s proposed overhaul of the Digital Networks Act from removing radios from cars. “It would seem like a strange idea of progress if the next generation of cars make it harder to listen to radio than the last generation,” Chris Doyle, the top executive at leading radio operator Bauer Media Audio Ireland, told our colleague Mathieu Pollet. Industry representatives and policymakers gather in Dublin today to discuss the issue.

State of play: Under the draft bill, carmakers must ensure that radio receivers can pick up digital terrestrial broadcasts — but only if they choose to install one in the first place. That leaves a potential loophole for manufacturers opting for internet-only and smartphone-based audio systems instead.

Missed opportunity: “Having access to something that important like radio shouldn’t depend on the commercial decisions of individual vehicle manufacturers,” Doyle argued. The industry is making the case for preserving free-to-air radio, which can provide critical information during emergencies and remain accessible when broadband and mobile networks go down.
