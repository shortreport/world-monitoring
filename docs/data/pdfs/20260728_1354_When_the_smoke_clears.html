DRIVING THE DAY

EXCLUSIVE — AFTER THE FIRES: The European Commission will consider whether the wildfires causing so much devastation in southern Europe mean it needs to bring in extra measures to tackle such crises.

It won’t have a big rethink just yet — first comes the literal firefighting. “Our immediate priority is supporting efforts to save lives and bring the fires under control as quickly as possible,” Crisis Management Commissioner Hadja Lahbib told Playbook.

But once the emergency phase ends, “as we always do, we will carefully assess our overall response to this crisis,” Lahbib said. The Commission will draw lessons from this summer and consider whether “further measures or initiatives are needed.” She added: “Our policies evolve continuously based on experience.”

What can Brussels do? The EU executive doesn’t have many powers in this area — firefighting remains a national responsibility. The Commission steps in when capitals request help through the Union Civil Protection Mechanism and its strategic reserve tool, rescEU, which is coming under pressure as several countries ask for aircraft at the same time.

Still waiting for the planes: For now, Europe’s firefighting fleet remains largely rented. This summer, rescEU has access to 22 firefighting aircraft and five helicopters, provided by member countries. The Commission is getting its own permanent fleet of 12 Canadairs and five helicopters — although deliveries will run until 2030 because manufacturers can’t build them fast enough, an EU official said.

What’s happening: France and Spain are battling what the EU describes as an “exceptionally severe” wildfire season. Authorities are in a race against time before temperatures rise again in the middle of the week. In the two countries more than 300,000 people have been evacuated.

Future plan: At the moment, the most concrete idea for tackling such emergencies is the Commission’s guidelines to national governments on integrated wildfire risk management — a non-binding roadmap on prevention, land management and coordination — along with a gradual strengthening of rescEU, Commission spokesperson Anna-Kaisa Itkonen told Playbook.

The budget hope: “The next MFF isn’t agreed yet, so there might be adjustments,” Itkonen pointed out. As part of a compromise text designed to get a deal on the EU’s next seven-year budget, Cyprus, which held the six-month presidency of the Council in the first half of this year, earmarked €9.73 billion for the Union Civil Protection Mechanism.

The European Parliament wants to go further: French liberal MEP Grégory Allione — a firefighter since the age of 17 — told Playbook the Parliament is pushing for €12 billion, arguing Europe needs not only more EU-made firefighting equipment, but also more people on the ground, including through an EU directive on citizen engagement.

The context: The EU’s permanent firefighting fleet currently under construction was funded with just €600 million.

Capacity isn’t everything: More planes alone won’t solve the problem, a second EU official said. “You can buy 24 Canadairs. You can buy 50. It will never be enough.”

One option on the table: stripping the rebels of their party membership, Polish media report.

The pause in fighting was put to the test on Monday, with Saudi Arabia, Jordan and Iraq reporting drone attacks, per Reuters.

MAGA AND UKRAINE

MR. ZELENSKYY GOES TO WASHINGTON: Volodymyr Zelenskyy is in the U.S. capital today to attend Lindsey Graham’s funeral and meet Donald Trump. His visit comes as some in the MAGA universe seem to be learning to love Kyiv, Zoya Sheftalovich reports.

Far-right activist Laura Loomer, who has in the past amplified Russian narratives, visited Kyiv last week, met Zelenskyy and said she had been “bamboozled by Russian propaganda.” Podcaster Tim Pool, another former critic of Ukraine, said this week that Kyiv was “fighting for its very existence against an evil dictator.”

Even Vice President JD Vance, among Zelenskyy’s top U.S. antagonists, earlier this month softened his tone, praising Ukraine’s defensive strategy and saying Russia’s offensive capabilities are “getting close to zero.”

“There is something happening there,” Trump’s former special representative for Ukraine negotiations Kurt Volker told Zoya. Trump’s declaration at this month’s NATO summit that he had a “good relationship” with Zelenskyy — alongside his backing for Ukrainian production of Patriot interceptors — was “a signal to the MAGA world that it’s OK to support Ukraine,” Volker said.

Note of caution: The new mood music doesn’t amount to a full MAGA embrace of Kyiv and if Zelenskyy wants to get the anti-interventionist wing of the Republican Party on his side, he’ll have a lot more convincing to do during his time in the U.S. Some close to the White House also question the sincerity of Loomer’s about-face.

Sanctions test: MAGA’s apparent evolving stance will face an immediate test in the U.S. Senate, where lawmakers are considering Graham’s stalled Russia sanctions bill. Senate Majority Leader John Thune wants a vote before the August recess, but Trump’s push to include Iran measures has complicated negotiations.

Backing a winner: Ukrainian officials believe Trump is responding to results on the battlefield, where Russia is losing troops, money and momentum, and Ukraine looks increasingly like a winner. Volker appears to agree: “Trump sees that Putin is losing, and he sees that Zelenskyy has actually pulled this all together and is winning.”

DUBLIN, WE HAVE A PROBLEM

A MESSAGE FROM MERZ: German Chancellor Friedrich Merz is heading to Ireland today with a warning: If the Irish presidency of the EU Council doesn’t produce a substantially cheaper blueprint for the EU’s next seven-year budget, hopes of reaching a deal this year could be dashed.

Cut it down: Merz will use his three-hour visit with Irish Prime Minister Micheál Martin to press Ireland — which in the second half of 2026 chairs negotiations among EU governments — to make far deeper cuts to the European Commission’s nearly €2 trillion proposal, my Berlin Playbook colleague Hans von der Burchard reports. Cyprus, which held the presidency in the previous six months, made only modest changes.

Why Germany matters: As the EU’s largest net contributor, Berlin’s backing is essential. Merz believes that only a considerably smaller package can create a “landing zone” acceptable to Germany and other budget-conscious capitals, according to a European diplomat familiar with his position, granted anonymity to discuss the confidential plans.

The clock is ticking: Merz will warn that failure to strike an agreement this year could push the negotiations deep into 2027 — when elections in France, Italy, Spain and Poland risk further complicating talks.

No new-tax enthusiasm: The pair will also discuss proposals for new sources of EU revenue. Berlin doubts measures such as a digital levy are politically viable and remains hostile to the Commission’s proposed charge on large companies.

Why bother? Brussels is considering exempting loss-making and smaller firms, but the diplomat noted that would also reduce the money raised. “Is it worth the trouble?” the diplomat asked, echoing Merz’s argument that the levy would undermine European competitiveness.

BRUSSELS CORNER

WEATHER: Sunny. High of 29C.

BRUSSELS IS A WASTELAND: Not everyone is on a beach sipping a piña colada right now. Playbook is using the summer break to clear out its trash. But where do you go in Brussels if you have a load of literal junk to get rid of?

The region has six Recyparks — recycling centers … or dumps, to you and me. One is in Auderghem and serves residents of four communes: Auderghem, Ixelles, Etterbeek and Watermael-Boitsfort. Another, in Woluwe-Saint-Pierre, is open to residents of both Ws: Woluwe-Saint-Pierre and Woluwe-Saint-Lambert.

The remaining four — Buda in Neder-Over-Heembeek, Demets in Anderlecht, and Sud and Humanité in Forest — can be used by anyone domiciled in the Brussels region. Take your ID card or proof of residence — to be checked, not thrown away — and sort everything before you arrive.
