Document: Irish presidency proposes emissions credits banking for automakers
By Jordyn Dahl · Nov 23, 2026, 2:23 PM · View in your browser

The Irish Council presidency is proposing new flexibilities for automakers as part of the Automotive Package, including allowing them to bank emissions credits from one year for use in the next, according to a steering note obtained by POLITICO.

EU member country diplomats will meet on Wednesday for working party talks to discuss the auto package, which the European Commission put forward in December 2025.

The Council presidency had earlier this month asked deputy ambassadors to give political guidance, after talks stalled at the working party level.

Under the latest draft text — also obtained by POLITICO — the Irish presidency would keep the Commission’s proposed mandate of cutting emissions by 90% from 2021 levels by 2035, so long as the extra emissions are offset with investments in green steel and alternative fuels.

But the presidency has proposed an additional mechanism: Automakers would be able to bank credits earned in 2029 if they beat emissions targets that year, and use them to meet the stricter targets that will come into force in 2030 and 2031.

The credits would be capped at 7 grams of CO2 per kilometer, according to the steering note, which sets out options or guidance for the discussion. That is about 14% of the 49.5-gram-per-kilometer average target that applies from 2030, and is more than the 4-gram cap on existing eco-innovation credits for those years.

Van makers, meanwhile, are given a reprieve in the text, with the presidency proposing averaging their emissions over a five-year period from 2025 to 2029, in addition to the Commission’s proposed three-year emissions averaging from 2030 to 2032.

Two issues of contention remain: Which low-carbon materials can be used as offsets for extra emissions, and the fate of a new category of vehicles running exclusively on alternative fuels.

Several member countries argue that previously proposed deals would not give “sufficient support for the development of low-carbon steel and aluminium” compared with the Commission’s proposal, the steering note says.

Countries are also split between those wanting more support for alternative fuels and those saying that creating a new category of vehicles running on such fuels raises “concerns on environmental integrity.”

The latest Council presidency compromise proposal would freeze the utility factor, which is used to calculate emissions from hybrid vehicles based on how often they are driven on the electric motor versus the internal combustion engine.

In the deal, the factor would be frozen until Jan. 1, 2030 for vehicles with an electric range of at least 100 kilometers, meaning it would be calculated on lab data rather than real-world data. That data shows that people use the engine more often than the motor, but carmakers want to keep the lab calculation because a lower official figure helps them meet fleet CO2 targets.

The presidency’s proposal would also let automakers use super credits and emissions offset mechanisms while also pooling with other manufacturers to meet their reduction targets, a major ask from the automotive industry. The Commission’s plan required them to choose between the two.

There is growing pressure for Council to reach a deal. The European Parliament reached a draft compromise in October, with a plenary vote currently set for Dec. 14.
