DRIVING THE DAY

TURKEY-EU IMPASSE OVER IAA INCLUSION: The EU-Turkey Business Summit on Tuesday was dominated by one question: whether goods manufactured in Turkey would be considered ‘Made in Europe’ under the Commission’s proposed Industrial Accelerator Act.

Talking Turkey: Trade Minister Ömer Bolat was clear: If the EU wants closer ties, it should ensure Turkey is not blocked from the IAA. European Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič, however, failed to offer any concessions or reassurances to Ankara on the issue. Instead, he used the summit to highlight the close economic ties and trade relations between the EU and Turkey, saying: “What is very important right now is that we will continue our engagement and we will work toward deepening our trade relations even further.”

What’s at stake: Ankara is on a lobbying blitz in Brussels to ensure its automotive industry isn’t cut out of the Industrial Accelerator Act, which would set local content requirements for the sector in public procurement and subsidies. European manufacturers are also raising the alarm that their Turkish factories will be cut off if Turkey is left off the list of countries counted as Made in Europe.

Scale of the problem: Turkey is the EU’s third-largest export market, with nearly 812,000 vehicles crossing from the EU into Turkey in 2025. The EU imported roughly 798,000 vehicles from Turkey last year, according to data from car lobby ACEA. More than half of the components in vehicles manufactured in Turkey by Ford come from the EU, Nicola Walker, Ford’s head of government affairs for Europe, said at the summit. Ford has a joint venture with Turkish producer Koç.

All about the IAA: Yaprak Balkan, Turkey’s ambassador to the EU, estimates that 70% to 80% of her time is now spent on the IAA and urging European regulators to consider Turkish goods as Made in Europe. “We just need to find the stamp of approval on the arguments that we are putting forward,” she told summit attendees.

We did our part: The EU-Turkey Customs Union established in 1995 requires Ankara to match EU regulations, Balkan said, adding the country has over 60 people working in Brussels “constantly following every new regulation that the EU comes up with and making sure that we keep up the standards.”

A threat? If the IAA is implemented in its current form, it would go against the trade agreement as it stipulates no tariffs on industrial goods, Balkan warned. “We must abide by our commitments, ” she said of the trade pact.

Urban legend: The Commission has raised fears that including Turkey as Made in Europe would give Chinese manufacturers a back door into the EU market. Balkan hit back on that notion, calling it an “urban legend,” adding “our own industry is facing serious pressures from unfair competition.”

AVIATION

GLOBAL AVIATION TAKES AIM AT ETS EXTENSION AND E-SAF MANDATES: The backlash against EU climate policies is spreading beyond European airlines, with global industry lobby IATA and Turkish Airlines criticizing the Commission’s plans to expand carbon pricing under the Emissions Trading System and maintain synthetic fuel (or e-SAF) mandates.

Aviation is special: Marie Owens Thomsen, IATA’s senior vice president for sustainability, said at a press conference during the lobby’s four-day gathering in Brussels that the ETS has succeeded in reducing emissions in other sectors but would not deliver the same results in aviation.

Milking airlines: “The ETS has worked for the power sector because [it] has alternatives,” she said, pointing to solar and wind energy, which allow electricity producers to reduce fossil fuel use and their need to buy ETS allowances. “We don’t have any alternative,” she added, referring to jet fuel, as SAF currently accounts for only a small fraction of airlines’ fuel consumption. “So, we’re the ultimate cash cow in this system.”

Money flying away: Owens Thomsen also reaffirmed the sector’s preference for the U.N.’s Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation. “’It doesn’t meet my criteria,’ it sounds very petulant, and it’s obviously self-appointed criteria,” the IATA representative said, referring to the EU’s critical assessment of CORSIA, which it considers insufficient to meet the bloc’s climate objectives.

Turkish Airlines Chairman Murat Şeker criticized the Commission’s proposal to extend the ETS to flights to the Middle East, including Turkish airports. Speaking at the EU-Turkey Business Summit, Şeker warned that “measures based on geographic location, on flight distances could create unequal and disproportionate cost burdens.” The EU, he added, “should pursue sustainability without creating new barriers to fair competition.”

Grilling e-SAF mandates: IATA also reaffirmed its opposition to mandatory SAF and e-SAF blending requirements. Synthetic fuel targets are a particular concern, since the lobby estimates that if the EU’s e-SAF mandates were missed entirely, penalties could amount to €8 billion in 2030 and €16 billion in 2031. Unlike Airlines for Europe, which wants the targets postponed, IATA opposes mandates altogether. “We don’t support mandates in the first place … incentives are the starting point when it comes to new technologies,” said Preeti Jain, IATA’s head of net zero research.

E-SAF industry reacts: Producers, however, argue that weakening the mandates would undermine investment in European production. “It is no longer a question whether there will be e-SAF available in the market in 2030, but whether that e-SAF is going to be produced in Europe or imported into Europe,” said Mariano Berkenwald, head of strategy at German e-fuels producer Ineratec.

A gift to China? Speaking to Tommaso on behalf of the European e-SAF Coalition industry alliance, Berkenwald pointed to emerging e-SAF production in South Africa, China and the U.S. But “the reason no major projects are under construction [in Europe] is because of the lack of binding offtake agreements,” he added, referring to long-term purchase contracts that airlines could sign with European e-SAF developers.

Angry climate campaigners: The airline industry’s complaints didn’t get much traction with Diane Vitry, aviation director for green lobby Transport & Environment. “While airline executives gather at IATA’s symposium to whine about EU climate laws, the numbers tell a different story: compliance with SAF laws and the ETS would add only €28 (3%) to a Stockholm-Bangkok flight with a stopover in Munich in 2035.”

MARITIME

BLACK SEA SHIPPING RISK IS BECOMING EUROPE’S PROBLEM: A fresh wave of drone attacks on merchant ships off Bulgaria is sharpening concerns that the war’s maritime spillover is becoming a European security, trade and investment problem.

More vessel attacks: Two more merchant ships were hit by drones off the Bulgarian coast early Tuesday. The Ukrainian-owned and Maltese-flagged Chelsea 3 was struck near Bulgarian territorial waters; all 15 crew were brought safely to Varna, though five needed medical attention. A second vessel, the Guinea-Bissau-flagged HS Dignity, sank, with five crew rescued and 11 reported missing. Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev summoned Russia’s ambassador and said Sofia would seek international help to investigate the strikes and strengthen air and maritime surveillance. He stressed there was not yet clear and conclusive evidence assigning responsibility.

The investment hit: Oleg Grygoriuk, chairman of the Marine Transport Workers’ Trade Union of Ukraine, confirmed to Martina that repeated strikes are making Ukraine port reconstruction harder because investors know infrastructure repaired today could be hit again tomorrow.

The dilemma: Grygoriuk said keeping ships away from high-risk areas would be safest for crews, but Ukraine still depends on maritime traffic and dock workers depend on functioning ports for their livelihoods. He wants commercial vessels and port infrastructure kept out of the fighting rather than shipping halted, warning that further disruption to Ukrainian exports would also hit countries beyond Europe.

Wish list: Stronger air defenses around Ukrainian ports are part of the answer, but Grygoriuk said EU leaders should also use their political weight to push for commercial shipping and port infrastructure to be protected. The warning lands as EU leaders prepare to discuss Ukraine this week.
