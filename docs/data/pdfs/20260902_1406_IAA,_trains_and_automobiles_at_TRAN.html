DRIVING THE DAY

BACK TO SCHOOL: The European Parliament is back in session with a public hearing this afternoon on the Industrial Accelerator Act and the first TRAN Committee meeting of the fall.

First up: This afternoon, the three committees responsible for the IAA are hosting a public hearing at the European Parliament, with industry and academia to discuss the bill unveiled in March by the European Commission. The outcome has far-reaching implications for the automotive sector.

Carmakers, assemble: The three-hour hearing is divided into two blocs. The first will cover the proposal’s controversial trade dimension of what qualifies as “Made in Europe” and the foreign direct investment conditions. The automotive industry is set to dominate the discussion, with Volkswagen’s EU lobbyist Sebastian Schaffer attending, along with Benjamin Krieger, the secretary-general of car supplier lobbying group CLEPA.

Next steps: A draft joint report by the three lead committees — industry, internal market and international trade — is penciled in for Sept. 7. The final report should be ready by the end of the month, with a plenary vote on in December.

Trains, too: TRAN also kicks off its own work today, with lawmakers holding an exchange of views with the Commission on the Passenger Package — the trio of proposals unveiled in May covering multimodal booking, rail ticket issuance and protections for passengers travelling on single tickets.

Rome has thoughts: The Passenger Package drew pushback from major rail operators when it landed in May, and over the summer, Italy’s lower house added its own formal objection, arguing that the rules are heavier-handed than the problem actually calls for. Rome’s worry is that solutions designed to fix sluggish, less competitive rail markets elsewhere in Europe could end up doing more damage than good to a market that’s already liberalized like Italy. Other partially liberalized rail markets include Spain, Austria, the Czech Republic, Sweden, the U.K. and Poland.

MARITIME

IMO DAY 1: The first day of talks on the IMO’s contested Net-Zero Framework ended with negotiators still far apart, but with countries signaling a willingness to compromise as the organization tried to steer governments toward text that could command broader support by the end of the year.

Some common ground: Several countries indicated they could consider elements of competing proposals if that helps build consensus — with Ireland, speaking for the EU, open to ideas from Brazil, Liberia, Japan, Tuvalu and China, provided they remain compatible with the IMO’s 2050 climate goals. The chair’s own guiding questions, obtained by POLITICO, similarly focus talks on where the existing framework can be adjusted, from emissions trajectories to compliance options and the future of the Net-Zero Fund.

Saudi draws the line: Riyadh was the clearest holdout, rejecting the framework’s current structure and arguing that cleaner-fuel requirements must reflect what is actually available, affordable and scalable. Saudi Arabia and Kuwait also challenged the chair’s approach with their own paper, also seen by POLITICO, arguing that negotiations must consider outright alternatives rather than merely amendments to the existing framework — including whether to pursue a technical measure first and leave an economic component for later.

Next up: No breakthroughs yet, but plenty of negotiating room. The chair will spend the coming days working through the framework piece by piece — with the emissions trajectory, compliance mechanisms, fund and clean-fuel rewards still among the biggest fights ahead.

SHIPOWNERS BACK INDIA RECYCLING MOVE: Europe’s biggest shipping groups are throwing their weight behind the Commission’s proposal to add two Indian shipbreaking yards to the EU’s approved list, arguing in a statement exclusive to POLITICO that Europe needs more recycling capacity as growing numbers of vessels reach the end of their lives.

Industry united: BIMCO, European Shipowners, the International Chamber of Shipping, Intercargo, Intertanko and the World Shipping Council said facilities outside the OECD should be approved whenever inspections show they meet EU safety and environmental standards. They argue the two Indian yards have invested substantially in infrastructure, worker safety and environmental management to reach those requirements.

Remember the other side: That pits shipowners squarely against environmental groups, which argue Europe already has enough capacity and should keep more end-of-life vessels — and their recyclable steel — inside the bloc rather than encouraging dismantling in South Asia.

TEN-T

EU LOOKS NORTH FOR ITS NEXT TRANSPORT CORRIDORS: Europe needs stronger ports, railways and roads across the Arctic as military mobility and supply-chain security reshape the region’s infrastructure needs — just as Russia and China step up efforts to develop the Northern Sea Route into an alternative Asia-Europe trade corridor.

Russia goes east: Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov on Monday described growing Chinese use of the North Sea Route as the beginning of a “new phase” in Arctic development, pointing to regular container services and plans to increase traffic.

Europe connects west: Speaking at the EU Arctic Forum on Tuesday, Mikko Tarvo, program manager for the EU-funded New North transport, logistics and security-of-supply project, said northern Scandinavia needs to ensure it is “well connected to the West.” He highlighted bridges, roads and links to ports as immediate priorities, while calling for longer-term investment in northern railways and pointing to Finland’s rail-gauge transition as something he’d like Europe to adopt as a shared project.

Military mobility moves north: Tarvo also said “military mobility and the dual use are now the drivers” of infrastructure development and land-use decisions in the region as northern transport links take on greater strategic importance. But the structures for coordinating those links have weakened: Cross-border working groups previously operated under the Barents Euro-Arctic framework, he said, but that cooperation ceased following Russia’s war against Ukraine. “Right now we do not have a structure to replace that,” Tarvo added, leaving EU-funded projects to help maintain cooperation between northern regions.

Why it matters: With the Commission preparing a new Arctic strategy, the High North is fast becoming a transport and security question for Brussels — one where Moscow and Beijing are setting the pace on one route while Europe tries to build out its own.

CARS

GERMAN STUDY ADVOCATES FOR LIFECYCLE ASSESSMENT ON CARS: The Technical University of Munich published a study on Tuesday that lambasts the 2035 emission target for automakers because it calculates emissions at the tailpipe rather than through the entire lifecycle of the vehicle. The study calls the method a “scientifically indefensible stance by the EU.”

Coincidence? The university’s study was commissioned by the university’s president at the start of the year “given the political decisions on the future of Europe’s automotive industry.” The study’s recommendation of moving to a lifecycle assessment mirrors the demands of Munich-headquartered automaker BMW.

“Misdirection”: The study’s lead author, Johannes Fottner, calls the Commission’s method “the greatest economic and environmental misdirection,” arguing that EVs are considered climate neutral while investments in green steel or circular economic practices “go unrewarded.”

Political ramifications: The paper made a splash in Germany, with the Christian Democrats seizing it as another reason to further weaken the 2035 emission targets. “Once again, it becomes clear: We urgently need the real end to the combustion engine ban from the EU,” Bavarian state minister Marcus Söder wrote on X.

Hitting back: “The fact that electric cars don’t fall from the sky, but require raw materials and energy, is nothing new,” German MEP Michael Bloss of the Greens said in a statement to POLITICO. Accounting for every ton of CO2 would be create “an unparalleled bureaucratic monster,” he continued.

Researchers distance themselves: The study is similarly dividing researchers at TUM, with the university’s automotive engineering chair Markus Lienkamp telling a German outlet that “relevant professors from TUM … were not involved. I would have rejected the paper as a reviewer.”

COMPETITION

EUROPE LAGS IN SCALING CLEAN TECH: The authors of a new study on European competitiveness made clear in a presentation to Parliament that the EU is falling behind the U.S. and China in clean technologies. “The conclusion is not that EU lacks capacity to innovate. It’s that EU struggles to translate research into private investment and scale,” Sara Banfi, the study’s lead author, said in her opening remarks.

What’s this about: The ENVI Committee commissioned a study looking at how the EU compares to the United States and China on clean technology and overall competitiveness. (You can read it in its entirety here.)

The reaction: “Europe does not lack technology or ambition. We repeatedly fail when innovation has to become industrial production at scale,” said Bulgarian MEP Kristian Vigenin of the Socialists and Democrats. French MEP Anne-Sophie Frigout, meanwhile, said the study’s results show the EU needs “to go further to improving European preference [requirements], especially in the steel industry.” The Commission wants to do exactly that through the Industrial Accelerator Act.

PIT STOPS

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned in a speech Russia’s airspace is becoming unsafe as Kyiv ramps up its drone attacks on the country. He urged airlines, insurers and governments to reconsider flights to Russian airports.

A deal has been reached for the former Audi factory outside Brussels that will convert the site into a business park.
