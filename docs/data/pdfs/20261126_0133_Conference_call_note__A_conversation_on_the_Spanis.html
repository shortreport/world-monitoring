Spain election update: A conversation with Kiko Llaneras on opinion polls

25 Nov 2026 11:32 EST

Long-term trajectory:

Probability Landscape: Right Wing Favored, But Margin Has Narrowed

Eurasia Group assigns an 80% probability to a PP + Vox majority government. Llaneras places that probability at 83%, having moved from 90–91% just one week prior. He breaks down the remaining probability as roughly 5–6% for a straightforward PSOE-led left-wing majority, 5–6% for a hung parliament scenario in which a pro-independence Catalan party (Junts) becomes the deciding actor, and approximately 1% for Coalición Canaria serving as kingmaker.

The hung parliament scenario — previously considered too marginal to assign a probability — is now described as "still very unlikely but a realistic possibility."

The structural reason the race is not considered close: Llaneras calculates PP + Vox need approximately 47% of the combined vote to secure a majority; polls currently show them at approximately 49%, a 2-percentage-point margin. That margin is not impossible to close, but it exceeds the error observed in 2023, which was already quite large.

What Is Driving the Late Left Surge

The PSOE surge has two sources. First, undecided left-leaning voters from the 2023 election are returning to PSOE — a development Llaneras says he anticipated. Second, and more novel, PSOE is absorbing voters from smaller national left-wing parties with a national base (Podemos, Frente Amplio), whose support is collapsing. This consolidation is particularly valuable under the Spanish electoral system, which tends to favor larger parties.

Notably, regional left-wing parties (Bildu, Esquerra Republicana, BNG) are not transferring voters to PSOE. The consolidation is a national-level phenomenon only.

Llaneras attributes the surge primarily to mobilization, not persuasion: polarization has locked most voters into their blocs, and the late movement reflects base activation rather than cross-bloc switching. Approximately 10% of 2023 PSOE voters who said they would switch to the right have not reversed course — that flow is unchanged.

Polling Reliability and the 2023 Comparison

The left needs a polling error larger than 2023's to win. Llaneras notes that in the last 20 years, when a late trend is present, polling errors have tended to run in that direction — which is why he has adjusted his probability downward. However, he flags two complicating factors: (1) since 2023, regional and European elections in Spain have seen right-wing parties underestimated, suggesting pollsters may have overcorrected; and (2) herding is visible in the current polls, with pollsters converging toward the left rather than independently arriving there. Private trackers are circulating but have not shown dramatic movement. Prediction markets have barely moved since Monday's final public polls.

Fiscal Policy: Promises Will Be Scaled Back

Spain has experienced meaningful fiscal slippage in 2026, complicating compliance with the EU fiscal framework from 2027 onward. The incoming PP-led government is expected to renegotiate its adjustment path with the European Commission, softening but not eliminating the required consolidation. The combined tax-cut pledges of PP and Vox are described as "quite substantial" and will need to be scaled back significantly, delayed, and spread across the parliamentary term. The first budget since 2023 will likely be passed within a few months of the new government taking office, and it will be the first real test of this constraint.

Migration Policy: Structural Tightening, Not Mass Deportation

Migration has been a key driver of Spain's economic outperformance versus European peers since 2022, with net migration averaging well above half a million annually. The incoming government will tighten pathways to residency and citizenship, restrict welfare access to some degree, and end mass regularization programs — the most recent of which will have regularized upwards of one million undocumented residents. Mass repatriations of the kind seen in the United States are not expected. The net effect is downward pressure on labor supply growth — gradual, not a cliff.

Vox: Strong Result, Managed Expectations

Vox is expected to roughly double its seat count but has lost some ground in the final weeks of the campaign. Llaneras describes the party as "overpriced" relative to expectations. If Vox lands at, say, 16–17% vote share versus expectations of 20%, the narrative will likely be one of disappointment despite the objective gain. In government, Vox is expected to receive three to four ministries and focus primarily on migration, law and order, and cultural issues. Santi notes Vox has moved away from hard Eurosceptic positions and is more supportive of Ukraine aid than peers such as Rassemblement National or the AfD (Alternative für Deutschland). An EU exit referendum is extremely unlikely for the foreseeable future.

Key Watch Items

  1. Turnout on Sunday: Polls project approximately 75% turnout, which is already factored into the 83% PP+Vox call. Turnout above 75% would likely benefit the left, though the effect is dampened because many 2023 non-voters are currently expressing intent to vote right.
  2. Private polling trackers in the final days: No dramatic signals as of the call, but movement in private trackers or prediction markets in the final 48 hours would be the earliest indicator of a polling error materializing.
  3. Seat arithmetic in the hung parliament scenario: If neither bloc reaches a majority, Junts' posture becomes decisive — and its willingness to support a PP government that includes Vox is highly uncertain.
  4. 2027 budget negotiations: The first budget since 2023 will reveal the true extent of fiscal constraint and how much of the PP+Vox tax-cut agenda survives contact with EU fiscal rules.
  5. Migration policy implementation: The pace and scope of tightening will have direct implications for Spain's labor supply and economic growth trajectory.
  6. Catalan independence dynamics: Llaneras notes pro-independence sentiment has partially recovered in polls, but the movement is fragmented, with younger Catalans shifting toward right-wing parties including the pro-independence but right-wing Aliança Catalana. A repeat of 2017 is assessed as very unlikely for the foreseeable future.

What Clients Are Asking

Higher turnout and who it benefits: Clients asked whether elevated turnout would help the left. Llaneras confirmed it likely would, but noted this effect is partially offset by the fact that 2023 non-voters are currently polling heavily for right-wing parties — meaning a 75% turnout is already consistent with a PP+Vox majority.

Vox's likely influence in government: Clients probed how powerful Vox will be as a junior partner. The consensus view on the call is that Vox will secure three to four ministries and focus on migration, law and order, and cultural issues, with limited influence over economic or EU/NATO policy. Santi noted Vox has moderated its Eurosceptic positions.

Polling error risk and the 2023 comparison: Clients asked whether pollsters have corrected for the 2023 miss. Llaneras said it is genuinely unclear — subsequent elections have shown a right-wing underestimation bias, suggesting possible overcorrection, but the current late trend mirrors 2023 and he has adjusted his probability accordingly.

Democracia 21 and small-party spoiler potential: Clients asked about the new liberal party. Llaneras assessed they will not win seats — they have not registered in polls, and polarization has effectively eliminated the center. Podemos has a roughly 40% chance of retaining seats; Cañamero/Fiesta (referenced as "cabola fiesta" in the transcript — note to analyst: verify party name) has approximately a 25% chance.

Catalan independence under a conservative government: Clients raised the risk of renewed independence pressure. Santi assessed the probability of a concerted independence push or referendum as very low for the foreseeable future, regardless of polling sentiment, given the experience of 2017.
