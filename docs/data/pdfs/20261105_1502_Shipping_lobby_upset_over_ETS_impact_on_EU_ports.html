DRIVING THE DAY

SHIPPERS WARN OF ETS IMPACT: The European Commission’s proposed changes to the Emissions Trading System for shipping could encourage cargo operators to shift traffic away from European ports, the World Shipping Council warned as negotiations on the ETS revision intensify.

Intermediate destinations matter: Simon Bergulf, vice president for the environment at shipping lobby, warned about proposals affecting transshipment, the transfer of goods from one ship to another at an intermediate port. “One of the industry’s concerns with the maritime ETS revisions is its impact on European ports handling cargo that neither originates in nor is destined for the EU, but simply changes ships on its way between other countries,” Bergulf told Tommaso. He warned that cargo “could move through competing ports outside Europe instead, where it avoids additional ETS costs despite producing virtually the same emissions.”

The current rules: Shipping companies pay ETS charges on 50% of emissions on voyages between EU and non-EU ports. Under the Commission’s proposal, ETS charges would apply only to the outbound leg of such journeys. But the WSC argues that competing non-EU ports would still offer an alternative with no ETS costs. The lobby is calling for a complete exemption from ETS charges for transshipment operations involving cargo that neither originates in nor is destined for the EU.

Competition struggles: “That’s a real competitiveness challenge for major transshipment hubs, particularly on the Mediterranean coast like Spain, Italy and Malta,” Bergulf said. “Keeping this traffic in Europe means more ships calling at European ports, better connections to global markets and more economic activity.”

Good side of the proposal: However, the lobby supports some of the Commission’s proposals, such as the introduction of a Sustainable Maritime Alternative Propulsion mechanism, which would channel ETS revenues into funding for the maritime sector’s decarbonization, particularly through the use of sustainable fuels.

Low-hanging fruit: “The challenge is that sustainable fuels still cost two to four times as much as conventional fuels, making it difficult for shipping companies to use them regularly,” he added, noting that using ETS revenues to close that price gap is one of the quickest ways to cut shipping emissions today.

CARS

AUTOMOTIVE PACKAGE PASSES COMMITTEES: The files that make up the Automotive Package — reforming emission targets for automakers, electrifying corporate fleets in member states and the omnibus — were approved by the three committees leading on the files on Wednesday, setting the stage for a plenary vote in December.

Reminder: While each of the regulations is a separate file, they were negotiated as a package in a deal reached by the European People’s Party, Renew and the Socialists and Democrats.

A resounding meh: The ENVI Committee passed the proposal to reform the 2035 combustion engine ban. It agreeing to lower the emissions reduction target from the current 100% cut from 2021 levels to 87.5% — when accounting for Made in Europe steel investments. The committee passed the compromise with 48 in favor and 30 against — hardly a resounding endorsement.

Differing opinions: The EPP, which campaigned on overturning the 2035 de facto combustion engine ban, declared victory after the vote. “We fought against the combustion engine ban from the very beginning, and today we have secured the majority to deliver,” said Massimiliano Salini, the lead negotiator on the file.

Sector wants more: “The agreed text includes some additional flexibility, but for passenger cars this remains too limited in scope and duration to adequately address the industry’s concerns,” car lobby ACEA said in a statement after the vote. Automotive supplier lobby CLEPA echoed the sentiment, saying “further improvements are needed to safeguard” the role of hybrids.

Concession on fleets: The deal among the political groupings means setting binding targets for member states to electrify their corporate fleets is all but dead. However, MEPs François Kalfon and Tiemo Wölken, the co-rapporteurs on the file, secured a concession that would prohibit capitals from giving public money to fossil-fueled cars starting in 2028.

Transport operators cheer: The International Road Transport Union’s general delegate to the EU, Raluca Marian, said Parliament’s committees had sent a clear signal that “imposing purchasing targets through Member States does not make vehicles affordable, nor does it create the infrastructure or energy supply needed to operate them.” She argued that replacing binding targets with aspirational ones showed the scale of political opposition to the Commission’s approach to corporate fleets.

Green anger: Climate groups were dismayed at MEPs for nixing the mandatory targets. “Today’s vote turns binding electrification targets into suggestions, even though they only required large companies to follow the market,” Sofie Grande y Rodriguez, clean fleets policy manager at Transport & Environment, told Jordyn in a statement. “Those with the biggest purchasing power should be leading the transition, not getting a free pass.”

More time for lobbying: This isn’t the final Parliament stance on the Automotive Package. It still needs to go before plenary in December, and that hinges on progress on the Industrial Accelerator Act. The S&D’s final approval “depends on whether an ambitious compromise on the IAA can be reached by then,” Wölken said.

SCOOP — IAA DECEMBER VOTE TIMELINE SHIFTING: Negotiations on the Industrial Accelerator Act could delay progress on the Automotive Package until next year. The target deadline of Dec. 1 for a parliamentary committee vote is unlikely, three people familiar with the talks told POLITICO. In other words: Parliament may not have a negotiating position before early next year. A likely scenario is for the committees to skip the plenary vote and move straight to negotiations with the Council, the people said.

Weekly shadows: The three co-leaders on the file have set a tight calendar, with shadow MEPs on the file meeting every week. Technical talks have focused so far on the least controversial aspects of the file, leaving for last the thorniest topic of determining which third countries qualify as Made in Europe.

AVIATION

EXPERT VIEW ON AIRLINE BOMB THREATS: Seven European flights were targeted by bomb threats earlier this week, prompting police searches that found no explosives aboard any of the aircraft. Jan-Arwed Richter, CEO of JACDEC, an aviation-safety analysis firm, said the risk of someone smuggling a bomb aboard an airliner is low. “Today there is hardly any thinkable way to get explosives aboard a commercial flight,” Richter told Tommaso. “The last real bombing of a flight took place on Oct. 31, 2015 on a flight from Egypt to Russia because of security loopholes and personnel shortcomings that were rectified immediately,” he added.

Fear factor: “In almost zero cases of bomb threats, there is actually any explosive device on board,” Richter said. “It is a tool to stoke anxiety and fear of flying.”

MARITIME

SHIP RECYCLING VOTE GOES THROUGH: A motion for resolution from leftist and centrist lawmakers urging the Commission to reconsider adding two Indian beaching yards to the bloc’s Ship Recycling List passed on Wednesday — 48 in favor, 26 against and five abstaining.

Parliament’s message: “With the adoption of this objection, we are preventing an unlevel playing field for EU ship recycling yards. This objection sends a very strong signal to the Member States to ensure any recycling facilities added to the list are held to our standards,” Renew MEP Gerben-Jan Gerbrandy told POLITICO.

Industry pushes back: Shipping groups say the two yards have already been through the EU’s inspection process and meet its environmental and safety requirements. “The Commission made thorough inspections and found that these yards clearly meet the stringent EU safety and environmental standards for ship recycling,” said Sotiris Raptis, secretary-general of the European Community Shipowners’ Associations, warning that blocking them would discourage further investment in safer recycling capacity.

Same line from container shipping: The WSC’s Bergulf said the Indian facilities had spent the past decade investing to meet EU standards, arguing that recognizing yards which pass the bloc’s audit process is important if Europe wants to encourage more responsible ship recycling capacity worldwide.

EU HARDENS LINE ON TURKEY’S EAST MED CLAIMS: The Commission’s latest report on Turkey’s EU accession progress takes a firm line on Ankara’s maritime border deal with Libya, calling the 2019 memorandum “null and void” and saying it cannot produce legal effects for third countries.

Why it matters: The deal draws a maritime boundary between Turkey and Libya that cuts across waters claimed by Greece and Egypt and ignores Greek islands including Crete. The Commission also criticized Ankara for obstructing survey work for the planned Greece-Cyprus electricity interconnector.

TOURISM

COMMISSION STEPS INTO THE TOURISM FRAY: The EU doesn’t have much sway over tourism, but a growing backlash against mass tourism is prompting Brussels to get involved. The Commission announced its first Tourism Strategy on Wednesday, aimed at steering visitors toward less-frequented destinations and spreading demand across the year.

Political pressure: The strategy never uses the word “overtourism,” a term often used to describe tourism pressures in highly popular hotspots — but it’s filled with ideas on how to lessen the negative impacts of a sector that generates 10% of the EU’s GDP and provides more than 20 million jobs.
