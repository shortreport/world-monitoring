Is AI a bubble? Will AI displace workers faster than it creates jobs? Will AI turbocharge political polarization even more than the internet already has? Can regulation effectively contain the use of AI for cyberattacks, biological weapons development, and deepfakes? How will the US-China rivalry influence AI development and regulation? Can Europe hope to compete at the AI frontier? How should middle powers and the Global South position themselves to not be left behind in the AI race? How do we build trust in the age of AI? How will the politics of data center construction affect the US midterm elections? 

Sitting at the crossroads of technology, politics, and economics, Eurasia Group professionals partner with our clients every day to address such questions. We were early in this space by starting to build out our Geo-technology practice in 2016 to understand issues at the intersection of technology and geopolitics. AI is the most important innovation since then and poses incredible opportunities as well as potential threats across all sectors, and everyone is exposed.

The upside is hard to miss, whether that's monitoring rainforest usage and deforestation in Brazil, machine-learning flood models that improve forecast lead times and response rates, recovery of cultural heritage, or medical breakthroughs, including global accessibility for tuberculosis screening and prevention of diabetes-related blindness in India and Thailand.

The upside has also clearly been noticed by investors, as inflows have driven a record-breaking equity boom in US stocks despite major headwinds, including rising interest rates and a global trade war. Our Geoeconomics team examined this AI market boom and compared it to other revolutions in their recent Top Calls report.
Along with AI’s potential, however, Eurasia Group is also mindful of the hazards. Domestic and international governance and oversight are falling further behind the multitrillion-dollar investments fueling the headlong rush to build AI’s foundations: power, chips, and digital skills.

We have highlighted the key risks from the widening gap between technological advancement and regulation of AI in our flagship Top Risks report each of the past four years: Weapons of Mass Disruption (2023), Ungoverned AI (2024), AI unbound (2025), and AI eats its users (2026). In this year’s report, we highlighted that AI can’t live up to investors' expectations in the short term. Under pressure to generate revenue and unconstrained by guardrails, leading AI companies will adopt business models that threaten social and political stability—following social media's destructive playbook, only faster and at greater scale.

But what about whether AI will replace workers faster than it creates jobs? There are companies that are starting to see a return on their AI investment, but the workforce transformation is in its early stages. Right now, success is best measured in terms of productivity, whereas the technology’s impact on profitability is less clear. Indeed, some companies are citing AI as a reason for layoffs, but hard evidence of its impact on the bottom line remains scarce (The AI jobs backlash is arriving before broad job losses, 8 September).

From our DC office, I work with colleagues who cover every region, market, and risk on the map. Technology and AI run through all of that work, and that of our clients across sectors.

The question clients keep asking us is whether AI will take jobs. I think the more useful version is who will be hired, who will be kept, and how the work itself will change.

Here's how we see it right now. AI is changing who companies hire before it changes how many. Three years into enterprise adoption, we don't see substantial displacement in the aggregate data, and we don't expect it over the next year.

Where firms are adjusting, it's through hiring freezes rather than firing, since a freeze is easy to reverse and a layoff isn't. Where we do expect pressure is on mid-career roles, where the work is mostly throughput, and judgment isn't yet the main part of the job. Judgment is the piece AI can't really do.

Meanwhile, the political and popular conversation is outrunning the evidence. Four in five Americans expect AI to take jobs, and legislators in both parties talk about displacement as if it were established. Over the next few years, how people perceive AI's effect on work will matter about as much as the actual changes.

That's part of why using AI and using AI well are two different things. There's a lot of experimentation happening, which is healthy, but experimenting isn't adopting, and adopting isn't necessarily conferring advantage. Knowing which questions to ask and recognizing when the answer on the screen isn't what you need are roles for people—not AI models. 

That's the job: helping you ask, and answer, the right questions.
The million-dollar question is whether AI changes the relative value of/premium commanded by foxes (generalists) vs hedgehogs (specialists), and if so, what does that mean for Eurasia Group analysts?

As a company with its fair share of subject matter experts, Eurasia Group is using AI to streamline operational tasks and things that are necessary but not core to our business. We’re using the time this frees up to strengthen what sets us apart—like our robust human networks. When it comes to analysis and advisory work, we use AI as a thought partner alongside our colleagues—an aid and provocateur, not a replacement.

In the spirit of asking better questions, we encourage employees to experiment with AI to enhance their workflow. For our research teams, this means automated conference call notes, standardized decks, assistance with timesheets, and added insight to inform our analysis. These tools are designed to help analysts do what they do best: apply judgment and assess what happens next, rather than rely on AI to answer questions. 

Our AI enablement team is setting up ad hoc monitors and sentiment trackers for emerging crises—for example, compiling satellite data on ship traffic in the Strait of Hormuz so analysts can quickly get an overview and respond faster to breaking developments. Noah Daponte-Smith (senior analyst, United States) is using AI to aggregate and screen public opinion polling to build a poll average that will provide additional insight into the upcoming Israeli and French elections. Allie Korbey (analyst, Consumer & Retail) is building a dynamic dashboard that consolidates, organizes, and tracks key news and Eurasia Group notes relevant to each client's core issue areas—streamlining and deepening client engagement.

Some tasks are high-friction because they demand deep thinking; others are high-friction because they're cumbersome, not cognitively demanding. Just as Allie laid out, our value-add for clients isn’t necessarily compiling the information but rather making sense of it.

Jon Lieber (head of research) says he’s noticed the biggest improvements in speed and quality across our research products, including notes and conference calls, which are now clearer and easier to digest. Analysts also have more time for higher-value work that can be done only by humans, such as applying judgment and experience to make sense of data, building policymaker networks, mentoring junior colleagues, and connecting face-to-face with clients.

Connecting our global network of executives, thought leaders, and policymakers

The breadth and depth of our network have always been a key strength for Eurasia Group. While AI tools may escape containment and hack Hugging Face, they cannot nurture, learn from, and connect our global network and clients.

We see our relationship with clients as a two-way street. The best relationships are those in which we learn from each other. In July, we had Tom Key (Mubadala Investments), who previously established BCG's Executive Placement Services and The Bain Executive Network [BEN], stop by our NYC office to lead a workshop on networking for virtual and in-person audiences. 

Tom emphasized that there are a lot of unknowns when it comes to AI, including where the technology is headed and what it will mean for our careers, our day-to-day lives, and for future generations. But what we do know is that conversations and personal connections aren’t going anywhere; if anything, they will be more valuable than ever before.

GZERO Summit Asia is purpose-built for the Indo-Pacific moment

I was just on the other side of the world in Singapore on 10 September to kick off our inaugural GZERO Summit Asia. It was purpose-built for the Indo-Pacific moment, as middle powers grapple with opportunities for growth in an increasingly G-ZERO world.

Singapore is a hub where regional dynamics and global spillovers intersect, making it the ideal place to discuss issues such as trade and supply chains, the energy transition, and how Asian countries can scale their AI infrastructure. It provides a unique vantage point for connecting US-China competition, Middle East spillovers, Europe’s de-risking, and global elections into a single integrated view for Asia-based leadership. Unlike broader business conferences, our Asia Summit was thoughtful and candid, giving leaders from throughout the region the opportunity to grapple with how local and global geopolitical dynamics are affecting their bottom line.

Moving beyond the headlines of the day, speakers on the main stage included Dilhan Pillay Sandrasegara (CEO, Temasek Holdings), Irving Tan (CEO, WD), and Josephine Teo (Minister for Digital Development and Information). Participants in closed-door roundtables discussed practical means of addressing some of the region’s most pressing challenges including evolving trade and security alliances, gaps in human and financial resources that are hindering AI diffusion, and the need to deepen investments across the continent. 

To deepen our impact in the region, on 15 October, we will host a series of breakout roundtables in Bangkok during the IMF and World Bank Group Annual Meetings, focused on how the current geopolitical state of play is affecting emerging markets. These roundtables will feature off-the-record briefings with global policymakers and thought leaders, similar to those we have hosted in Washington, DC, during the Spring Meetings.

Intern season: What the next generation thinks about AI, algorithms, and working in an office 

This year, we somehow managed to make our intern program even more competitive. We received more than double the number of applications we did last summer, driving down our acceptance rate from 0.4% in 2025 to 0.2% in 2026. Learn more about who made the cut here and here.

So, how did they spend their time? Our Geo-technology intern, Amelia Frank, worked with our AI enablement team to develop an AI tool that categorized fourteen years’ worth of Eurasia Group's analysis, creating an automated call tracker that measures the accuracy of our analysis and identifies common patterns and analytical errors.

In addition to their day-to-day work, the interns delivered group research presentations at the end of their tenure on pressing issues of the day. This year’s topics: US foreign policy after President Donald Trump, global semiconductor supply chains, and the impact of the Iran war on the AI landscape in the Gulf.

Marc Gustafson (managing director of analysis) hosted a dinner for our DC-based interns alongside summer interns from other DC-based organizations. I always say that we learn as much from our interns as they learn from us. 
Marc and Gerard DiPippo (director, Global Macro) hosted a dinner with Eurasia Group and other DC summer interns
Unsurprisingly, AI was a hot topic in our conversations with the next generation. Several points stood out:
  • Their range of capabilities and awareness of AI were very uneven—some had limited exposure to the technology, while others were deeply immersed in it.
  • We had lively debates about AI rights, whether AI has feelings, and AI sentience.
  • Everyone in this cohort expressed a desire for human connections with people from different backgrounds. They described how they felt they had grown up after the era of monoculture; algorithms—a word that came up repeatedly—catered to their micro-interests, fracturing a collective sense of cultural identity.
  • Many feel an impulse to return to past practices and seek more connections away from the virtual world.
  • None of them wanted to work from home. They are eager to be with people.
  • They are optimistic about the future, even though everyone keeps telling them that bad things are trending.
