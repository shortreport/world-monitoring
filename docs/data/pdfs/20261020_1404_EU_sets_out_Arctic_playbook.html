DRIVING THE DAY

EU SETS OUT ARCTIC PLAYBOOK: Brussels wants to prepare for a future in which retreating sea ice opens new Arctic trade and shipping routes, while tightening surveillance and infrastructure around a region it increasingly sees as critical to Europe’s economic security, according to a draft Arctic Strategy seen by our colleague Gerardo Fortuna. The strategy is set to be published today.

New routes, new rules: The EU plans a strategic foresight study on Arctic shipping routes, looking at future corridors, traffic flows, fleet profiles and ice conditions. It also wants to defend freedom of navigation, push higher maritime training standards through the International Maritime Organization and make greater use of state control of ports to enforce the Polar Code, the mandatory IMO regulations for shipping in the Arctic and Antarctic.

Shadow fleet watch: Brussels is particularly worried about the growing number of shadow fleet vessels operating in Arctic waters, including ships switching off their tracking systems or carrying out high-risk ship-to-ship transfers. The strategy calls for stronger monitoring using satellites, sensors and data-sharing, and says the EU will continue sanctioning shadow fleet vessels entering via the Northern Sea Route.

Maritime security hub: One of the bigger ideas is a new Arctic Maritime Security Hub, designed to combine national and EU information from “seabed to space” on shipping, critical infrastructure, shadow fleets, illegal fishing, pollution and search and rescue. An Arctic Cable Hub would come first, focused on monitoring and protecting submarine cables across the North Atlantic and Arctic.

Icebreakers, anyone? The EU also wants more European icebreaker capacity and will use its new maritime industrial alliance to map needs and manufacturing capabilities, while exploring joint procurement and sharing arrangements with countries and partners.

Trade meets security: The plan also links Arctic commerce directly to Europe’s transport network. Brussels wants faster upgrades to the Trans European Transport Network, stronger east-west and multimodal links across Finland, Sweden and Norway, and more dual-use infrastructure connecting Arctic ports and hinterlands to the single market.

Green shipping too: Brussels also wants tougher action on black carbon from ships, including a push at the IMO to turn voluntary measures into binding rules and potentially expand the geographic area covered by Arctic shipping regulation.

AVIATION

COMMISSION’S CONTRAIL POLICY CAUGHT IN THE CROSSFIRE: The Commission’s approach to tackling contrails, the ice-crystal clouds that form behind aircraft flying at high altitudes, has been caught in the crossfire between the industry and environmental NGOs. Airline representatives believe the EU executive is moving too quickly to address the issue despite incomplete data and the lack of a clear methodology for mitigating contrail-related climate warming.

Green response: NGOs Transport & Environment, Blue Lines and Green Transition Denmark are holding a two-day conference in Brussels to call for stronger policies to reduce contrails, including targets to mitigate their warming effects.

A climate problem: Some persistent contrails contribute to warming by trapping heat in the atmosphere. The Commission included new incentives in its recent proposal to revise the Emissions Trading System — up to 3 million ETS allowances through the end of 2033 — for airlines that incorporate contrail forecasting into flight planning and, where possible, adjust their routes.

Not enough: “What we are missing is a regulatory framework,” said Krisztina Hencz, T&E’s aviation policy manager. Besides free ETS allowances, which T&E wants increased to 10 million, air traffic controllers should also help by recommending routes that avoid areas where persistent contrails are likely to form, Hencz said.

Then there’s fuel: “You can reduce or mitigate contrail-induced warming if you change the composition of the fuel,” Hencz said. She added that sustainable aviation fuels could help, but conventional jet fuel could also be treated through additional hydrogenation to reduce its contrail-forming potential.

Cost-effective: According to Antony Froggatt, T&E’s senior aviation director, contrail avoidance through rerouting “costs only around €20 per ton of CO2 equivalent,” compared with around €360 per ton of CO2 equivalent avoided through energy storage for wind and solar power.

Commission, slow down: Ourania Georgoutsakou, managing director of airline lobby A4E, argued that the ETS revision proposal “gives the impression that this is something that airlines can solve. And I think that’s not correct,” pointing out that airspace is managed by air traffic control authorities. She called any further measures to tackle contrails “premature,” noting that European airlines are already subject to monitoring obligations, which will expand from intra-EU flights to all flights next year.

Who pays? Jean-Denis Larrere, vice president of the Air Traffic Controllers European Unions Coordination, told Tommaso that rerouting even the relatively small share of flights responsible for persistent, climate-warming contrails would have “knock-on effects” across Europe’s congested airspace. He added that allowing detours “requires more staff, new working positions, new systems, and careful planning,” potentially generating significantly higher costs “with consequences for ticket prices.”

Tough compromise: “In the EU policymaking, if you make everybody equally unhappy, you’ve done a good job,” DG CLIMA’s Polona Gregorin said at the conference. She described the ETS proposal as “a carrot,” which rewards airlines for improving contrail forecasting without requiring them to avoid contrails.

No doubts: “The science is crystal clear on one essential point: Taken together, these [contrails] have a warming effect, a very significant one,” Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said in a video message at the event.

AIRPORTS REBUFF AIRLINE PUSH TO REFORM CHARGES: Airport lobby ACI Europe pushed back against airline lobby IATA’s claim that some airports operate as monopolies. According to Olivier Jankovec, director general of ACI Europe, said: “Airports compete fiercely across Europe to attract and retain mobile airline customers.”

Uncovered costs: Responding to IATA’s call for commercial revenues to be taken into account when setting airport charges, Jankovec said that “what airlines pay to use airports generally falls short of covering the full cost of operating and developing airport facilities.”

Airports are businesses: “There is no doubt that the dual-till approach is the most economically rational,” he added, referring to the model under which revenues from shops, parking facilities and other commercial activities are excluded from the calculation of aeronautical charges. This model, he said, “compensates airports for their commercial and investment risks while aligning with the fact that airports are businesses in their own right.”

TRANSPORT BUDGET

KALLAS SLAMS PLANNED GLOBAL EUROPE CUTS: The EU’s chief diplomat, Kaja Kallas, is leading a charge against planned cuts to the bloc’s development budget, according to a letter seen by our Brussels Playbook colleagues. The cuts could also affect transport funding.

United front: The letter, co-signed by European commissioners Marta Kos, Dubravka Šuica, Jozef Síkela and Hadja Lahbib, marks a major push by top officials in charge of the EU’s external relations against what they see as unacceptable planned cuts to the €200 billion Global Europe funds in a recent negotiating document prepared by the Irish Council presidency.

Why transport should care: Global Europe is the EU’s main external financing instrument and a key source of money behind Global Gateway projects abroad. That means cuts would not just hit development aid: they could also squeeze funding and guarantees for rail links, ports, shipping corridors and other cross-border infrastructure connecting the EU with neighboring countries and strategic supply chains. It also helps extend transport links toward places such as Ukraine, Moldova and the Western Balkans.

Key context: The proposed cuts come in response to pressure from frugal nations led by Germany to substantially scale back the size of the EU’s long-term €2 trillion budget. Following talks about the Multiannual Financial at a gathering in Brussels last week, Ireland is due to propose an updated budget framework in coming weeks.
