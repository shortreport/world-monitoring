Upper-caste defections over job quotas threaten Modi’s electoral coalition
25 Nov 2026 14:34 EST
Discover more on the Portal
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:
  • Despite receiving significant attention, recent youth protests are likely to have little electoral impact amid infighting among the movement’s leaders and the lack of a coherent political strategy.
  • A more pressing threat for Prime Minister Narendra Modi is upper-caste discontent over government job quotas, which is fraying his electoral coalition and is likely to hurt the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) in state elections next year.
  • To stem the bleeding, Modi will focus on soft economic reforms that he can link to medium-term job creation, but he will put more politically painful policy changes on hold pending the outcome of the state contests.

Cockroach movement is not evolving into an electoral threat. The youth protests that roiled India starting in May, led by the Cockroach Janata Party (CJP), have petered out. CJP leaders have maintained neutrality, opting not to ally with the opposition Indian National Congress party. Amid internal bickering, the movement has failed to spread beyond urban centers in north India. While selectively harassing some CJP leaders, the government has launched youth outreach programs, and the BJP has dialed back the stridency of its social media messaging to appeal to the youth vote.

In October voting for upper house seats in four state assemblies reserved for college students and teachers, the BJP won 16 seats and the Congress party just one, with the remainder going to regional parties. As it claims not to be a political party, the CJP did not contest, but the results show little sign of a youth protest vote that could influence electoral outcomes.

BJP’s bigger concern is erosion of upper-caste support over expected changes to job quotas. There is growing tension within the cross-caste coalition that has kept Modi in power. He used a combination of personality and ideology to electorally merge the BJP’s traditional Hindu upper-caste base with emerging lower middle castes (known officially as the Other Backward Classes or OBCs). However, the more numerous OBCs have now become the BJP’s primary source of votes, posing a dilemma for the party.

Underserved groups receive quotas for government jobs and public college admissions, with the current OBC quota pegged at 27% for both allocations. A census scheduled for next year will probably show that OBCs make up about half the population, forcing the government to increase the quota, largely at the expense of the upper castes. Although the Modi government resisted the idea of including caste in the census, the upper castes have started to turn against the BJP over the likely impact. The initial CJP protesters were largely upper-caste youth, though the movement outgrew that base and never took up the quota issue.

Upper-caste anger first appeared at the ballot box in August, when the BJP lost by-elections in Bihar and Madhya Pradesh despite ruling both states. The pattern held in sweeping local election defeats in Rajasthan, though local issues were also a factor. The opposition parties are not necessarily the beneficiaries, with the upper castes largely voting for independents to register a protest vote while indicating continuing affinity for Modi. This resentment is largely confined to the BJP strongholds of north and west India.

Key 2027 election in Uttar Pradesh will be close contest for BJP. Of the five state elections slated for February-March, Uttar Pradesh—the most populous state—will be the most closely watched. The BJP holds over 63% of the assembly seats, but a five-to-six-point swing could drive it below the halfway mark. Private polls indicate the BJP would just about hold on to the state but remains vulnerable.

With elections beginning in three months, the BJP has no clear answer to job-quota frustrations amid weak private-sector job creation. It is rolling out a mix of welfare payments, promises to build a temple to the Hindu god Krishna, and efforts to reassure upper-caste party workers that the BJP remains committed to them. At the same time, the BJP chief minister of Uttar Pradesh has criticized the controversial deletion of millions of voters from the electoral rolls, implying many were BJP supporters (see: Electoral roll protests further strain Modi’s electoral coalition, 15 October 2026).

Modi will pursue soft economic reforms while putting bigger changes on hold. The government will push ahead with soft economic reforms—deregulation, simplified tax rules, and subsidies for strategic industries—and the completion of trade agreements, seeing both as credible grounds for promising the employment situation will improve. The BJP’s political outlook is difficult, but not dire, and not enough for Modi to give up his tight fiscal targets. Reforms that will hurt pocketbooks, like a draft bill to introduce market-based electricity prices, will be revisited after the February elections.

The government has begun calculating how to strengthen its electoral position ahead of the 2029 general elections. At the top of the list is expanding the number of parliamentary seats in line with the 2011 census, which would improve the BJP’s prospects. The party is also taking comfort in its recent gains in eastern India, which might offset potential losses in the north and west. However, the most important determinant will be the economy’s trajectory over the next two years.
