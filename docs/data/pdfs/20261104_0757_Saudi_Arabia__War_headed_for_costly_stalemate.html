Yemen war likely to settle into costly stalemate through mid-2027
3 Nov 2026 17:54 EST
Discover more on the Portal
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:
  • A costly stalemate will likely persist in the Yemen war through mid-2027 (60%), as coalition airpower limits Houthi advances but fragmented Yemeni government forces struggle to reverse gains, while missile and drone attacks continue to disrupt Saudi aviation and raise Red Sea shipping and insurance costs.
  • A US-Iran escalation would increase the odds that President Donald Trump directly intervenes in the Yemen war, intensifying Houthi attacks on Saudi civilian and oil infrastructure, but this scenario remains unlikely for now as the Yemeni militant group would prefer to avoid further US involvement.
  • A Saudi-led coalition recovery may enable a pause in fighting (25% odds), although a Houthi breakthrough that sharply escalates the war cannot be ruled out (15% odds), during which the group would step up strikes on the kingdom’s infrastructure to coerce concessions, severely compounding aviation and shipping challenges and driving a major oil price spike.

Saudi-Houthi war will likely settle into costly stalemate through mid-2027 (60%). Fighting will continue around Taiz, Marib, Lahj, Al-Jawf, and the western Red Sea coast without a decisive victory for either side. The Houthis maintain the initiative across multiple fronts, but Saudi-led coalition airpower and renewed Yemeni government operations limit their ability to consolidate gains. Poor cohesion among Yemeni government forces will probably prevent them from capitalizing on air superiority to reverse Houthi gains, with limited territorial shifts between the sides.

Houthi missile and drone attacks will continue against Saudi civilian and oil infrastructure, including airports, Aramco-related facilities, the East-West Pipeline, and Yanbu port. The group also retains scope to harass vessels moving through Saudi Red Sea ports—effectively trying to impose a blockade on these ports. However, the Houthis will likely attempt to avoid attacks that trigger higher global oil prices, in a bid to avert US involvement. This suggests Saudi Arabia will continue facing air travel disruptions, while the economy will have to contend with higher Red Sea shipping and insurance costs, among other challenges.

Iran escalation would raise odds of US action in Yemen even as wars unlikely to merge. If there is a US-Iran escalation after the US midterms, the Houthis are unlikely to go all-in with Tehran and escalate against Saudi oil (see: The US will likely strike Iran in mid-November, 27 October 2026). They would aim to keep the two conflicts separate to avoid drawing Washington directly into the Yemen war. Iran may not prioritize strikes on Saudi Arabia in its retaliation to a US escalation, but such action is not out of the question, with Tehran signaling intent to engage in broad retaliation with no geographic constraints.

An Iran escalation would raise the risk that Trump would intervene against the Houthis. He may capitalize on the US military footprint in the region by mid-to-late November, framing intervention as protecting oil markets, regional allies, and freedom of navigation. Other triggers could include a Houthi miscalculation, such as a strike on Saudi oil infrastructure causing a major price spike, or attacks on US-linked assets. If the US were to intervene, the Houthis would likely escalate to demonstrate resolve, expand attacks on Saudi oil and civilian infrastructure, potentially supported by Iran-backed Iraqi Shia militias, and increase threats to Red Sea shipping. Oil and refined product flows would suffer, oil prices would spike, and Saudi aviation and logistics would face significant disruption.

Instead, the US will likely continue to focus on providing Saudi Arabia with arms, intelligence, and logistical support. Other Saudi partners, including Turkey and Pakistan, as well as European countries, will also keep defensive postures to protect the kingdom rather than conduct offensive operations.

Increased anti-Houthi coalition cohesion could pressure the group into a pause (25%). Achieving this would require better coordination among Yemeni government forces and improved Saudi integration of air operations with ground maneuvers—enabling the coalition to retake Dhubab and the Bab al-Mandab corridor and restore leverage over maritime routes. More precise Saudi targeting of Houthi command centers, launch sites, and logistics hubs, plus improved missile and drone defenses, would undermine the group’s strikes. Israel could quietly assist with detection, interception, and targeting.

A weaker or more conciliatory Iran could emerge after the expected US escalation. A more constrained Tehran might discourage the Houthis from pursuing maximalist demands. This would not necessarily produce a durable peace, but it would raise the odds of a pause in fighting or phased negotiations on ceasefire arrangements, humanitarian corridors, or partial de-escalation.

Tribal discontent is a key Houthi vulnerability. The group’s conscription demands are intensifying, straining relations with tribal constituencies and prompting localized dissent. That dissent is fragmented and largely private for now, allowing the Houthis to sustain recruitment and preserve sufficient force levels. If tribes start to refuse to provide fighters, that would alter the military balance and raise the odds of a coalition recovery.

A Houthi breakthrough would sharply escalate the war (15%). This would require the Houthis to overcome financial and manpower constraints, possibly through more coercive recruitment, greater external support, or relocation of forces, and to breach government defenses around Taiz city. The group would then press toward Lahj, Aden, and key southern corridors. The continued inability of Saudi Arabia and the UAE to align on Yemen, and weak coordination among their local allies, would maintain fragmentation among anti-Houthi forces and accelerate their territorial losses.

The Houthis could then be emboldened to step up missile and drone strikes on Saudi airports, oil facilities, and logistics hubs to hasten concessions from Riyadh. War-related fallout would become more sustained, with severe aviation and Red Sea shipping disruption, greater threats to Saudi oil exports, and a major oil price spike.
