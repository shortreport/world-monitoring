DRIVING THE DAY

THE NO-GO BOX: The European Commission has come out strongly against Ireland’s proposed cuts to the EU’s next seven-year budget, days before the bloc’s leaders are set to meet in Brussels for a summit on Thursday and Friday.

During a meeting of EU ambassadors on Sunday, the Commission’s top budget official Stéphanie Riso warned that cutting over €22 billion from rainy-day funds would make Europe less equipped to deal with future crises, four EU diplomats briefed on the talks, granted anonymity to speak freely, told Gregorio Sorgi.

The Commission is “very concerned about cuts to flexibility,” one of the EU diplomats said. As a result of the changes, “we will have no ways left to help farmers and regions when crises hit, because of climate change or geopolitical tensions,” they added.

Pushing back: Ireland’s Europe Minister Thomas Byrne disputed that claim, saying in a press conference on Saturday that “each heading, under this negotiating box, offers increased flexibility throughout the proposal.”

The Irish presidency of the Council of the EU, which is steering the budget talks, cut €159 billion in current prices from the Commission’s proposal to appease a German-led group. But that’s not enough for Berlin and its allies, who sent an email on Sunday to European Council President António Costa’s team to complain about the proposal, according to two EU diplomats.

Frugal view: “We will definitely have to cut cohesion and agriculture” in the next draft of the budget, as it “doesn’t make sense to cut in all the priority topics the EUCO is discussing this week,” an EU diplomat from one of the so-called frugal states told Playbook.

Ireland focused cuts on competitiveness, foreign aid and emergency funds, while leaving untouched traditional policies such as farmers’ subsidies and regional payouts, known as cohesion policy.

That was in response to 17 countries, known as the Friends of Cohesion, asking the Irish presidency to shield agriculture and cohesion from cuts. Most governments preferred reducing flexibility to preserve politically sensitive payouts to regions and farmers.

The big loser: But these changes are a major setback for the Commission, which tried to “modernize” its own budget by steering billions towards new priorities and creating more flexibility to cope with crises. The EU executive warned that Ireland’s proposal will make the next budget more rigid than the current one — and potentially require a midterm review to respond to future crises.

In a further blow for the Commission, Ireland also watered down the link between EU payouts and economic reforms in the next budget — which is unpopular among most governments.

All roads lead to EUCO: These tensions set the scene for a fraught European Council, where leaders will react to the Irish proposal and try to make progress toward securing a deal before the end of the year. “Every day you come close to the French election they will be less willing to pay for cohesion,” said the EU diplomat.

RUSSIA SANCTIONS

BRUSSELS TURNS THE SCREWS ON MOSCOW: EU ministers will today sign off on the bloc’s biggest package of sanctions against Russia since the start of its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, targeting Moscow’s ability to produce missiles in a show of force amid escalating hybrid attacks, four EU diplomats and officials said.

That’s not all: Foreign ministers gathering in Luxembourg will also push ahead with plans to restrict visas for Russian tourists and ex-combatants, while examining ways to limit the movements of Russian diplomats inside the bloc, per the same officials.

What it’s about: The EU is flexing its legal muscles just over a month after Germany formally blamed Russia for a thwarted drone attack in Leipzig. The bloc will target more than 1,600 individuals and “entities” (mainly companies supporting Moscow’s war effort).

Hitting the chain: Those sanctions — more than half of which target Russia’s ballistic missile production chain — will be locked in for three years, the officials said.

Hey, Moscow! The package is a “clear response from the EU” to hybrid attacks, said a senior EU diplomat, granted anonymity to discuss confidential sanctions. “We certainly hope this will further hamper the ability for Russia to produce missiles.”

Trump trouble: Yet any hope that Europe and the U.S. would join forces to sanction Moscow seemed to have been dashed after U.S. President Donald Trump announced on Friday he’d struck a deal with Russia’s Vladimir Putin to purchase Russian diesel — and then called on Ukraine to install a new leader.

European politicians rallied behind Volodymyr Zelenskyy over the weekend, with Polish PM Donald Tusk posting on X that the Ukrainian president can count on “Europe’s full solidarity.” Trump’s surprise diesel deal has also sparked a bipartisan backlash in Washington: One group of senators has urged the president to “immediately reverse course,” saying the decision was illegal and risks giving Putin a “financial lifeline while Russia continues to kill Ukrainian civilians.”

But Trump wasn’t done with the surprises, announcing yesterday that Kyiv and Moscow had agreed to an energy ceasefire, “effective immediately” — though it wasn’t clear if both sides had, in fact, agreed. Zelenskyy said last night that Kyiv wants to see more details. American officials have been ringing around European capitals to persuade Kyiv to stop striking Russian refineries, and Ukraine could be cut off from weapons supplies and intelligence if it doesn’t play ball, the Financial Times reports.

Reasons to be skeptical: Kyiv’s former Foreign Minister Dmytro Kuleba earlier told the Axel Springer Global Reporters Network that Russia is gearing up to attack other European countries. “We [Ukrainians] don’t have to drag you — Putin will drag you into the war,” Kuleba warned. (Axel Springer is POLITICO’s parent company.)

ALSO ON THE AGENDA IN LUXEMBOURG: Alongside talks on Ukraine, foreign ministers are due today to green-light a new EU mission to train and advise Lebanon’s security forces.

Top EU diplomat Kaja Kallas, who’ll preside over the Foreign Affairs Council, will also press countries to stump up more ships to the EU’s Aspides maritime policing mission, an issue that’s only grown more urgent in the wake of deadly Houthi attacks on a Saudi airport, said a second EU official. (One person was killed and 11 injured in a strike on Riyadh airport yesterday, a day after an attack there killed 13, the BBC reports.)

Ministers will also discuss Trump’s decision to impose sanctions against the International Criminal Court in The Hague. EU diplomats have hinted that the Netherlands will seek to use the next six months before the sanctions are enforced to negotiate a solution with Washington.

Bonus: Kallas will also issue a “no tolls, no fees” declaration coordinated by the EU’s diplomatic arm and backed by 60 countries calling to protect freedom of navigation around the world.

There’s a Tripartite Social Summit later this week … but what is that? It’s a meeting between the EU institutions (Irish PM Micheál Martin and Commission chief Ursula von der Leyen will be there) and what are described as European social partners (in this case that’s the likes of BusinessEurope and the European Trade Union Confederation). These meetings take place twice a year and they discuss economic, employment and social policy issues.

CHRISTMAS AT THE AIRPORT?

DO BETTER, ZAVENTEM: Brussels Airport was singled out for criticism by Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner as he said EU countries and airports need to “do their job” so travelers aren’t stuck in airports over the Christmas holidays.

The EU’s Entry/Exit System “has to work,” Brunner told Zoya Sheftalovich. “The member states had enough time to prepare themselves for this system.”

Happy anniversary, now wait: A year ago today, Europe began rolling out the EES, which digitally registers the fingerprints and photos of non-EU visitors. Since it became fully operational in April, travelers have been plagued by long waits at some airports — including at Brussels Airport, where people were stuck for up to five hours last month.

“Some member states are a bit behind,” Brunner acknowledged. “We have some challenges, like Zaventem for instance, in Belgium, but also in other member states. And they have to do their job. They agreed on the new system.” The Commission has offered support from border agency Frontex and funding to countries to help implement the system, Brunner said, insisting he’s “very confident” the issues will be ironed out.

Infrastructure at Brussels Airport “cannot cope” with the number of passengers coming into Zaventem and the Brussels Airport Company must “make significant changes,” said a spokesperson for Belgian Interior Minister Bernard Quintin. The airport can process 500 to 600 non-Schengen travelers an hour, compared with nearly 3,500 who arrive between 6 a.m. and 10 a.m. on busy days, he said.

Brussels Airport didn’t directly respond to the criticism, instead saying in a statement that “all parties involved” were working to fix the delays, including by testing new e-gates and pre-registration kiosks and training more police. “The need for additional infrastructure is also being assessed,” the statement said.
