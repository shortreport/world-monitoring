DRIVING THE DAY

HOW TO BYPASS UNRULY MEPS: The decline of the once-dominant centrist forces in the European Parliament is leading to a new phenomenon: Getting positions agreed without risking all 720 MEPs voting on them, Max Griera reports.

What’s happening: Normally, all MEPs get the chance to amend laws before the Parliament brings its position into negotiations with governments. But the center parties are increasingly sidestepping votes in the plenary over fears that the chamber’s shifting alliances — particularly with the far right on the rise — could blow up legislation that lawmakers who are experts on the issues have negotiated at the committee stage.

Why this matters: It’s a less cozy Parliament these days, with many MEPs unwilling to toe the line. This practice is another symbol of the fragmentation of EU politics. But while the mainstream parties think it’s a handy way of avoiding their compromises being unpicked, some MEPs told Playbook it raises questions about democracy.

It’s “not democratic not to have it in plenary,” said a senior MEP, granted anonymity to criticize the widely accepted procedure. “That’s the trade-off.”

But it suits the center for different reasons. The center-left S&D, for example, thinks that the shortcut shields laws from being unpicked in the plenary by the far right with center-right EPP support. The EPP, meanwhile, worries that the S&D can’t control its own MEPs in the plenary — and that’s why the compromises could unravel.

The simple solution for both of them? Don’t risk it.

Nothing to worry about! In the full Parliament, “all the people that didn’t work on the text … come with their ideas and can disturb an equilibrium that is found in the committee,” Jean-Marc Germain, an S&D MEP, told our Agnes Rønberg. “The best way to proceed in this complex majority of the plenary is to go for committee mandate.”

How is this allowed? The groups are using a parliamentary rule that’s supposed to enable laws to be fast-tracked if there’s broad consensus. As our chart below shows, the share of files handled through this procedure in the first half of 2026 was already higher than in any full year since 2019.

Where it’s being used: Still reeling from plenary rebellions that derailed key laws, group leaders are allowing MEPs to bypass the chamber on long-term budget files. That’s a reversal from the last budget negotiations in 2021, when they pushed for plenary votes on all files — and despite a broad majority of committee chairs urging the same approach in September.

That means hundreds of billions of euros in EU funding could head into negotiations with the Council with the backing of just 20-30 MEPs instead of a plenary majority. Of 20 budget files, 11 are expected to take that route, according to an official.

“Using this mechanism in this case is undemocratic,” Raphaël De Wael, EU policy adviser at ocean protection NGO Bloom, told our Sebastian Starcevic.

There are protections, though. Lawmakers insist the process is democratic because 72 MEPs can force a plenary vote even if the committee wants to go straight to negotiations.

“The question is not whether Parliament has a mandate. We do,” the EPP’s lead budget negotiator Siegfried Mureșan told Playbook, arguing that committee positions were already backed by a plenary resolution adopted in April before the Commission unveiled its proposal. The committee-approved files “reflect that position, including on the figures.”

As the EU’s politics fractures, this is one of the ways the center is trying to retain its grip.

BIG AND BREAKING

DIGGING FOR TROUBLE: A mining giant wants to dig beneath Finland’s pristine wetlands in search of critical minerals, pitting environmentalists against EU China hawks.

VIENNA’S MFF TIFF: Austrian Europe Minister Claudia Bauer rejected any compromises on the EU’s next seven-year budget after her colleague Markus Marterbauer defied the government’s line to show openness towards new bloc-wide taxes in an interview with Playbook. Bauer said Marterbauer expressed “his own personal view” and that “Austria’s official negotiating objectives remain unchanged.”

MIGRATION DEAL: The EU signed a readmission agreement with Nigeria yesterday evening that would establish a framework for returns within 30 days — part of Brussels’ push to speed up deportations of unauthorized migrants through partnerships with third countries. “We are now stepping up our migration diplomacy to return those who have no right to stay,” Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner told Playbook.

Next up: Brunner will have a call today with Canary Islands President Fernando Clavijo about the recent surge in arrivals there, and he’s staying in touch with Spanish Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska.

SPAIN’S HOUSING NIGHTMARE: Could Spain’s housing crisis bring down the government? Five charts tell the story.

HEGSETH OFF THE HOOK: House Republicans last night defeated an attempt to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, sparing the Pentagon chief a trial and handing the White House a major win, my stateside colleagues report.

EUROPE’S SEAT STILL EMPTY (FOR NOW)

DEALMAKING IN THE DESERT: Ukrainian and Russian negotiators return to the table in Abu Dhabi today for the second and final day of this round of talks. Day 1 focused on security guarantees and a possible prisoner swap, my colleague Tim Ross reports from Abu Dhabi.

Next up, territory: Negotiators are expected to tackle the thorny question of territory today, according to officials briefed on the plans. Meanwhile, after months of European anxiety about being sidelined, U.S. envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff are now fully convinced Europe needs a seat at the table alongside Russia and Ukraine.

Hurry up and pick a rep: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy wants Europe to pick its representative — and fast. Speaking to reporters in Denmark on Tuesday, he called for a “dialogue” with Kyiv over who gets the job, rather than European leaders simply presenting Ukraine with a name. “I think that in November, not late, we need to accept who will represent Europe in negotiations,” Zelenskyy said.

Europe’s less good news: Washington is pressing European capitals to engage with one of Moscow’s long-standing demands: linking a Ukraine peace deal to a broader review of Europe’s security architecture, including NATO’s role.

Europe’s cunning plan: Ukraine’s allies hope that showing openness to discussing Europe’s wider security arrangements will coax Russia into making concessions in the Ukraine talks, according to officials. The trick is sequencing: Keep the two tracks moving, but hold the line against any deal on Europe’s defenses until after a peace agreement for Ukraine.

Moscow has other ideas: Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov says the reverse order — sorting out NATO-Russia relations first, Ukraine second — is “absolutely reasonable.”

Whoever gets the job of speaking for Europe in the next round of talks had better buckle up.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

Talks have stalled on the EU’s Automotive Package … but what’s that? Presented by the European Commission last December, the package is meant to support the auto sector as it gets cleaner. Among its aims are reforming the long-contested 2035 de facto combustion engine ban, making more batteries in Europe and slashing red tape. But member countries can’t agree on emissions targets and whether to create a new category for cars that run on alternative fuels.

MARKING ARMISTICE DAY: It was 108 years ago today that allied powers and Germany signed the agreement to end World War I. Here’s how Belgium is remembering the fallen …

In Brussels, there’ll be a ceremony at the Congress column, where members of the government, the parliament and King Philippe will be among those paying tribute to the “unknown soldier” and the victims of both world wars.

To the trenches: In Ypres — home of the Flanders Fields battleground where, according to the famous poem, “the poppies blow” — there’s a packed day planned, including a poppy parade, a concert and (as happens in Ypres every day) the sounding of the Last Post.

While you’re there … pop by the nearby Sint-Sixtus Abbey, which produces the famous but hard-to-get Westvleteren trappist beer that’s often been named the best in the world. You can try some at the aptly named “In de Vrede” (“In the Peace”) brasserie next door.
