DRIVING THE DAY

BERLIN AND BRUSSELS SQUARE OFF: A dispute over the EU’s future relationship with Canada is exposing a widening divide between German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, four diplomats granted anonymity to discuss the sensitive diplomacy told Nick Vinocur and Zoya Sheftalovich.

The latest clash centers on von der Leyen’s proposal to make Canada an “associate member” of the EU. Berlin objected to the label, and the tension ratcheted up after she repeated it at the summit in Montreal last week, just hours after Germany helped remove it from a joint statement approved by all 27 EU capitals.

The Kyiv connection: Merz had previously floated associate status for Ukraine as a step toward joining the bloc, an idea that was rejected by Kyiv. Officials in Berlin now see von der Leyen’s use of the label for Canada — which effectively precludes using it for Ukraine — as a rebuke of the chancellor’s initiative. A representative for Merz didn’t respond to a request for comment.

Different definitions: The disagreement is about more than terminology. Under von der Leyen’s definition, associate status would be for a country that could never join the EU — as she acknowledged in Montreal — rather than an intermediate step for Ukraine, two EU diplomats said.

Bigger than Canada: The clash sits among wider differences between the two over how the bloc should be governed. Berlin says it won’t approve the Commission’s proposed €2 trillion long-term budget unless “hundreds of billions” are cut. Germany has also backed a separate format with France and the U.K. for sensitive Ukraine-related matters, while von der Leyen’s proposed European Security Council has failed to gain traction in Berlin.

“There is this institutional dilemma between Brussels and Berlin,” said Jan Techau, head of Germany for the Eurasia Group and a former speechwriter for the German defense ministry. “Von der Leyen has taken a strongly integrationist approach to the EU while Merz has completely developed away from this and talks about the nation states as the primary driver of EU policy.”

There’s no sign of retreat on the “associate member” label: Berlin reiterated its objection at a meeting of EU ambassadors yesterday, two diplomats said, while a person familiar with von der Leyen’s thinking said she has no intention of dropping the term.

Despite the rift, both sides share the same basic goals for EU-Canada ties, Commission chief spokesperson Paula Pinho said. A German diplomat agreed, saying: “We both committed to form a unique partnership that we don’t have with any other country outside the European Union.”

Shared ground, different instincts: Both leaders belong to Germany’s conservative Christian Democratic Union, and Techau said they share “ideological overlap on many issues,” including warmer ties with Ottawa. But he said Merz has “proven disappointing to people in Brussels who hoped he would take a more integrationist approach to the EU, and this has been driven entirely by domestic prerogatives.”

The wider question: Techau said the label dispute reflects a broader argument over whether common EU institutions or national governments should drive policy. “The issue is, in many ways, ‘unresolvable,’” he said.

AI-PROOFING THE EU’S WORKERS

SKILLS GAPS COULD BE “LETHAL”: Europe must invest more in education and vocational training to stay competitive in the face of pressure from countries such as China and the U.S., Commission Executive Vice President Roxana Mînzatu told Zoya Sheftalovich in an interview.

“It would be lethal for any society, for any economy, and for Europe as well, not to urgently address the needs in education,” said Mînzatu, who is unveiling the EU’s education and skills package today.

Plumb jobs: The package includes a strategy to make vocational education and training (VET) a more attractive pathway. Mînzatu said more than 70% of jobs in some sectors require vocational training, while people who choose that path have employability “close to 90%.” Yet, she added, “parents, the society, kids do not see vocational training as the first choice.”

In the AI age, a degree may not be the best ticket to a secure career: roles requiring physical presence, manual expertise and applied technical skills may prove more resilient, per the Commission’s VET strategy.

Since generative AI tools took off in late 2022, vacancies have increased in fields including engineering technology, machinery mechanics, construction and transport. But those jobs are not immune to change, so education and training needs to keep pace with developments, the Commission will say.

Teach the children well: The package urges EU countries to make teaching more attractive, as student protests over teacher shortages flare up in France, Belgium and elsewhere. Most EU countries have teacher shortages, Mînzatu said, listing reasons including “unattractive working conditions, the wages, the salaries, the burden, the pressure of being a teacher, the pressure in the relationship that you have with a classroom, with the parents, with society.”

The Commission cannot set teacher numbers or salaries, Mînzatu said, but wants governments to act on working conditions.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The European Commission has presented the first EU-wide sustainable tourism strategy — but what is that? It aims to improve transport links to islands, coastal, rural and remote areas; make tickets for rail journeys easier to buy; improve high-speed and night train connections; and promote rail as part of the holiday experience. The Commission also plans to revise rules for air services, review airport legislation and make cross-border car rentals easier and more affordable.

GOLD WATCHES FROM THE COMMISSION

WHO’S PACKING THEIR BOXES AT THE BERLAYMONT? Forget the Commission work program. The hottest document circulating in Brussels’ Signal and WhatsApp groups last week was a list of top EU officials supposedly heading for retirement, which set off a frenzy of speculation about the next round of musical chairs in the Berlaymont.

Playbook has seen the list. Gert Jan Koopman (DG ENEST) and Pascal Leardini (deputy SG of the Secretariat-General) could leave by year’s end; Kerstin Jorna (DG GROW) and Florika Fink-Hooijer (HERA) in March; and Bernard Magenhann (DG JRC) and Themis Christophidou (DG REGIO) by mid-2027. Those nameddid not respond to requests for comment …

… except for Fink-Hooijer, who told POLITICO she’s aware of the circulating list but stressed that no announcements have been made. “My age is mentioned on Wikipedia, contrary to that of many men — so nothing new here either,” she added in one of this Playbook author’s favorite replies ever.

Some smoke, some fire: Playbook set out to fact-check the list. Two EU officials, granted anonymity to discuss internal shake-ups, said Koopman has told colleagues he’ll retire on Jan. 1. Two others called the timing of Christophidou’s departure “accurate,” despite pressure from her team to stay. One official said someone in Ursula von der Leyen’s inner circle already has an eye on Leardini’s job.

Meanwhile, three officials wondered why Sandra Gallina (DG SANTE) and Roberto Viola (DG CONNECT), both of whom have extended their tenures, were missing from the list.

Why the excitement? Because there’s no room on the Commission’s senior management career ladder. Former top official Catherine Day has proposed restoring fixed five-year appointments to get things moving, in her advice to von der Leyen on how to overhaul how the Commission works. With only one major reshuffle since von der Leyen took office, a retirement wave could finally get the Berlaymont’s musical chairs spinning again.
