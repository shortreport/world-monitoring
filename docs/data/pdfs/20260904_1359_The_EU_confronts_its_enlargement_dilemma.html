DRIVING THE DAY

BIGGER, FASTER: EU countries will today tell the Commission to speed up work that would make it easier for new members to join the bloc, warning that the bruising referendum defeat in Iceland should be a wake-up call for enlargement policy.

European affairs ministers and representatives from candidate and potential candidate countries — including Ukraine, Montenegro, Bosnia, North Macedonia, Moldova and Kosovo — will meet today in Dublin for the first major discussion in months on how to break the deadlock and get them into the EU.

Despite broad support for enlargement, EU capitals led by Berlin, Paris and The Hague are worried that admitting them too quickly could cause problems without rules in place to prevent a new member from turning into a troublemaker. It’s the Commission’s job to draft those rules — and until it finds a solution, the entire process is mired in uncertainty. Ideas such as reverse enlargement have been shot down by capitals.

High stakes: Iceland’s decision not to restart membership talks “doesn’t help the idea that the EU is a club that lots of people want to be part of,” Austria’s Europe Minister Claudia Bauer told Playbook. Not admitting those who do want to join, she said, would have “an impact on our credibility” and create a vacuum for “foreign influences, the influences of Russia, of the United States, of China.”

The solution, Bauer said, is to design “safeguards that protect the Union without turning accession into a permanent obstacle course.”

Tell us what you think: Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos, who will take part in today’s meeting, has declared this will be an “autumn of enlargement.” She’s working up proposals to strip new members of their rights if they break the bloc’s rules or disrupt key policies, but is keeping quiet on what these plans might look like. Ireland’s Europe Minister Thomas Byrne, who will chair the talks in Dublin, said the plan is expected “towards the end of the month,” and today will be a chance to agree what needs to be in it.

Candidate countries are getting restless. “Our government is working full speed,” said Moldova’s European Integration Minister Cristina Gherasimov, who will attend the talks. “What we need is for the member states to meet us halfway … the way the EU can help us now is to move [forward] with the political decisions necessary.”

Hearts and minds: Bauer also wants the EU to “improve communications on enlargement because there are still skeptical countries and especially skeptical citizens.”

Supporters of Germany’s far-right Alternative for Germany are particularly skeptical. In a poll conducted by Public First for POLITICO in June, only one-third of German respondents said Ukraine should never become a member of the EU. But among those who said they intended to cast their vote for the AfD in the next national election, that share shot up to 65 percent. It’s a strong signal for the EU ahead of this weekend’s pivotal election in the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt, which the AfD looks set to win.

BUDGET BATTLE HEATS UP

SERAFIN — BUDGET CUTS COULD BACKFIRE: EU Budget Commissioner Piotr Serafin fears that reductions to the bloc’s next long-term budget would hit the very areas Brussels most wants to strengthen: competitiveness, defense and security. Those top priorities could “become the first victims of cuts,” Serafin told reporters at the informal General Affairs Council yesterday.

Sounding the alarm: Serafin said the Commission’s role in the negotiations now is to point out the consequences of drastic cuts to its nearly €2 trillion proposal for the 2028-2034 budget. Asked what those consequences would be, he told Playbook’s Zoya Sheftalovich: “Less security, less competitiveness.”

Nobody’s baby: If the budget shrinks significantly, Serafin said national governments would likely defend their pet budget topics, such as agriculture and cohesion funding. “I’m not sure who’s going to put competitiveness and security as his or her red line,” he said.

Proving the point: Spain’s Europe Minister Fernando Sampedro said his country opposes any reductions to the Commission’s budget proposal, and wants to protect the ambition for cohesion, agriculture and fisheries. Security and defense are priorities, Sampedro said when pressed by Zoya — but not “at the expense of all other key priorities,” particularly climate change.

Serafin said he is still optimistic about the negotiations, arguing that growing tensions over the size of the Commission’s proposal showed that capitals were finally taking it seriously. “The worst thing for those MFF negotiations is when people take it lightly and don’t engage,” he said. Now, “we are in business.”

Frugals engage: Germany’s Europe Minister Gunther Krichbaum said Berlin’s own budget constraints, weak growth and pressure on its export-heavy industry, including from China and Donald Trump’s tariffs, made the Commission’s proposal a no-go. “We are suffering,” Krichbaum said. “We have to look what is realistic or not of this. The proposal of the European Commission is not realistic.”

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

With Europe paying close attention to the election in Saxony-Anhalt on Sunday, it’s worth asking: What powers do these states have? Quite a lot. The German constitution says the Länder (the states) have the right to legislate unless otherwise stated in the constitution itself. That means the list of regional powers is long and includes education, health care and policing. That said, the constitution makes it clear that “Federal law shall take precedence over Land law.”

PARLIAMENT UNITED?

NO ONE ON THE RIGHT WING: The European Parliament is getting a football team — but far-right lawmakers won’t be allowed to join, according to its founder.

German Renew MEP Lukas Sieper — known for his influencer-style videos on social media — is setting up the team to take on national parliaments from across Europe. There will be a couple of trials to pick the players, but no training sessions, since MEPs’ schedules are simply too full, Sieper told Playbook. Check out its planned logo here.

The first match will be against FC Bundestag, with the “big goal” (pun intended) being to play in the International Parliamentary Football Tournament, which features teams from the German, Finnish, Austrian and Swiss parliaments.

Cordon sanitaire: The team won’t be officially affiliated with the Parliament or receive any funding. MEPs will pay their own way. It will be mixed-gender and players will have to “support the principles of democracy, the rule of law and European integration” to be admitted, according to its draft statutes.

Sieper said it’s “unimaginable” that a member of the far-right Patriots for Europe or Europe of Sovereign Nations groups could meet the criteria, although such decisions will be made on a case-by-case basis by the club board.

HARASSMENT COMPLAINTS: Two MEPs are pressing the European Commission over reports of verbal and physical harassment inside the institution, after a POLITICO investigation revealed almost 1,000 requests had been made to its confidential counsellors since 2024.

ECR MEPs Sander Smit and Jessika van Leeuwen want to know what “concrete measures” the Commission is taking against abuses of power, whether it will publish data on complaints and whether the allegations could damage public trust in the EU.

BRUSSELS GOES BROCANTE: Rummaging through other people’s stuff at brocante stalls is practically a Belgian way of life. Everyone has that one miraculous find they’re still disproportionately proud of. And September brings the tail end of the season before the stalls disappear for winter. This weekend is particularly busy — coinciding, appropriately enough, with the mammoth Braderie de Lille just across the border.

The big one: Ixelles leads the Brussels pack this weekend, with more than 500 sellers taking over the university/cemetery neighborhood until Sunday. There’ll also be games, jazz and pop concerts, and other entertainment if rummaging through all those stands somehow isn’t enough.

Also supersized: Berchem-Sainte-Agathe’s Grand Kermesse promises around 450 sellers, while Watermael-Boitsfort hosts a neighborhood brocante around Avenue des Coccinelles with some 300 stands on Saturday. Schaerbeek’s Plasky area and Neder-over-Heembeek each offer another 250 or so.

Brocante with a purpose: Jette’s Woeste neighborhood is hosting a smaller, solidarity-minded edition with around 150 sellers, designed to strengthen neighborhood ties and combat social isolation.

Sunday shopping: Forest offers two options — Messidor, with around 90 stands, and Rodenbach, with 65 — while Rue Konkel in Woluwe-Saint-Pierre expects around 180. Thieffry in Etterbeek has roughly 200, the Quays district in central Brussels around 150, and Woluwe-Saint-Lambert a more intimate 30.

Missed your bargain? More are coming. Saint-Gilles has a brocante on Sept. 13, while Ganshoren is promising a monster edition beside its château with more than 500 sellers. Woluwe-Saint-Lambert also has a 500-plus-stand edition later in September, alongside Uccle’s Saint-Job brocante.

Happy hunting — and remember: That €3 lamp is only a bargain if you actually have somewhere to put it.
