Peltola win puts Democratic Senate supermajority within reach in 2029
18 Nov 2026 12:07 EST

  • APEC summit will likely yield limited progress on deliverables

Note: Next week the US Daily will be on hiatus for the US Thanksgiving holiday. Coverage will resume November 30.

Peltola win puts Democratic Senate supermajority within reach in 2029. Democrat Mary Peltola was called as the winner of Alaska's competitive Senate election last night, unseating incumbent Republican Dan S. Sullivan. This was the last Senate race to be called and was the ninth Democratic flip in this election, finalizing the party's 56-seat majority in the upper chamber.

  • With Alaska called, Democrats will need four pickups on net in 2028 to reach a filibuster-proof majority, which no party has achieved since 2009. The 2028 map is favorable to them, with obvious pickup opportunities in Wisconsin and North Carolina, but will require tough defenses in Nevada, Arizona, Ohio--where Senator-elect Sherrod Brown will be up for a full term—and Pennsylvania. Even if Republicans were to gain, they have essentially no path to reclaiming the Senate in 2028.
  • The major question will be whether the national tide is as blue as it was in 2026 (a D+14 national environment). If so, 60 seats is a real possibility for Democrats: Holding their own states and winning those they did in 2026 (South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa, and Alaska) would put them on 62. Utah and Florida could be wild cards.
  • More realistically, though, the national environment probably won’t be as strong for Democrats as in 2026, even if it leans blue thanks to President Trump’s low approval rating. A D+9 national environment would make pickups in those four states plausible but not likely. Democrats would be just short of a filibuster-proof majority, though it’s possible they could peel off a few Republicans on specific issues.

APEC summit will likely yield limited progress on deliverables. President Donald Trump's meetings over the next two days with Presidents Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will likely not lead to major breakthroughs on trilateral or bilateral issues.

  • While Trump has pushed to discuss arms control in the trilateral forum, China is extremely reluctant to negotiate until it can reduce its quantitative disadvantage compared to the US. A trilateral arms agreement is not on the table.
  • We expect Trump and Xi to discuss extending the Busan truce past its January 10 expiration, but this still faces significant hurdles. The US has suggested dissatisfaction with China’s delivery on agricultural purchases and critical minerals exports; it will want progress on both fronts before extending the truce. Talks will likely continue until the leaders’ next meeting at the G20 summit.
  • Trump may raise Ukraine in bilateral talks with Putin, but with both Russia and Ukraine prepared to escalate over the winter, we don’t expect any progress from the summit. Our most likely scenario is that the war will continue through 2027 (65% odds, see Ceasefire odds increase in second quarter of 2027, 5 November 2026).

Any FAA supplemental funding would fall short of request. Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy met with Senate Republicans in a closed-door lunch yesterday to advocate for $50 billion in additional funding for the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). Duffy's remarks, while not discounted outright, were met with skepticism.

  • Even after the nation-wide ground stop last week, Secretary Duffy’s request met resistance from GOP members for a few operative reasons: the Department’s requested number has grown from $10 billion to $30 billion to $50.1 billion in a short time period with little justification; much of the $9 billion that was set aside for FAA systems modernization in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) is still left unspent; and the scope of the modernization work under the request has broadened considerably to include unrelated infrastructure like terminal construction.
  • Politically, the FAA request also fell flat due to inconsistent messaging from the executive branch. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford undercut Duffy at a press conference over the weekend, decoupling the maintenance work which caused the outage and the modernization work that Duffy’s $50 billion request would fund.
  • Speaker of the House Mike Johnson (R-LA) and Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) have come out in support of attaching FAA funding to reconciliation or a year-end legislative package. However, they have also expressed a desire to gather more details from Duffy and have not committed to attaching funding or to attach funding at a certain level.
  • Other potential avenues to plus-up FAA also remain at play, including a Democratic-led proposal to redirect some of the roughly $76.5 billion in unspent funding for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) for FAA modernization efforts.
