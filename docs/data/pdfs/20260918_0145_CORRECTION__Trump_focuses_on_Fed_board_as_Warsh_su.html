Trump focuses on Fed board as Warsh survives first test. The Fed raised rates as expected yesterday in a unanimous decision, and Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference read as modestly hawkish. Markets now price three additional hikes over the next 12 months, versus two more expected before yesterday's decision.

  • From Warsh’s perspective, this was a good result: A muted response from Trump, a constructive market reaction, and no dissents on the vote.
  • But the Fed is not out of the woods yet, and even as Warsh himself appears insulated from threats from the White House, risks are shifting toward the institution more broadly. Trump’s Truth Social post and public statement after the meeting were directed at the other members of the committee, not at Warsh himself, suggesting that pressure could grow if the Fed, as the market expects, raises rates later this year and again in 2027.
  • The White House has the mechanisms to threaten the jobs of committee members Lisa Cook, Michael Barr, and Mary Daly; if it does so, that could force Warsh to choose between incensing the president and defending the independence of the institution.
  • That the long end of the yield curve fell only marginally—and has not unwound the sharp rise of recent weeks—in response to a credible message from Warsh indicates that Fed credibility is not the main force behind higher yields. The major drivers are negative supply shocks, compounded by concerns about the fiscal outlook. Fed communication, then, will likely have limited scope to bring down long-term yields; administration moves on tariffs or Iran would matter more.

Seoul will go big on US investments to placate Washington. South Korea will likely announce $200 billion in investments in the US this week, centering on gas turbines in Texas, eight large-scale nuclear reactors, and an LNG pipeline project in Alaska.

  • Political imperatives have driven this progress. US pressure, namely the recent reduction in joint military exercises and President Donald Trump’s request for aid in the Middle East, has forced Korea to move on these investments to avoid further antagonizing Trump, despite lingering skepticism about the viability of these projects.
  • Korea sees opportunity to build US nuclear reactors, seizing on Japan’s reluctance to sign onto large-scale projects. Any announcement will still require significant follow-up before projects break ground, but Korean engineering, procurement, and construction companies could provide lucrative services the US lacks.
  • Korean officials hope that the big investment commitments will please Trump and alleviate growing strains in the bilateral relationship, particularly over the US e-commerce company Coupang and the Strait of Hormuz, but a meeting between Trump and South Korean President Lee Jae-myung is unlikely at the UN General Assembly this month. Looking beyond the current administration, Seoul quietly hopes that its broader investment commitments could end under the next US president or before it reaches the $200 billion cap in its framework deal.

GM’s entry into missile production signals demand shift. Lockheed Martin announced Thursday that General Motors will supply parts for critical PAC-3 MSE interceptor missiles. PAC-3 MSE are an advanced variant of traditional missiles used in Patriot air defense batteries that have been seriously depleted in Iran and Ukraine.

  • The initial joint announcement is spare on details, including the specific order size or plans for future work, but is a welcome signal to the defense industry as it has struggled to overcome supply chain challenges and lagging production capacity to address continual interceptor stock depletion exacerbated by the Iran war. But the announcement is not necessarily indicative of a larger groundswell that will dramatically accelerate production and shorten delivery times sector-wide.
  • GM’s production capabilities will aid Lockheed in executing its framework deal to ramp up missile production brokered with the Trump administration this January. GM has reportedly slashed delivery times for certain components from months to weeks, offering immediate capability that can potentially reduce lead times for full-up PAC-3 interceptors.
  • However, in addition to GM, other automakers have expressed interest in expanding their defense business lines, reflecting auto sale plateaus and rising demand from the Pentagon. The Pentagon’s demand for industrial capacity could provide a lifeline for automakers facing diminishing returns, making further entrants into the contract manufacturing space an important signpost for defense production in the future.
