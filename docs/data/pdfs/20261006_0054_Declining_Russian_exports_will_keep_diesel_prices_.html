Declining Russian exports will keep diesel prices elevated
5 Oct 2026 11:52 EDT
Discover more on the Portal
  • Despite recent interventions, diesel prices globally are likely to remain high, partly because of the collapse in Russian exports.
  • Iran will continue to aggressively target shipping in the Strait of Hormuz to undermine recent US successes in raising regional output.
  • A Saudi-backed offensive in Yemen will trigger more aggressive Houthi attacks on infrastructure, including the East-West pipeline.

Russia’s output decline adds strain to a crisis-ridden oil market. While attention remains fixed on the Middle East conflicts, the ongoing degradation of Russia’s energy infrastructure by Ukrainian missile and drone attacks has caused a significant decline in the country’s crude production and halted virtually all exports of refined products.

As in the US-Iran and Saudi-Houthi conflicts, de-escalation in the Russia-Ukraine war is unlikely this year. As a result, Russian output is likely to remain depressed.

The collapse in Russian diesel exports is an additional stress factor. While Russia’s refined product exports have broadly fallen owing to domestic restrictions and Ukrainian attacks, the decline of middle distillates such as diesel has had an especially acute effect on the global market. Russian flows have fallen from 800,000 barrels per day (bpd) in 2025 to zero in September 2026 as Moscow cuts exports in order to meet domestic needs.

Aggressive Iranian attacks will challenge US success in Hormuz. By mid-September, US-led efforts in the Strait of Hormuz had helped Middle East output to exceed 75% of the prewar volume (see: Rising Middle East flows won’t bring prices down quickly, 28 September 2026). Now, Iran has struck back, with ten confirmed attacks on ships in the last two weeks. At least two vessels have been disabled by the attacks.

Iran has an incentive to escalate its attacks to counter US efforts in the strait (see: High oil prices likely through 2026 as US-Iran standoff endures, 18 September 2025), though for the time being it is likely to focus on expanding disruptions in the strait rather than attempting more aggressive attacks on infrastructure.

Escalation in the Saudi-Houthi conflict presents new risks for Saudi infrastructure. As Eurasia Group expected (see: Likely Saudi-Houthi escalation raises risk of oil disruptions, 27 September 2026), Saudi allies in Yemen launched an offensive to retake territory from the Houthis near the Bab al-Mandeb. The escalation is likely to trigger more Houthi attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure, putting the East-West pipeline at risk of further disruptions.
