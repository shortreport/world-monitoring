What the US midterm elections mean for Japan
  • The US midterm elections’ most immediate implications for Japan are a potential slowdown in US data center construction, greater congressional scrutiny of Japanese investments, and stronger pressure from Washington for Tokyo to increase defense spending and Host Nation Support for US troops.
  • As President Donald Trump focuses more on foreign policy in his final two years in office, he could prioritize diplomacy with China and Russia at Japan’s expense.
  • Despite growing anxiety about where US policy toward Japan may be headed, Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae will likely remain committed to fulfilling Japan’s investment commitments in the US.

The US midterm elections dealt a blow to Japan. Tokyo had been watching the race closely for months, with a steady stream of Japanese business delegations passing through Washington to assess the potential outcomes and gauge their political fallout for Japan. Even so, the election results proved more damaging for Trump and his party than many had anticipated—including most observers in Japan. The resounding defeat for Trump, with whom Takaichi has worked assiduously to cultivate a close personal relationship, raises many questions for Japan’s relationship with the US going forward.

While Trump’s presidency has created no shortage of headaches for Tokyo, he is a known quantity. By contrast, the attitudes and preferences of the incoming Democratic leadership are far less understood. Japanese officials are acutely aware that the executive branch, along with the levers of US foreign and trade policy, remains in Trump's hands. Nevertheless, the US-Japan alliance could come under greater strain during Trump’s remaining two years in office owing to a range of factors.

AI data center slowdown

The most immediate impact on Japan is a potential slowdown in US data center investment. Japanese companies are deeply embedded in the supply chains underpinning the data center construction boom, and the prospect of a slowdown—driven either by environmental opposition or increased scrutiny of Trump's economic agreements—could harm Japanese corporate interests. A data center slowdown could also negatively affect some of the flagship investments announced under Tokyo’s $550 billion commitment, including large-scale gas turbines and other power generation projects.

Moreover, a deceleration in AI investment in the US could allow China to close the technological gap with the US and its allies, potentially giving Beijing a chokehold over a key technology at a time when Beijing is already heavily restricting Tokyo’s access to rare earth elements.

That said, Japan is not facing an immediate risk. Data center projects already under construction—including the Ohio gas turbine project that is being financed with Japanese capital—are likely to continue in the near term. Meanwhile, over the medium term, the data center boom is more likely to slow than to reverse, and investment opportunities for Japanese companies will continue to exist even if the location of data centers shifts (see: States will condition, not ban, data centers after midterm backlash, 10 November 2026).

Corruption probes

The possibility of heightened congressional scrutiny of deals announced under Japan’s investment framework is another risk. Democrats have pledged to investigate alleged corruption in the Trump administration, which could extend to the investment projects that Tokyo has carefully negotiated with the Trump administration. The Japanese government is discreetly taking steps to advise and protect Japanese companies that Tokyo encouraged over the past year to commit to US-based projects.

Tokyo is rightfully anxious about what congressional investigations might uncover. Finding corruption in Japan’s investment projects could sour public attitudes in Japan toward the broader trade and investment framework with the US, complicating agreements on future investments. In a sign of the scrutiny to come, Japanese company SoftBank is listed among 27 companies that have received instructions from the incoming chair of the House Natural Resources Committee to preserve their internal records.

If congressional hearings reveal that Japanese firms participated in corrupt arrangements—even passively or under duress—the domestic backlash in Japan could be severe. For the Takaichi government, whose foreign policy credibility rests on the perception that it drove a hard but honest bargain with Washington, any taint of corruption would be politically damaging.

Trump’s foreign policy pivot

Trump is widely expected to direct a greater amount of his energy toward foreign policy over the final two years of his presidency given the domestic constraints he will face under a Democratic Congress. Far from being a lame duck internationally, Trump and his administration will likely increase pressure on Tokyo to raise defense spending—including financial support for US troops in Japan under the next Special Measures Agreement (SMA)—and deliver on Japan's investment pledges.

The SMA represents the nearest-term friction point. The US and Japanese positions remain far apart, with only four months remaining until the existing agreement expires in March 2027 (it is renegotiated every five years). Washington is pushing for a tripling of Japan's host-nation support payments, an exorbitant increase that, while small in terms of overall defense spending, would likely put Takaichi in a challenging political position at home. To date, Japanese negotiators have not yielded to US demands, and there is little prospect of Washington softening its position as the two sides look to reach an agreement before the end of December.

A refusal to meet Washington’s defense spending demands could invite further US pressure. Washington is quietly considering options to exert leverage over Japan, including reducing joint military exercises or other forms of security cooperation. These moves would be a source of embarrassment for Takaichi, who has so clearly oriented her foreign policy around the indispensability of the US-Japan alliance and her deft management of Trump.

US-China diplomacy

The recent trilateral summit among China, Russia, and the US on the sidelines of the APEC meeting was another negative development for Japan. Tokyo is wary of the emergence of a G-3 group of countries that negotiate over global security while Japan is excluded from the room. This anxiety is compounded by the recent spike in tensions between Russia and Japan (see: Tokyo’s growing Russia problem, 27 October 2026), Trump’s ongoing outreach to Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Takaichi’s inability to secure a bilateral meeting with Trump at the ASEAN summit in Manila ahead of APEC.

Despite receiving reassurances from Washington about its commitment to Japan, including a phone call from Trump to Takaichi while he was on board Air Force One returning from APEC, Tokyo will continue to worry that Trump and Xi could strike an agreement behind Japan’s back. Another trilateral meeting of the leaders of the US, China, and Russia at the G20 summit next month in Miami would add to this concern.

A more hawkish US congressional stance toward China could present other challenges for Japan. Legislation to restrict Beijing’s access to leading-edge semiconductors would prohibit Japan from supplying semiconductor manufacturing equipment to China. This would not only harm Japan’s economy but could also further strain Tokyo’s ties with Beijing (see: Beijing will maintain calibrated pressure campaign against Tokyo, 20 July 2026).

Steady as she goes

Despite the heightened uncertainty surrounding the US-Japan relationship, Tokyo is unlikely to fundamentally alter its approach to Washington. Takaichi has staked her foreign policy on cultivating strong ties with the US and Trump, who will retain authority over the policy levers that matter most to Tokyo: tariff policy, investment in the US, and the security alliance. Japan is also aware that any visible softening in its commitment to the US could provoke the kind of rupture in its relationship with Trump that it is seeking to avoid.

Japan will therefore not back away from its investment commitments, which remain the most visible achievement in US-Japan relations under Trump. Tokyo is eager to maintain the goodwill it has earned in Washington as a stalwart ally that upholds its commitments. As a result, Japan will likely press ahead with the projects it has already announced, despite ongoing uncertainty about the commercial viability of many of these initiatives and a diminished threat of Trump imposing higher tariffs. However, the pace of new project delivery will likely slow as the two sides run out of suitable investment opportunities and the end of Trump’s presidency draws nearer.

The wild card for Tokyo is the fate of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. As the primary interlocutor on Japan’s investment framework, he is a known quantity and personally attached to many of the announced deals. Lutnick’s potential departure from Trump’s cabinet would force Japan to rebuild a relationship with his successor, with no guarantee that whoever follows Lutnick would look as favorably upon the broader investment framework.

Tokyo will therefore continue to play the hand it has been dealt. The midterm elections raised the risks of Takaichi's Trump strategy but did not offer a compelling alternative. Japan will instead watch closely, looking for any shifts in Trump’s policy toward Tokyo while seeking to guide his engagement with China, Russia, and North Korea. Nonetheless, Takaichi will be very reluctant to deviate sharply from her established Trump playbook (see: Lessons from Takaichi’s Trump playbook, 29 September 2026).
