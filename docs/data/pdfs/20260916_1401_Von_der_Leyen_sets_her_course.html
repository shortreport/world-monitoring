DRIVING THE DAY

SOTEU IS FINALLY HERE … BUT THEN WHAT? We pretty much know what Ursula von der Leyen is going to say in her biggest address of the year. The harder question is what comes next.

Von der Leyen’s sixth State of the Union will be critical to defining the remainder of her Commission presidency. If she has something big left to do, she’d better get on and do it.

Or, as Parliament President Roberta Metsola put it here in Strasbourg yesterday: “The legislative files that will be proposed or that will come this year will realistically be the ones that will be closed by the end of the mandate.”

With a difficult electoral year ahead, today will tell us a lot about what von der Leyen wants to do with the rest of her time in the EU executive’s top job. Watch particularly for where she gets personal: Social media restrictions for children, wearing her hat as a former family minister; big security plans, drawing on her time as a defense minister. But the broader ambition she signals will be just as revealing as any individual policy.

Can von der Leyen get Europe behind her? She’s got several factors in her favor. Nearly two years into her second five-year term, the Commission president has more control than in the past, with officials describing a firmer grip on her team.

And the Parliament is different, too. Playbook has been doing some highly scientific research around Strasbourg — hanging around the notorious Swan bar, the corridors and outside the hemicycle. The verdict: It’s noticeably calmer than during the run of confidence votes against her.

Virginijus Sinkevičius, a commissioner from von der Leyen’s first term, now a Green MEP, thinks the president is in a strong position. “She’s more powerful than [in] my time,” he told Playbook. “You also improve and learn how to do your job by doing it.”

An official from the center-right European People’s Party (von der Leyen’s camp) agreed with that when Playbook bumped into them while queuing at the Parliament bar. They pointed to the way von der Leyen has structured her second Commission — and the concentration of power around her own cabinet.

That doesn’t mean she’s getting everything right. Arrangements for Canadian PM Mark Carney’s appearance in Parliament on Thursday were something of a mess, with von der Leyen apparently not planning to attend — until she was. Canada’s ambassador had made Ottawa’s expectations clear: Carney would attend her speech, so “it would be great if she could be there,” as Sebastian Starcevic and Max Griera report.

Adding to the awkwardness: The EU is set to rebuff Carney’s push for a “unique alliance” with the bloc, the FT is reporting this morning.

Another thought: There could be more at stake for von der Leyen today than charting a course for the remainder of her presidency. “I think they’ll be listening in Berlin,” said one right-wing Parliament official. Speculation that she wants to become German president has faded — but never quite disappeared.

AI APOCALYPSE

TECH TIGHTROPE: The U.S. and China are turning artificial intelligence into a geopolitical contest. Leading industry figures are issuing grave warnings about the dangers of advanced systems. Von der Leyen’s task: Reassure Europeans who are spooked by the tech while emphasizing the opportunities it could bring.

Her message: Europe must avoid becoming so risk-averse that it misses AI’s potential to transform its economy and industry, particularly as the U.S. and China move ahead.

Apocalypse soon? The backdrop is troubling. Over the weekend, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for a slowdown in the development of frontier AI, warning that safety measures may not be keeping pace with the technology. That was backed by OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, xAI founder Elon Musk and Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis. (Though Nvidia boss Jensen Huang reckons the industry doesn’t need new laws, my stateside colleagues report.)

Europe’s dilemma: The EU’s seminal AI Act is operational, and it’s meant to protect safety and fundamental rights. But tough enforcement could deepen concerns that the bloc is falling behind. Meanwhile, leaving AI unchecked, or relying entirely on systems developed elsewhere, could create serious security vulnerabilities.

“We already open the newspapers and see news about tens of thousands of jobs lost every day,” said one national official, granted anonymity to speak freely. “We can’t be left behind on this too.”

The other big risk to Europe: If it tries to regulate American AI companies, U.S. President Donald Trump will hit back. Trump has dismissed Amodei’s call for an AI slowdown as a “hoax,” arguing that regulation would undermine the U.S. in the race with China. He signaled that Washington is unlikely to adopt broad AI regulation.

“Von der Leyen is too scared of Trump,” Brando Benifei, the European Parliament’s lead lawmaker on AI, said, accusing the EU of hesitating to enforce its rules. Instead of listening to Trump, she should “listen to Carney, who will be with us … We should not chicken out because Trump has demonstrated that when it goes wrong, you need to be tough.”

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The State of the Union speech is delivered in Strasbourg, but how long has the European Parliament been meeting in the French city? Well, since before there was a Parliament as we know it. The Common Assembly of the European Coal and Steel Community (the precursor to the Parliament) held its first meeting in Strasbourg in 1952. The European Parliamentary Assembly (the Parliament’s original name) picked its first president, Robert Schuman, in Strasbourg in 1958, and EU member states formally designated Strasbourg as the official seat of the Parliament in 1992.

STATE OF DISUNION

THE COALITION BREAKS DOWN (AGAIN): The EPP and the Socialists and Democrats have flunked the first major post-summer test of whether they can repair their relationship, failing to agree on a text setting out the Parliament’s position on the Ceuta migrant crisis, Max Griera writes.

Yesterday’s negotiations ended without a deal despite a flurry of proposals going back and forth before the 7 p.m. deadline. That’s a blow to those pushing to revive the alliance after months of quarreling over the EPP voting with the far right and the S&D’s increasingly confrontational stance. And it casts a shadow over von der Leyen’s State of the Union, with her key parliamentary supporters clashing once again.

The main disagreement was over the EPP wanting to blame Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s government and its migration policies for the problems in Ceuta, which was a red line for the S&D, according to officials familiar with the talks.

Next step: Despite the lack of a deal, the S&D expects the EPP “not to side with the extreme right when it comes to amendments and to uphold the responsibility that comes with being a pro-European political group,” said Andrea Maceiras, a spokesperson for President Iratxe García. Right-wing and far-right groups could support the EPP’s version of the text with some amendments.

A choice to make: Assita Kanko, the lead lawmaker on the file for the right-wing European Conservatives and Reformists, called on the EPP to have “courage” and embrace the right-wing alliance. The “EPP is trying to sleep in several beds, but you cannot be with S&D and also be with us at the same time,” she said.

SRB BLAST TRAGEDY: The EU’s staff union has written to its office management arm demanding answers after a gas explosion at the Single Resolution Board’s offices on Treurenberg in central Brussels killed a firefighter and injured two Sibelga employees and another emergency worker, according to a letter seen by Playbook’s Gabriel Gavin.

“This serious accident, which resulted in the death of a rescue worker, took place in a European Union building,” the request reads. The SRB is the EU agency responsible for managing failing banks in the eurozone’s banking union. “Who commissioned the works? Was a risk assessment carried out before work began, including, in particular, plans of the gas pipework?” Union representatives are now demanding a report along with “measures to prevent a recurrence of the accident.”

The Commission said it stands ready to provide further assistance if needed, and expressed condolences and sympathies.
