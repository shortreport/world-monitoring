DRIVING THE DAY

PARLIAMENT VOTES ON CAR FILES: MEPs from the European People’s Party, Socialists and Democrats and Renew serving on Parliament’s leading committees working on the Automotive Package reached a series of deals on the automotive omnibus, greening corporate fleets and CO2 standards for automakers ahead of today’s committee votes. We scooped the proposals.

Dead on arrival: The centrist coalition’s draft deal rejected the European Commission’s proposal to set binding targets for member states to electrify their corporate fleets, saying instead that the goals should be “aspirational.” Member states are also opposed to mandatory targets. More here from your anchor.

Commission on the back foot: Bertrand L’Huillier, the head of cabinet for Industry Commissioner Stéphane Séjourné, acknowledged the binding targets are unlikely to “survive political realities in the two other institutions.” But even without mandatory targets, the regulation will still shift corporate fleets “from fossil-based vehicles,” he said at a Tuesday Bruegel event.

Omnibus: The co-rapporteurs also proposed requiring retractable door handles — popularized by Tesla — to have a mechanical override.

Emission targets: The coalition proposed reforming the 2035 de facto combustion engine ban by lowering the emissions target from the current 100% reduction from 2021 levels to 87.5%.

Utility factor: The most-watched aspect of the omnibus is the utility factor, which estimates the share of driving done on electric power and is used to calculate a plug-in hybrid’s official emissions. Parliament’s draft agreement would freeze the utility factor only for plug-in hybrids with an electric range of 120 kilometers or more.

Lobbying bonanza: Parliament won’t settle its final position until at least December, depending on when the Industrial Accelerator Act positions are finalized (more on that here). That gives lobbyists another month to influence the final position — and they’re not wasting any time.

Green warnings: William Todts, executive director of green NGO Transport & Environment, warned that lowering emissions targets now would undermine European innovation and competitiveness and help Chinese competitors. “If you’re lowering the bar, you’re going to get less investment and less modernization,” he said at the Bruegel event.

Potential backstop: The Industrial Accelerator Act — which would set local content requirements for vehicles purchased through public procurement or given subsidies — would help mitigate the impact of weakening CO2 standards. “This would lead to a situation where we have more production in Europe,” said Peter Mock, managing director of the International Council on Clean Transportation, adding that incentivizing local production “will help competitiveness and ultimately will help with jobs.”

EU TRANSPORT POLICY

BRUSSELS SETS ITS 2027 TRANSPORT AGENDA: The Commission’s new work program puts simplification at the center of next year’s agenda, with a legislative transport simplification package due in the first quarter of 2027 and a review of ReFuelEU Aviation — the EU rules meant to boost sustainable aviation fuels — planned for the second quarter, according to a Commission draft document obtained by POLITICO’s Jacopo Barigazzi.

More here from us on the full agenda.

Trucks next: Brussels also plans a legislative review of CO2 standards for heavy-duty vehicles in the third quarter, reopening the rules that set emissions-reduction targets for new trucks and buses.

Simplify, simplify: The main work-program document, obtained by POLITICO’s Kathryn Carlson, says the Commission wants to look for regulatory simplification opportunities in transport, alongside housing and digital policy.

Maritime security: The document also says the EU will work to restore stability in the Middle East and Red Sea and promote freedom of navigation and maritime security through the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab — two of the world’s most important shipping chokepoints.

Clearing out old rules: Brussels also plans to repeal some outdated transport legislation, including a 1990 road-freight crisis regulation and an old air-traffic rule linked to functional airspace blocks the Commission says is now obsolete under the latest Single European Sky framework.

AVIATION

LUFTHANSA WARNS OF HIGHER AIRFARES: Lufthansa’s latest quarterly results and comments from its CEO underlined that higher jet fuel prices will be passed on to passengers as airlines’ hedging coverage gradually decreases next year. Carsten Spohr said Tuesday that airfares could rise by double-digit percentages. Tommaso has more.

It’s already happening: The Lufthansa CEO said airlines are already passing on higher fuel costs to passengers on lucrative long-haul routes. “In the North Atlantic region, we managed to pass on jet fuel price increases almost completely. And the same applies to our Asian routes,” Spohr told reporters during Lufthansa’s quarterly earnings press conference.

How bad is it? After jet fuel prices fell slightly in June and July, “now in October, we’ve almost reached again the levels of April and May,” Spohr said. However, he also stressed that the airline group can still count on hedging contracts covering more than 75% of its fuel consumption through the first half of 2027, with that share falling to 60% in the second half of the year.

Other trends: Although Lufthansa’s fuel costs are projected to rise by €2 billion over 2026 compared with last year, the carrier reported “the highest revenue quarter of our corporate history,” the CEO added. That was partly thanks to businesses other than passenger transport, including air cargo operations. “Among other factors, the continuing boom in the construction of data centers for artificial intelligence in the third quarter clearly boosted the business of our cargo segment,” he added.

BOMB THREATS TARGET EUROPEAN FLIGHTS: Seven passenger aircraft were searched on Monday and Tuesday by authorities in Spain, Poland, Italy and Bulgaria following bomb threats. No explosives were found, but the bomb threats also led to the temporary closure of Warsaw Chopin Airport.

How it began: On Monday, a Wizz Air flight from London to Sofia made an emergency landing in Budapest after being escorted by Hungarian military jets following a bomb threat received during the flight.

On Tuesday, three Ryanair aircraft — one bound for Murcia, Spain, and the other two for Rome — were searched following bomb threats. Two other flights bound for Warsaw Chopin Airport — one operated by SAS and the other by LOT — were also targeted, prompting the diversion of the LOT flight to Warsaw Modlin Airport. A Wizz Air flight from Bergamo, Italy to Sofia was also targeted by a similar threat, the Bulgarian government said.

Reactions: The Commission said: “Any threats against civilians are unacceptable. Ensuring the safety and security of citizens remains a priority. We are closely monitoring the situation and are in contact with the affected Member States. European Aviation Safety Agency is also involved.” Wizz Air confirmed Tuesday’s Bergamo flight incidents and said it is cooperating fully with the authorities.

MARITIME

SHIP TO (INDIAN) SHORE: Lawmakers from centrist and left-wing groups are resisting the Commission’s decision to add two Indian beaching yards to the EU’s list of approved facilities under the Ship Recycling Regulation. The debate takes place today, with a motion for resolution due for a vote later in the afternoon, after lawmakers deal with speeding-up environmental assessments.

Shiver me timbers: “This decision by the Commission is a death sentence for European ship recyclers. We would never accept ships to be dismantled on tidal mudflats and beaches in Europe, so why should we organize unfair competition ourselves by allowing Indian competitors to do exactly that?” Greens MEP Sara Matthieu said. She is leading the motion, arguing that the beaching yards do not meet the bloc’s environmental standards, something the shipping industry has pushed back against here.

TOURISM

EU TOURISM STRATEGY LANDS: The Commission will unveil its first EU Sustainable Tourism Strategy today, with Brussels trying to square Europe’s position as the world’s top tourism destination with growing pressure from overcrowding and weak transport links.

Parliament wants more: Maltese S&D MEP Daniel Attard, who led Parliament’s tourism report, told Martina the strategy should deliver “better connectivity, fair rules on short-term rentals and investment that strengthens competitiveness while protecting our natural and cultural heritage.” He also called for “concrete measures, clear funding and measurable targets.”

PIT STOPS

German auto supplier Schaeffler announced it is cutting 3,460 jobs across Europe and closing four German factories.

From us: Spain’s Transport Minister Oscar Puente said on Tuesday he would resign after two deadly train crashes in less than a year.
