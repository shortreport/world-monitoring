DRIVING THE DAY

BUY EUROPE PUSH HITS TRANSPORT: The Commission’s public procurement overhaul lands today with a strong transport-industrial policy angle, giving public buyers more room to favor European suppliers and pushing quality, resilience and security ahead of a lowest-price-only model.

Going European: The proposal, a draft version of which has been obtained by POLITICO, would let contracting authorities favor EU and treaty-covered suppliers through participation restrictions, minimum European-content requirements or evaluation bonuses for bids with a stronger EU component. Brussels could also make those preferences mandatory in sectors where it believes resilience, security or reciprocity concerns justify tougher action against non-EU suppliers.

Rail in the frame: Rail and infrastructure groups have specifically been pushing the Commission to stop treating public procurement as a simple race to the lowest price and instead use it as an industrial policy tool. Rail manufacturers’ association UNIFE, together with other construction and infrastructure lobbies, has been calling for European preference in strategic sectors including rail, construction and dredging for years, warning about competition from heavily subsidized third-country players like China.

Today’s timeline: Commissioners meet from 9 a.m., with a packed agenda including the PPA, the European Innovation Act (more on that below), the Affordable Housing Package and the Outermost Regions package. The responsible commissioners will then brief journalists.

What’s at stake: Public procurement represents up to 15 percent of the bloc’s gross domestic product — roughly €2.5 trillion a year. However, not all tenders run in the EU have to follow its rules, as these kick in when the estimated value of a contract reaches certain financial thresholds that vary by sector. Today’s proposal will set out a new framework for these tenders.

Going for a regulation: The Commission chose a regulation instead of a directive — despite the objections of a majority of EU countries. This would reduce the scope for divergent national transposition of EU procurement rules. It’s an attempt to make implementation in national law simpler and avoid “gold-plating” by member countries.

Going for quality: The draft proposal shows that the Commission wants public authorities to give quality criteria a minimum weighting of 30 percent, rising to 50 percent for labor-intensive contracts, unless they can justify relying on price alone as quality would be sufficiently guaranteed through technical specifications. Environmental, social, innovation, security and resilience considerations would all be counted as quality — although the draft does not define them.

Going digital: The Commission also wants to make it easier for companies to participate in tenders across the EU. Instead of suggesting one platform, the EU executive wants each national authority to have its own platform — or use the Commission’s — to publish their calls for tenders. Credentials obtained on one platform should allow companies to access the others.

What’s next: Parliament and EU countries now have to negotiate the proposal, setting up a fight over how far “European preference” should go.

MARITIME

NEW CAPTAIN WANTED AT EMSA: Brussels is hunting for a new executive director for the European Maritime Safety Agency — meaning the winner will run the EU body dealing with shipping safety, pollution and an ever-growing pile of maritime rules. Applications close Sept. 29 at noon.

Who’s leaving: The job has been held since 2019 by Croatian maritime lawyer and former transport ministry state secretary Maja Markovčić Kostelac, who has overseen EMSA as its remit expanded well beyond classic ship safety into pollution response, digital monitoring and greener shipping.

AVIATION

AVIATION WORKING GROUP FOCUSED ON ETS: EU countries meet today to discuss how the Commission’s Emission Trading System proposal will impact the aviation industry. The working party meeting is one of six ETS-focused discussions this month. So far, member states are divided, with a skeptical alliance led by Poland and Italy versus a coalition of ETS-backers fronted by Sweden and the Netherlands.

Reminder: The meeting comes after a leaked Commission presentation revealed the executive is preparing to pull more aviation emissions into the EU carbon market. The ETS would keep the current geographical scope until the end of 2028 but add flights between the EEA and Gibraltar. Four EU diplomats tell our colleague Ban Makuch that talks have progressed “positively” but it’s still “too early to tell” how a vote on a comprise will look.

What industry wants: Trade association IATA rejects the Commission’s proposed geographical expansion, arguing it would impose European costs on legs of international flights that are outside EU jurisdiction. Such a scheme could see airlines paying twice for the same emissions.

All about SAF: Aviation lobby A4E, meanwhile, is concerned that the new ETS will lead to more costs for carriers while doing little to generate more sustainable aviation fuel. “ETS reform should go further by guaranteeing that every cent passengers pay in their tickets for ETS is completely invested back into cleaner aviation,” Ourania Georgoutsaku, A4E’s managing director, told POLITICO in a statement. “ETS should be a tool to accelerate SAF offtakes and bring down SAF costs if Europe wants to cut emissions and keep flying affordable for the everyday European.”

Carbon freebie: European airlines received €10.9 billion in free EU ETS allowances between 2013 and 2025, according to a new Opportunity Green report, which argues the giveaways boosted profits without driving decarbonization.

Big six: Lufthansa, Ryanair, easyJet, IAG, Air France-KLM and Wizz Air received €6 billion while collectively making €21 billion in profits, the researchers estimate; they spent only around €1 billion on what the report counts as genuinely sustainable investment.

Next up: The Irish Council presidency said it’s aiming to strike a deal by Dec. 11 on ETS. Parliament, on the other hand, is likely to have a tougher time reaching a position.

CARS

PARLIAMENT SIDES WITH AUTO SUPPLIERS: The Industrial Accelerator Act draft report from the three MEPs leading the file is set to land today. A readout seen by Jordyn reveals they heard the pleas from auto supplier lobby CLEPA and argue that EU-made components must make up 75 percent of cars, instead of the Commission’s proposed 70 percent.

Some help: The auto sector is in crisis — shown by Volkswagen laying off 100,000 workers and shuttering plants. But suppliers are also bleeding jobs and Made in Europe is a way of ensuring the companies remain viable in Europe. During a public hearing earlier this month, CLEPA Secretary-General Benjamin Krieger urged lawmakers to maintain high local content requirements.

Doubling down: Despite strong protests from the U.K. car lobby, Parliament’s rapporteurs agreed with the Commission’s proposal that only cars produced in the 27 EU member states should qualify for public support schemes, according to the readout.

FDI criteria: The MEPs also suggest tightening the criteria to vet foreign direct investment, the readout states. The report suggests making the six criteria proposed by the Commission cumulative and mandatory, rather than allowing investors to pick and choose.

Record speed: Socialist Pierre Jouvet, Renew’s Christophe Grudler and Anna Cavazzini of the Greens all issued press releases on Monday trumpeting success. The three MEPs managed to produce the draft in record time, given it’s the first time that three rapporteurs were tasked to work on a new proposal.

Pragmatic approach: “We took a pragmatic approach,” Grudler told our colleague Francesca Micheletti. “This isn’t a topic for political division, it’s an urgent topic for European industry.” Grudler explained that all three rapporteurs got to comment on the entirety of the file to deliver a single report.

Next steps: The report will be presented to the other committees on Sept. 28, with a deadline for amendments expiring two days later. The rapporteurs will then work to incorporate amendments into a draft aimed for a plenary vote on Dec. 1.

Coming up in Council: Diplomats will be poring over the text at the working party on competitiveness and growth today and Thursday, ahead of a ministerial-level discussion on the file scheduled for Sept. 24.
