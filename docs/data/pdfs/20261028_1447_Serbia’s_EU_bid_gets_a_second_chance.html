DRIVING THE DAY

EU PONDERS FUTURE OF SERBIA’S ACCESSION: Serbia’s political shake-up is stirring hopes in Brussels that a new, pro-European government in Belgrade could revive the country’s stalled EU bid, according to four European Commission officials and three diplomats who spoke to Playbook.

Times a-changing? After the student-led opposition’s election win ended Aleksandar Vučić’s 12-year grip on power, there’s a sense of optimism among those working on enlargement at the Commission, one official said, with another calling the vote a potential “game changer.” They were granted anonymity to speak frankly about the internal mood.

Serbia’s EU bid has been stuck for years. Since 2021, the Commission has considered Belgrade technically ready to open its next cluster (EU jargon for a group of negotiating chapters in accession talks), which covers competitiveness and growth. But a group of countries including the Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden and Croatia have resisted, citing concerns over democratic backsliding and media freedom.

If Belgrade gets a pro-EU government, it could move the needle in national capitals and bring Serbia closer to becoming a member of the bloc, two of the Commission officials said.

But that’s a big if, one diplomat from a country that has opposed Belgrade’s bid cautioned. “Any positive change on all of those issues would be more than welcome of course, but for now all that remains to be seen,” the diplomat said.

Positive signs: The victorious party’s election manifesto contains elements that are promising for accession and has been welcomed at the Berlaymont. But the Student List has yet to name who would lead its new administration and any progress on joining the bloc would hinge on the enactment of reforms.

Residual resentment: Brussels may also have to contend with a perception by the incoming government that the EU had previously been too willing to use Vučić as its go-to interlocutor, Una Hajdari reports.

Opening the door: Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Council President António Costa congratulated the Student List after the election, pledging “to work together to advance Serbia’s future in the European Union.” Others went further. “The Serbian people have given a strong mandate for change,” said Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos, while centrist MEP Helmut Brandstätter called for EU accession talks to “resume as soon as possible.”

EU ambassadors are set to meet today to discuss the Commission’s proposal for democratic safeguards in future accession treaties. Montenegro, whose treaty is already being drafted, is set to be the focus. But last week’s election could mean Serbia is discussed too, two diplomats said.

The next litmus test will be the Commission’s enlargement report, due next week. It covers the past 12 months of progress in the EU’s candidate countries and should provide early insight into the benchmarks Brussels expects the new government to hit to move its bid forward.

REPORTERS WITHOUT DOCS

PRESS VS. COMMISSION: Long-awaited plans to revamp the Commission could make it harder for journalists to gain access to documents and hold the EU executive to account. That’s according to a letter sent today to Ursula von der Leyen by a group of 16 associations representing journalists and news outlets, seen by Mari Eccles.

Canteen scoops only: Recommendations for reforming the Commission mention releasing info to the press “where appropriate.” That, according to Tom Gibson from the Committee to Protect Journalists, could leave “a lot of leeway for the Commission to classify everything except the lunch menu.”

A senior EU official told Playbook that under any reorganization there should be a “shift from documents to focus on information,” and that timely decisions should be made about “information that should be released, with some exceptions for personal data or security.”

Delayed and redacted: The group, which includes the International Federation of Journalists, Follow the Money and the European Federation of Journalists, says that under existing rules reporters are already unable to do their job properly. It’s a system in which there are “huge delays,” access is denied on “questionable grounds” and documents that are shared contain so many redactions they’re basically unintelligible, the group says. “Stories which should emerge do not.”

What kind of stories? The reporters flagged difficulties in finding out who got post-Covid-19 recovery funds, why the Commission withdrew controversial legislation on patents and the kind of communication the executive had with TikTok about the Romanian election in 2024.

#Journorequest: The groups want to see, among other things, the Commission publish figures on waiting times for responses, implement EU court rulings on transparency and improve internal procedures on filing and archiving documents (to avoid another Pfizergate-style hunt for texts).

“We have not received the letter,” Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier told Mari, but added that the EU executive, which received around 7000 requests every year, takes its transparency responsibility “extremely seriously.” The Commission updates its internal guidelines when required by the court, he said.

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

The EU has come out with its first climate-proofing law … but what does that mean? The proposed Climate Resilience Law would require EU members to take into account a warmer world when they draw up policies and budgets. The Commission wants national governments to prepare for temperatures that are 2C higher by 2050 and 3C higher by the end of the century. The law also obliges countries to carry out national climate-risk assessments every five years, starting in June 2029.

PARLIAMENT ASSESSES HUNGARY’S REFORMS

D-DAY FOR MAGYAR: A group of MEPs, led by Parliament Vice President Sophie Wilmès, will today deliver its preliminary assessment on whether to lift the Article 7 procedure that was imposed on Hungary in 2018. That assessment will come after a meeting with Transport and Investment Minister Dávid Vitézy, Max Griera writes.

Budapest wants to see the end of the Article 7 procedure, a mechanism that’s deployed when a country is considered at risk of breaching the bloc’s core values (sometimes called the EU’s “nuclear option”). But EU members are waiting for the Parliament to give its opinion.

The European People’s Party is pushing to have the Article 7 lifted soon (Prime Minister Péter Magyar’s Tisza party is an EPP member), with a vote scheduled for the December plenary, following a debate in November. “The Tisza government is working to rebuild democracy … they have achieved impressive results in a short time,” said EPP Vice-Chair Tomas Tobé, who will lead a 15-strong EPP mission to Budapest next week.

But the Socialist, Renew and Green groups are more cautious and may want time to see if reforms are implemented. “It’s quite symbolic when and how we call for the ending of it, so we need to be careful,” said Slovak centrist MEP Veronika Cifrová Ostrihoňová, a member of Parliament’s rule-of-law monitoring group. “The delegation’s findings … will be crucial in deciding whether to support the December deadline.”

Magyar speaks: Parliament and Magyar have been in contact about an appearance in Strasbourg for months, according to two officials, but no date has been set.

POLISH SURPRISE GOODBYE: The Brussels bubble is today coming to terms with the departure of Polish Perm Rep Agnieszka Bartol, who’s off to Warsaw to take on the role of minister of funds and regional policy. Ambassadors are lamenting the loss of one of their “funniest colleagues.”

Exit stage left … Bartol’s departure appears to open up the race for the European Council’s powerful secretary-general job, with incumbent Thérèse Blanchet expected to leave before late 2027. Bartol was a potential contender, alongside France’s EU Ambassador Philippe Léglise-Costa, who is also expected to leave his current post after May 2027, following the French elections.

… or not? As Poland heads into an election in October 2027, Bartol could yet make a Brussels comeback via the Council job, one diplomat suggested. Never say never in the EU bubble.
