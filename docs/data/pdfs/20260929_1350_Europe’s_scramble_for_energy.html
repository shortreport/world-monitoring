DRIVING THE DAY

EUROPE’S WINTER ENERGY SCRAMBLE: EU energy czar Dan Jørgensen is leading urgent talks in Ireland today on how the bloc can secure its energy supply and ward off a winter crisis, three diplomats and officials told Playbook.

The EU is pushing to diversify energy sources as Donald Trump threatens to cut off diesel exports to Europe, wars in Ukraine and the Middle East drive up prices and Russian hybrid attacks unsettle suppliers. Ministers from the U.K., Ukraine and Norway are among those attending talks on possible energy partnerships, Ben Munster reports from Dublin.

The bloc’s battle with high energy prices has been raging for months, but the alarm level ratcheted up over the weekend as Jørgensen sent a letter to EU capitals urging them to consider energy savings similar to measures put in place after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

EU gas stores are running low, while NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte has urged alliance members to increase oil-refining capacity amid escalating Russian hybrid threats.

Widening the net: The bloc is also seeking closer energy ties with neighboring non-EU countries that could help offset a U.S. diesel cutoff. That includes members of the Energy Community, a group of countries aspiring to join the EU and working to integrate their energy markets with the bloc — if falteringly.

Help from friends: “The solution for European energy security does not begin and end at the EU borders, it includes both neighbors and candidates like Moldova — the highest-performing Contracting Party of the Energy Community,” Moldovan Energy Minister Dorin Junghietu told Ben.

Trump doubles down: The EU’s Oil Coordination Group also meets today to assess what a U.S. ban on diesel exports could mean for Europe. Reminder: After the White House denied reports it was preparing a 90-day ban, Trump said Sunday he was still considering one “very seriously.” Oil companies and industry groups are opposed, warning it could disrupt supplies and push prices higher.

Politics at the pump: Brussels sees a potential Trump move to ban diesel exports as driven by domestic politics ahead of November’s midterms, a Commission official said.

The bottom line: Brussels is scrambling to stop this winter turning into another energy nightmare. Trump’s diesel threat, low gas stocks, wars and Russian hybrid attacks are stacking the odds against it.

LIGHTS OUT FOR EPSO?

THE EU WEIGHS AXING EPSO: The European Commission is considering big changes to its civil service exam board amid a budget squeeze and criticism that the body is no longer fit for purpose — including possibly scrapping it, two EU officials told Playbook.

EPSO, created in 2002, is the gateway for EU civil servants, designed to pick the best and brightest from thousands of applicants. But it’s under growing scrutiny amid high-profile failures and the fact that the EU has fewer jobs to offer due to pressure on its long-term budget.

One scenario is simply to “get rid of EPSO,” said an EU official who’s involved in the thinking about reforms, granted anonymity to speak about the plans. The last open hiring round — which drew a record 174,922 applicants — proved “completely unmanageable,” the official said, adding that “the cost of administering this sort of mass exam is out of proportion to what we get out of it.”

The current model is “reaching its limits,” a second EU official said, lamenting that hiring currently takes between two and three years. “You will see quite far-reaching ideas on how the system could be changed.” A spokesperson for the European Commission did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The plans follow a string of high-profile failures, including one last year that forced nearly 10,000 aspiring EU civil servants to retake an exam after a technical blunder voided their results.

In EPSO’s defense: Cristiano Sebastiani, who represents the Renouveau et Démocratie EU staff union, said the exam board isn’t delivering on its aim to boost hiring from underrepresented geographies and among younger applicants — but it should evolve, rather than be abandoned. “There is a role where it provides expertise to different services for their recruitment. But abolishing it entirely would be the wrong message to EU citizens.”

20-SECOND PLAYBOOK PRIMER

EU countries last week reached a deal on using €6.6 billion in EU cash to aid Ukraine. That money will come from the European Peace Facility — but what is that? It’s a fund that’s not part of the EU budget and allows member countries to be partly reimbursed for military equipment they provide to Ukraine. The fund’s initial focus was to provide funding for military operations in mainly African countries, but that was before Russia invaded Ukraine.

RIGHT TO STAY

SHOULD I STAY OR SHOULD I GO? The European Commission’s plan to help people stay in rural areas is taking shape — yet it’s unlikely to bring fresh funding where it is wanted as governments fight over regional spending in the EU’s next long-term budget, Zoya Sheftalovich reports.

The budget must give regions the capacity to build their own futures, said Kata Tüttő, president of the Committee of the Regions. “We cannot talk about people’s freedom to stay while designing an EU budget that risks concentrating both decisions and investment in fewer hands and fewer places.”

The plan: Expected in early 2027, the “Right to Stay” strategy will target gaps in jobs, housing and essential services that push people out of rural areas — but it’s not expected to come with its own dedicated cash pot. Commission Executive Vice President Raffaele Fitto will host residents and regional representatives at the Berlaymont today to discuss obstacles to local prosperity.

Meanwhile … EU governments are haggling over the bloc’s next budget, with Germany leading a faction pushing to cut hundreds of billions from the total amount while southern and eastern countries are trying to protect EU support for farmers and poorer regions.
