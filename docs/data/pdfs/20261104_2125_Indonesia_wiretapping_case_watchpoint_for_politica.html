Indonesia
By Olga Letticia
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:

Wiretapping case could further erode Prabowo-Jokowi relations and undermine political stability. Former National Police chief and presidential chief of staff Listyo Sigit Prabowo has been arrested along with two senior officers over alleged illegal wiretapping of President Prabowo Subianto and other prominent figures. Investigators say the interceptions took place in 2025–2026 without a law enforcement basis and that records were deleted in October 2026. Authorities have not established a motive or found evidence of treason.

  • Listyo’s ties to former president Joko Widodo (Jokowi) add a political dimension to the case. Jokowi appointed him police chief in 2021, and Listyo remained in the role until Prabowo named him presidential chief of staff in October. He is also seen as one of Jokowi’s most influential allies. The investigation could therefore fuel speculation about whether the case reflects a breakdown in relations between Prabowo and Jokowi, although there is no evidence so far that Jokowi was involved.
  • The arrest comes amid signs of strain between the two leaders, including Prabowo’s sidelining of some Jokowi-linked figures. It also follows renewed scrutiny over the educational credentials of Vice President Gibran Rakabumbing Raka (Jokowi’s son) after a recent Constitutional Court ruling, which has prompted speculation about impeachment. Gibran is among the contenders who could run against Prabowo in the 2029 presidential election.
  • Watchpoints for broader political stability include reports of an increased military presence in Jakarta, whether authorities establish/allege a motive or implicate additional figures linked to Jokowi, and if parliament begins to pursue Gibran’s removal.

Student protests will likely persist as frustration with the government rises. On 28 October, during Youth Pledge Day, hundreds of students and civil-society groups protested in Jakarta and other major cities under the banner of #IndonesiaSadar (#WakeUpIndonesia). Their demands centered on the government’s handling of land-clearing fires and the resulting haze, including officials’ refusal to declare the fires a national disaster, alongside broader criticism of policies seen as failing public needs. Protesters say the government has made little meaningful progress and have vowed to sustain demonstrations and build broader public support.

  • The risk of further demonstrations will remain elevated if economic pressures persist or the government is seen as unresponsive. The movement could gain momentum if there are any triggering events—such as a controversial policy decision or more interventionist police and military involvement—that could intensify public frustration.
  • For now, protests are more likely to remain episodic and pose little threat to Prabowo's position. However, they point to growing discontent with the government’s responsiveness and could increase pressure for Prabowo’s administration to deliver more populist policies.
  • The government’s responses to the forest fires and other demands will be key watchpoints, as its limited action could further erode public confidence. Protests shifting toward broader opposition to Prabowo’s spending priorities and governance could raise political pressure to adjust flagship programs, while sustained demonstrations could disrupt activity in major cities and increase scrutiny of companies linked to the fires.

Singapore–Indonesia ties will likely remain resilient despite energy tensions. Jakarta has reiterated that domestic gas needs will take precedence over exports, following a reduction in pipeline gas supplies to Singapore by 31 million standard cubic feet per day from 24 October. Energy Minister Bahlil Lahadalia said recently that Indonesia would export only surplus gas after domestic demand is met, and that the government is reviewing future export policy, including possible adjustments to LNG shipments and electricity supply. However, the gas reduction is limited, and both governments continue to pursue cooperation, suggesting the dispute remains manageable for now.

  • Singapore has said the reduction represents less than 2% of its daily gas demand and that contingency supplies are available. The government has welcomed Indonesia’s assurance that suppliers will honor contracts, while the affected importers are assessing their contractual rights.
  • The move reflects Jakarta’s growing emphasis on domestic energy security and industrial demand. Although the current cut is limited, further changes to pipeline or LNG exports could hamper negotiations over energy supply and cross-border projects.
  • On 27 October, the two countries’ foreign ministers also reaffirmed cooperation to establish a joint task force on transboundary haze, including information-sharing and evidence-gathering on companies linked to fires. Key watchpoints are whether Indonesia adjusts LNG export policy, how the planned electricity trade proceeds, and whether future contracts provide stronger protections against domestic supply interventions.
Malaysia
By Fadli Yusoff
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:

General election alliances will remain uncertain despite current cooperation. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim’s Pakatan Harapan (PH)-led government signed a parliamentary confidence and supply agreement (CSA) with smaller parties Bersatu and Muda, alongside independent members of parliament (MPs), on 20 October. Meanwhile, PH’s federal coalition partner Barisan Nasional (BN) signed a memorandum of understanding with the opposition Perikatan Nasional (PN) coalition on 28 October to become “strategic partners” for the 14 November Melaka state election.

  • The CSA brings seven additional MPs in the 222-seat parliament into Anwar’s support base—a modest but important boost amid growing public disagreements with BN. In return, these MPs will receive constituency funding for local infrastructure and welfare projects, as well as promises of governance reforms to be implemented by the government. Led by former prime minister Muhyiddin Yassin, Bersatu draws support from the ethnic Malay Muslim majority, a constituency Anwar wants to strengthen his standing with ahead of general elections.
  • The CSA increases the likelihood of an electoral alliance between PH and Bersatu, but this remains far from certain. The two sides will still compete in three seats in the Melaka state polls. More broadly, Bersatu harbors ambitions to form a separate coalition, and PH likely prefers to enter a coalition agreement with BN, which has a larger support base compared to Bersatu.
  • Meanwhile, BN’s partnership with PN builds on the coalitions’ cooperation in recent state elections. The two coalitions have agreed to avoid contesting the same seats in Melaka and to produce a joint manifesto. This will reduce vote splits among the ethnic Malay Muslim constituency, reinforcing Eurasia Group’s expectation that BN will retain control of the state. However, the delay in reaching an agreement points to difficulties in allocating seats—negotiations will grow considerably more complex and represent a key watchpoint ahead of general elections expected in late 2027.
Vietnam
By Peter Mumford
Short-term trajectory:
Long-term trajectory:

Sharp fuel tax cuts aim to combat inflation without hurting double-digit growth drive. Consumer prices rose 5.1% in October year-on-year, above the government’s full-year target of 4.5%. Continued high global oil prices have put significant pressure on Vietnam, which relies heavily on fuel imports.

  • The special consumption taxes on gasoline (previously 10%), E5 biofuel (8%), and E10 biofuel (7%) have all been cut to zero; the measures took effect on 1 November and will run until 31 March.
  • These are the latest steps by the authorities to tame inflation. The government previously extended fuel import duty cuts to the end of 2026—and will likely maintain these longer if global oil prices remain elevated—and reduced administered electricity prices by 2% with effect from 1 November. Authorities have also rolled out support for households and small businesses.
  • Hanoi hopes these steps will avoid the need to further tighten monetary policy. Doing so would undermine General Secretary To Lam’s fervent push for double-digit GDP growth, which remains the higher political priority, at least for now. The government also believes inflation is mostly supply-side driven—with energy and transportation among the fastest rising components of the inflation basket—and is focusing on targeted steps to reduce costs for businesses and households rather than broad-based cooling measures. Vietnam’s strong economic growth—just shy of 10% in the third quarter year-on-year—has boosted revenue and created the fiscal space to cut taxes and expand support measures.
New Zealand
By Fadli Yusoff

Ruling coalition will face a tougher path to election victory. Two recent polls ahead of the 7 November general election suggest that Prime Minister Christopher Luxon’s National Party has dropped below 30% support among potential voters, down 1-3 percentage points from previous levels.

  • Nevertheless, combined support for National and its governing partners, ACT and New Zealand First, remains narrowly ahead of the left-leaning bloc comprising Labour, the Greens, and Te Pati Maori in most polls. The centrist Opportunity Party has also edged up from 5% to around 7%, strengthening its potential kingmaker role. Luxon could be forced to reverse his earlier position on excluding Opportunity from his coalition post-election in order to retain office.
  • Chris Hipkins’s opposition Labour Party is closing the gap with National or edging ahead in the recent polls. Surveys also indicate that inflation remains voters’ leading concern—likely amplified further by climbing fuel prices in recent weeks—and Labour leads National on perceived competence in addressing cost-of-living pressures.
  • An uneven economic recovery compounds National’s vulnerability. Strong growth in tourism and agricultural exports has boosted its appeal, but urban households continue to face weak job prospects and a housing downturn. This could erode the party’s previous gains among urban voters, though Luxon’s promise of no new taxes will appeal to much of the electorate.
Charting ASEAN

The IMF forecasts Vietnam will reach $5,259 in per capita GDP this year, overtaking Indonesia’s $5,153. The milestone reflects Vietnam’s rapid economic expansion and strong investment inflows, underpinned by political stability and its growing role in global technology supply chains, particularly semiconductors and electronics. These strengths reinforce Vietnam’s appeal as a regional manufacturing hub as Hanoi targets 10% GDP growth this year. Indonesia is targeting a more modest 6% growth rate while contending with a weakening currency and concerns over fiscal policy that have weighed on investor confidence.
