[Heads Up] The US-Iran 60-day deadline expired with no deal, and Trump is threatening to bomb Oman over Hormuz.

The negotiating window closed August 17 with no final agreement, and Trump is demanding Iran 'raise the white flag' as his team preps a new pressure campaign. He has separately warned Oman he will bomb 'thoroughly' if it interferes with US control of the Strait of Hormuz. POLITICO reporting flags that Trump's public claims about the strait don't match actual tanker traffic data, suggesting the rhetoric is running ahead of the reality on the water. Eurasia Group's base case is that the standoff drags on rather than erupts immediately, but the risk of a military incident in Hormuz over the next several weeks is now the top tail risk on the board — a spike above $100/barrel oil is the immediate market signal to watch.

[Changed] Trump ordered a major cutback in US-Korea joint military exercises, citing his relationship with Kim Jong Un.

The instruction to Defense Secretary Hegseth marks a real shift from deterrence toward direct engagement with Pyongyang. Seoul is visibly uneasy, reading this as a weakening of the US defense commitment at a moment when it can least afford ambiguity. This lands alongside a separate US crackdown on Chinese tech (DJI and others), so Washington is simultaneously softening on North Korea while hardening on China — a split-screen that allies will need to parse. Watch for South Korean officials to push back publicly this week.

[Changing Now] Israel's Lebanon operations and the Hormuz standoff are compounding in real time, raising the odds of a wider Middle East flare-up before September.

Eurasia Group and Politico both flag cross-regional fuel flows as the current buffer preventing shortages, but that buffer depends on no disruption in the strait. Watch tanker movements and insurance rates over the next 72 hours as the earliest signal of real (not rhetorical) escalation. If Hormuz tensions harden US China policy as a side effect, expect faster action on chip and drone export controls this week. TSMC's stock, already a target of fresh US fund buying below $440, is the cleanest market proxy for how investors are pricing Taiwan Strait risk against this backdrop.

[Medium Term] Chinese EV makers are locking in structural advantages abroad — in Europe, Africa, and Southeast Asia — faster than Western policy can respond.

Chinese EV sales in Europe jumped 65% in H1 2026 to nearly 792,000 units, ironically fueled by German and Italian clean-car subsidies; Brussels is now weighing local-content rules like France and the UK already have, but German automakers fear Beijing retaliation given their China market exposure. Meanwhile Chery is buying a Nissan plant in Africa and BYD's chairman is in the Philippines expanding Southeast Asia distribution — a coordinated global land-grab, not opportunistic expansion. China's auto exports have topped one million units for two straight months, with NEVs now over 60% of domestic sales. This is a slow-moving industrial story, but the window for the US and Europe to erect effective barriers before Chinese EV supply chains become entrenched is closing within the next 12 months.
