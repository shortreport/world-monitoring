EXECUTIVE BRIEFING
Wednesday, September 2, 2026  |  World Intelligence Monitor
[Heads Up] Iran is threatening to mine the Strait of Hormuz just as the US Treasury launches a new weekly sanctions campaign.

Treasury Secretary Bessent announced during the G20 summit that Washington will roll out sanctions on a weekly basis — a 'D-Day' style pressure campaign against Iran. In direct response, Iran's Revolutionary Guard has warned it will lay mines in the southern Hormuz Strait, the corridor through which roughly 20% of global oil supply passes. Trump has denied plans for full-scale war but says limited operations to secure the strait will continue. If Iran follows through this month, expect an immediate spike in oil and shipping insurance costs with no diplomatic off-ramp currently on the table.

[Changed] Japan's borrowing costs just hit a 30-year high, and currency intervention has stopped working.

Japan's long-term interest rate reached 3% for the first time in three decades, driven by expectations of further US rate hikes. The yen has weakened from the 150 range toward 160 to the dollar, and neither US nor Japanese intervention has slowed the slide. Rising US Treasury yields — fueled by Hormuz-driven dollar demand and Trump's tariff agenda — are compounding the pressure. This puts Japan's central bank in a bind heading into its mid-September policy meeting: raise rates further and risk domestic pain, or hold and watch import inflation worsen.

[Changing Now] A 50% US tariff on Canadian-made cars is about to hit Toyota and Honda harder than any other automaker.

Trump is pushing to eliminate Canadian auto and parts imports entirely through a 50% tariff, a move US officials are calling a 'death sentence' for Canada's auto industry. Toyota and Honda face the largest exposure of any manufacturer given their Canadian production footprint. In a defensive countermove, Honda and Nissan — after their merger talks collapsed earlier this year — have now agreed to collaborate on next-generation vehicle software instead. Watch for formal tariff implementation this month and any resulting production cuts at North American plants.

[Medium Term] Washington is quietly testing a new US-China 'strategic stability' arrangement — and Taiwan is the pressure point that could break it.

The Trump administration is recalibrating China policy around trade and diplomacy, with Beijing notably staying 'relatively inactive' during the Hormuz crisis — seen by some as a bid to position China as a stabilizing broker. At the same time, the US is publicly declaring US-Taiwan relations at an 'all-time high,' expanding chip investment, and backing Palau against Chinese economic coercion. This is a contradiction that can't hold indefinitely: analysts flag rising risk of miscalculation or a Taiwan-related flashpoint in the September-to-October window, precisely when markets appear unprepared for a shock of that scale. Any concession on Taiwan during upcoming US-China talks would be a major signal to track.
